## What would it mean to solve consciousness?

Okay, so that's the qualia of the day. We're going to get into very serious business today — actually solving the phenomenal binding problem, for real. I've been waiting so long to make this video.

Number one: what would it mean to solve the problem of consciousness to begin with? The hard problem, from David Chalmers, is this very interesting observation that it is very difficult to conceive of a way in which form and structure could give rise to qualia and conscious experience. As stated, the hard problem is actually conceptually unsolvable. There are many approaches that people have taken: either trying to solve it directly, or deconstructing it, or proposing a different framework that is solvable.

An analogy that David Pearce and Mike Johnson both like to make — and of course I repeat it often — is that talking about the hard problem of consciousness as *the* problem of consciousness is sort of like talking about "the hard problem of matter." Whereas, no, actually, there are a lot of sub-components: the problems of forces, of particles, of fields, electromagnetism, the strong force. "The hard problem of matter" is really an improper reification. You really should decompose it into a bunch of sub-problems, which eventually gives rise to metallurgy and chemistry and so many sub-areas. There's no one hard problem of matter.

### Four sub-problems of consciousness

Following that analogy, I think it makes a lot of sense to talk about the hard problem of consciousness by reframing it into sub-problems — sub-components that can actually be tackled successfully. Mike Johnson has a bigger list, the eight sub-problems of consciousness, and I recommend checking it out.[^principia-qualia] But it's very technical, and it would take us hours and hours to dig through them and provide the solutions that we have at the Qualia Research Institute for them. Which is why I'm going to be focusing on David Pearce's breakdown,[^table-stakes] which is that any scientific theory of consciousness must be able to solve and explain:

1. **Why does consciousness exist to begin with?**
2. **What are the qualia values and varieties, and their interrelationships?** Once again, the logo of the Qualia Research Institute, in a sense, exemplifies the qualia variety of phenomenal color. The way its colors are geometrically related is a Euclidean layout, such that the geometric distance between the points corresponding to qualia values is proportional to their subjective difference. Now, this is just for phenomenal color. We don't have equivalent maps for every qualia variety and every qualia value; eventually, we want to have them. But even if you have those maps, there is the further question of why those in particular. Why is the qualia of color the way it is? And why are they related in that way? Those are perfectly legitimate questions that really deserve an answer.
3. **The binding problem of consciousness**, which is what we're going to be focusing on today. Of course, they're all interconnected, so providing a solution to the phenomenal binding problem will in turn facilitate solving the other problems.
4. **The problem of causality.** (a) How is it possible that natural selection recruited consciousness for information-processing purposes, or for causal effects? And (b) how is it possible that we can talk about consciousness? What am I doing right now? I'm trying to talk about the structure of my subjective experience. How is that possible? In some sense, that rules out at least a wide variety of epiphenomenalisms — the claim that consciousness in and of itself doesn't have causal power. We're going to dig deep into that, because epiphenomenalism is really complicated. Pretty much every theory of consciousness has one way or another in which epiphenomenalism shows up, and it's very non-trivial to actually solve.

## Going to the Moon: illusionism and partial theories

Different theories of consciousness have different approaches to these problems. I'm going to make an analogy: solving the problem of consciousness is more or less like solving the problem of how to go to the Moon and back.

Take a theory like illusionism, which ultimately says that it's an improper reification — there's really no weird substance that is qualia or anything of the sort. The way it solves the problem of how to go to the Moon and back is by saying that the Moon is an optical illusion; it's not actually there. That sort of explanation would actually work for a rainbow, or for the halo around the Moon. Yes, those are optical phenomena: the rainbow is not physically there where you think it is. But the Moon actually is there, and you can go to the Moon. People in ancient times may not have been convinced of that. They may have thought the Moon was infinitely far away, or a cosmic entity, or indeed just a weird projection — maybe the gods painting the sky — but not really there. Now we know the Moon is actually there, and it is really freaking far, but you can go there.

We're not pursuing the illusionist route. I think there's a sociological component to it: it makes sense to have very broad-brush skepticism around consciousness, because pretty much every solution that has been proposed fails to satisfy all of the constraints that a real theory of consciousness ought to satisfy. So, as a matter of priors, if somebody is proposing a theory of consciousness that is going to satisfy a lot of these constraints, it's overwhelmingly likely that they don't actually have that theory, or that it doesn't work in one way or another. Postulating illusionism, eliminativism, or functionalism is ultimately a way in which you can take this skeptical stance firmly and not make a fool of yourself by actually taking the problem of consciousness seriously. We're not doing that here. We are okay with making fools of ourselves, because we actually want to solve the problem — and the problem can be solved.

A very similar situation that tends to arise is people who dedicate the entirety of their careers to thinking about consciousness only in terms of a subset of these sub-problems. For example, they might exclusively think about causality. I would say integrated information theory, which is now also trying to develop a theory of qualia, is something like that: for the most part, people thinking about IIT are thinking about the causality problem, not necessarily the binding problem, or even why consciousness exists to begin with. It's not really trying to solve all these constraints at once. In the Moon analogy, it's something like trying to solve only how to achieve escape velocity, without taking into account the fact that there is air drag you have to compensate for. Or, if you are taking the air drag into account, you may think that it extends all the way to the Moon, not realizing that at some point it becomes a vacuum — in which case you may not be concerned about building spaceships that are actually airtight. And that's something you really need to do as well.

So by formulating these four sub-problems of consciousness, or Mike's eight sub-problems, I am in some sense saying: "Hey guys, you need to take into account the air drag. You need to take into account the escape velocity, the angle of re-entry, how you're actually going to land on the Moon and get off the Moon again." If you are not holding all these constraints in your mind simultaneously, all of the solutions you come up with are going to be partial. Of course, constraint satisfaction problems can be NP-hard. So what you have to do, to a large extent, is focus on some sub-problems, find a partial solution, see if it matches with the other sub-problems, and iterate over and over until you find something that — as an equilibrium, a point of consilience — solves all of them at once. That can take years and years.

## Desiderata for a solution to the binding problem

Those are the sub-problems. Now let's get into the binding problem more specifically. There is a set of desiderata: what would a solution to the binding problem be required to do? I think a good breakdown is the following:

1. **You have to avoid strong emergence.** I don't believe in strong emergence. I don't think that — as in the hard problem — you can put form and structure together in specific kinds of ways and then, bam, a completely new ontological entity like qualia appears that is unphysical. I don't think that is plausible in any way.
2. **You have to sidestep the hard problem**, either by reformulation or by doing something else. We'll explain how we do that.
3. **You have to avoid epiphenomenalism.** Again: how is it possible that we can talk about consciousness? Why was it recruited by natural selection? And why is our consciousness properly ordered — why does the way I talk about my consciousness causally co-vary with the content and the structure of my consciousness? All of that has to be taken into account.
4. **Whatever solution to the binding problem you provide should ideally be compatible with physicalism.** To remind you, physicalism says that the laws of physics are, in a sense, causally complete: there's causal closure. All of the behavior of what goes on in the universe is accounted for by the laws of physics, or by some future iteration of them that, I don't know, solves the problem of putting quantum mechanics and relativity together. But essentially, the Standard Model of physics gets really close. So let's just say a solution has to be compatible with the Standard Model, and if it's not, that's a huge red flag. I wouldn't necessarily take seriously a theory of consciousness that introduces completely new laws of physics, or denies the laws of physics, or something like that.

We are physicalists at QRI, but not materialists.[^non-materialist-physicalism] Materialism is physicalism plus an addendum that says: "And by the way, all that exists is matter itself" — that the primitive of reality is insentient, brute, non-sentient matter. In some sense, that's a stronger claim. Physicalism is only talking about form, function, and behavior — what things look like from the outside, and whether they can be predicted by the laws of physics. It is silent about the intrinsic nature of the physical.

So those are the desiderata. I'm going to review various possible solutions to the binding problem and show that they don't really work, until we get to our solution, which I think does work.

## What the binding problem is

I should clarify exactly what I mean by the binding problem. If you assume that the world is made of particles and forces between those particles, how is it possible that a hundred billion neurons, distributed across a brain — or across an entire body, if you're an embodied-cognition or embodied-consciousness type of person — how is it possible that something spatially distributed, involving so many particles, somehow comes together to not only behave as a unit but actually instantiate something that is unitary, i.e., your moment of experience? Every moment of experience contains multiple pieces of information at once.

One way in which some philosophers dismiss the phenomenal binding problem is by saying that our consciousness is actually less unified than it seems. That is all well and good. I am very willing to accept that, in fact, a lot of the unity of consciousness is somewhat illusory. If you get very advanced in insight meditation, it becomes really obvious that we draw illusory connections between things, and in some sense we think there's more information there than there really is. But there's a fallacy in extending that observation into saying that the binding problem is not a problem at all. If there is any unification whatsoever — if you have an experience that contains two bits of information at once — that already presents a very, very deep problem. Let alone the complex and rich textures of experience that we're acquainted with in everyday life, or even crazier things, like the super states of consciousness on 5-MeO-DMT or DMT, where there's so much energy, so much intensity of consciousness, all of it bound in each moment of experience. What is going on with that? It doesn't matter how little the unification is: as long as it's non-zero, the binding problem is still a real problem.

*(I do think I have a lot of the illusionist aesthetic, actually. I can't help but notice that some people have pointed out that I look a little bit like a magician. So an illusionist — but not about consciousness.)*

One way in which you can potentially see clearly why the binding problem is such a bizarre thing — indeed, such an existential puzzle — is to ask: where does the observer come from? If you have only particles, interrelated by forces, how do they clump together in order to interact as a unit, and be a unit, with respect to other particles? In fact, if you have an assembly of particles, there's really no principled way of drawing boundaries between them and saying that this subsystem and that subsystem are, in some sense, observing each other. So you have a very, very fundamental **bootstrapping problem**. Suppose you say: "Well, the binding problem is that all these neurons are creating interrelated patterns and high-level structures." Yes, but how do the structures observe themselves? They can't, because they themselves are made of those particles. There's no way of bootstrapping larger structures unless you shift to a different kind of ontology.

## From the binding problem to the boundary problem

One way of talking about the binding problem that actually does advance a possible solution, conceptually, is to reformulate it as the **boundary problem**.[^boundary-problem] This is a shift in ontology. The standard conception, that of classical information processing, is that the world is made of particles and forces, and, to a first approximation, that's it. Of course, there's quantum mechanics, but quantum mechanics really doesn't have obvious macroscopic effects, so probably quantum mechanics is not actually involved in consciousness. So let's go with particles: in that universe, there are particles and there are interactions between them.

But you can start out in a very different ontology, where you say: no, actually, reality is made of fields. Quantum field theory, for sure, but not even just quantum field theory: electromagnetism is a theory of particles, but also of fields. So you do have fields as building blocks of reality. And if everything is a field, then everything is unified to begin with, by default. Then the question becomes: how do boundaries arise within those fields? I think that is actually a much more promising way of thinking about this.

### Classical versus quantum is a false dichotomy

When you talk about this problem, overwhelmingly, in today's zeitgeist, people will say: "Well, to a first approximation, it's just particles, right? For quantum mechanics to matter, you would have to show femtosecond-level computation going on in the brain," which is quite unlikely. But it's a false dichotomy. It's not classical physics versus quantum computing: you actually have field dynamics — macroscopic field dynamics, with electromagnetism.

Then they ask: "But what could possibly tell you that consciousness has any kind of field quality?" There's actually so much that indicates field behavior in your consciousness, and I'm going to go deeper into that. If you notice in meditation, for example, how different components of your experience can enter into sync with one another, it feels like there is resonance between fields going on — quite similarly to how a string in a piano, or let's say a guitar, has this holistic behavior where it moves all at once. It's not moving faster than light or anything of the sort, but you do have **holistic field behavior**. If you pay attention to your experience, you will notice holistic field behavior everywhere, especially in exotic states of consciousness: when you take a psychedelic and experience crazy things like wallpaper symmetry groups.[^algorithmic-reduction] Whenever you experience a symmetry, such as a wallpaper symmetry in the visual field, it behaves as a unit. It starts to form, and then it clicks into place. I would say that behavior is the same kind of holistic field behavior you have when you pluck a guitar string. At the very beginning, you have a wave propagating, and that doesn't look very holistic. But after a little bit, the only frequencies that survive are those that fit an integer number of times in the string, and at that point the string actually starts to behave like a unit. That would be an example of what we call holistic field behavior.

If you pay attention to consciousness, and to psychophysics, there are so many perceptual effects — like the Kanizsa illusions, those three Pac-Men that create an illusory triangle. I've got the book right here: *Conscious Mind, Resonant Brain* by Stephen Grossberg. And Steven Lehar has done a lot of research on this.[^lehar] There is a ton of evidence suggesting that conscious perception is field-like. In some sense, the particle-like nature of experience is actually the exception: it only shows up when you have attentional pinches — something really itching in a part of your body, say, generating these tiny pinches. That's particle-like. But if you introspect on how it affects the rest of your experience, it actually feels much more like waves of energy bouncing off those particle-like pinches. Most of your experience is made of what feels like waves of energy bouncing around. All of that would be field behavior. And yes, I take electromagnetic theories of consciousness very seriously for that reason.

Likewise, if you look at brain waves: brain waves are macroscopic oscillations of the electromagnetic field. They are used routinely for diagnosing diseases and for biofeedback. They're macroscopic, and that would be a holistic field behavior effect. Thinking about this in terms of fields is so much more generative and, I would say, so much more correct — so much closer to the actual phenomenon, and to the properties of the phenomenon we are studying. Especially so if you actually take exotic states of consciousness seriously: nothing on a high dose of LSD makes sense except in light of field behavior. Nothing. Pretty much everything that happens on a high dose of LSD is crazy field interactions. We're going to get back to that.

## Candidate solutions that fail

I think the reformulation of the binding problem as the boundary problem is actually very generative. Not every solution does that, but I think only solutions that do have any chance whatsoever of actually solving this problem. So let's go through the list.

### Cellular automata

Let's start with cellular automata. As far as I can tell, it's a common belief. I remember somebody in college saying: "Oh, don't you believe that a glider is conscious? Maybe minimally conscious?" Okay — but what defines the boundary of a glider? Here's exactly the problem I was talking about: how do you bootstrap an observer? We, as conscious entities looking at the cellular automaton, can say: "Oh yeah, the glider forms a natural functional unit." But the glider doesn't see itself. To presume that a glider is conscious, you actually need to postulate something very weird: a kind of extra-dimensional reality compiler, some kind of god entity, looking at the cellular automaton of reality and saying: "Oh yeah, this looks like an integrated pattern. Let's give it a stamp of approval and give it a soul," or something like that.

Because if you don't have that — if you're just looking at what is truly going on in the cellular automaton — there is no glider. There are just black and white squares flickering. That's all. Maybe if you consider the entire grid, the entire lattice, as a unified entity, that could be unified. But otherwise, you're going to be stuck with each cell being its own being, at best. In which case, a glider is not suddenly generating a new being. That's an illusion. It's an illusion because we have the capacity for parsing visual data and extracting things that are more or less functionally unitary. In that sense, yes, gliders are a thing — a thingy; they have some thinginess to them. But for sure, they are not natural units in any form whatsoever. It's illusory. So, to be a little bit dramatic, we can cross off cellular automata. I'm not trying to be offensive or mean, but they're not a very plausible solution.

### Complexity

Now complexity. Some people seriously say: "Oh, it's just a matter of complexity. When you have enough complexity in a system, all of a sudden you have this unity." But complexity is ill-defined. In fact, there are a gazillion different definitions of what complexity might be, and none of them can, in a principled way, actually solve the bootstrapping problem, or give you the necessary discontinuity to say: "Here is the boundary around this complex phenomenon" — unless you define it that way. But then it's something that you are defining. If you set criteria — "this and this and this are the criteria for a minimally complex conscious entity" — again, that's in your mind. That is you looking at the cellular automaton and saying: "Oh, this glider looks like it meets the minimum criteria." But it's not actually doing anything at the ontological level.

And you do need some kind of modification to the ontological substrate of reality in order to create actual new beings, new moments of experience. Again, this sounds completely impossible to do, especially with the criteria laid out, which are very harsh — they include avoiding strong emergence. Gliders being conscious, or complexity giving rise to consciousness and unity of experience, would be strong emergence. So complexity fails that criterion. Believe it or not, our solution actually satisfies the criterion of avoiding strong emergence, and hopefully you'll see that in a second. But complexity is off the list.

### Synchrony

Synchrony. There is actually quite a bit of neuroscientific backing for the claim that synchrony in the nervous system is associated with local binding. Here's an important piece of vocabulary. There is **global binding**: take any two qualia in your experience — the fact that they belong to the same experience means that they are globally bound. **Local binding** means that the features of your experience are connected in such a way that they generate a coherent percept within your experience. For example, the color and the shape of my glasses are locally bound: the green is not locally bound to my eyes — you're not seeing green eyes — but it is locally bound to the frame of the glasses.

This kind of binding can actually break down. There are people with specific kinds of brain damage who are able to tell that there is green in the scene but not what it belongs to. And on LSD, for example, you will see a drifting effect where the green sometimes belongs to the frame and sometimes belongs to something else. There's a dislodging of local binding: the local binding connections become unstable, and you get exotic bindings.

So that is local binding. And empirically, synchrony does seem to be very, very tightly related to local binding. When there is synchrony between, for example, an area of your visual cortex that has been identified with color perception and some other part of the visual cortex that has been identified with shape perception, that will usually mean that they are locally binding those features. But this is an empirical observation. Why would synchrony actually solve the binding problem?

To begin with, it doesn't solve the global binding problem: a lot of the things that belong to the same experience are not in synchrony in your brain. So right off the bat, empirically, it doesn't work. Even if you were to say, "Well, maybe it is synchrony in a different sense — maybe coherence" (there are many additional definitions and statistical constructs, some of them more plausible than others; coherence is a very important neuroscientific concept, a generalization of synchrony) — why can't that be the case? Because it is not frame-invariant, and in that sense it would actually be incompatible with physicalism.

Here's the thing. Whether two neurons are activated at the same time depends on your frame of reference. What is simultaneous with a given neuron will depend on which neuron you're looking at, and on its movement. Well, neurons are not moving very much, but you would need to localize them. And there's no universal plane of simultaneity; we know that from relativity. So there is no matter-of-fact ground truth about what is in synchrony with what. I'm sorry: synchrony is not a brute fact of reality, so it's not going to solve the binding problem. But it is a very important hint, and we will come back to what synchrony is doing in our solution. Synchrony is very relevant, but it's not ontologically fundamental, and in that sense it's not a solution. Sorry — synchrony gets removed from the list.

### Integrated information

Integrated information. Oh, gosh. Giulio Tononi and his lab are doing fantastic work. What I really admire about integrated information theory is that they are, in some sense, the first movers for a qualia-formalist account of consciousness: they take very seriously the idea that you can translate a physical system into a mathematical object, and then, in some sense, read off what is going on in the conscious experience of that system by looking at the features of the mathematical object that is isomorphic to it. So IIT is fantastic in that it is exploring that. But the devil is in the details. If you actually look at the specifics of the math of IIT, there are strong reasons to be skeptical that it is something that will work out.

In particular, I have what I think is a silver-bullet critique of IIT, having to do with their postulate number four and how they define the thing called the minimum information partition.[^iit-postulates] This is all unfortunately quite technical, but just to give you the gist: when you're analyzing a system and computing the integrated information of each of the partitions of that system, they say that you look at all of them, and the largest one has — in the words of Giulio Tononi — "the highest claim of existence,"[^exclusion] and therefore it is the one thing that is conscious in that system. There are a lot of problems with that, but the biggest, most glaring one is that it actually makes consciousness epiphenomenal. And that's crazy, because the whole point of IIT is to reduce consciousness to, in some sense, irreducible causality — integrated causality. But when you say, "Yes, there are all of these integrated subsystems, but I'm only going to say that the largest one is conscious, because it has the highest claim of existence," that is in your mind — in your brain. It's like the glider thing. It's not actually having any further causal effects.

That's very different from physics. In physics, there are what are called extremal principles: you can tell where a photon will travel by looking at what is, in some sense, the least action — the shortest path, and so on. That is not epiphenomenal. If you want to compute, for example, where an electron will travel, you use the path-integral formulation by Feynman: you assume that the electron travels through every possible path, absolutely every possible path, interacting with itself in every possible way. Then you add up the amplitudes and the phases of all of them, and it turns out that almost all of them cancel out; essentially only those with very similar trajectories have enough amplitude to make a dent or a difference. So the minimal path for an electron is not epiphenomenal, because all the other paths are cancelling each other out, and that is a causal effect. Whereas in IIT, the minimum information partition is not cancelling every other partition, and therefore it is epiphenomenal. It is like a glider: it's you projecting it into that system. It's not doing any computational legwork, so as is, it couldn't even be recruited by natural selection. I do have hope for a future version of IIT going somewhere deeper, but for the time being, in its current formulation, it's not going to solve the binding problem, because it's epiphenomenal. So integrated information is not it.

### Causality

How about causality? Some people may try to explain the binding problem in terms of irreducible causality, very similarly to IIT. Consider this (sorry, I'm just using some random objects here — I don't know if I can do this): imagine a row of dominoes. One falls, and then the next one falls. You can trace back exactly which one made which one fall. Okay, fine. But what if one of them, call it C, is very heavy, so that the two before it, A and B, have to fall onto it at once? In some sense, you have an irreducible causal effect, where A and B need to put pressure on C simultaneously for C to move. So that could be an approach: saying that consciousness is when you have assemblies of causal structures such that you need all of them at once in order to effect the change that you observe in the world.

That is promising, but there are two problems with it. First, again, lack of frame invariance. When you're talking about integrated causality — about exactly what caused what — you have the problem of simultaneity, and it's not clear that you will be able to say that A and B were actually simultaneously putting pressure on C for it to move. That might be possible to solve with some tricks, perhaps. But the deeper problem is that now you don't have a boundary in time. Maybe with integrated causality you're solving a bit of the boundary in space, but I don't think you can solve the boundary in time. How do you draw that boundary? A and B sure are effecting the change in C, but "C moving" is not a natural kind. There's no sharp discontinuity in physics marking when that is happening, and in that sense it's not a true solution.

Everything is causing everything else in the universe. You, right now, are the result of the entire backward light cone, all the way from the Big Bang, which involves a huge number of galaxies and black holes colliding, and enormous processes. In some sense, you are deeply connected with all of that. But are you irreducibly that entire light cone? No: you're a much, much smaller part of reality than that. Your consciousness doesn't have all of that information explicitly there — maybe implicitly, but then we get into some issues. Anyway, causality has the problem that there are no boundaries in the time direction, so we can rule it out as well.

### Spatial proximity

How about spatial proximity? This is a very bad one, but it raises a very, very important question: why are my left hemisphere and my right hemisphere simultaneously contributing to one experience, but not my hemisphere and your hemisphere, even if we're in the same room? Why are they not simultaneously contributing to, let's say, the same visual field? Somebody might say: "Spatial proximity — they're just closer." But you could do brain surgery and squish your brain against mine without adding connections, and I don't think that would actually generate just one entity. So I think spatial proximity is totally a red herring. Credit where credit is due: a lot of functionalists deeply get this. People who believe that a Turing machine can be conscious rightfully understand that spatial distance is not necessary — things can be very far apart and still contribute to the same experience. I do believe that.

Very similar to that view is the number of connections, and IIT would say something like that: the left and right hemispheres have a lot of connections, whereas my brain and your brain don't. But again, that is not a hard boundary, because even if we're just in the same room, we are still causally connected — less so, but connected. Yet the difference between my experience and your experience actually seems to be a hard boundary. We are different experiences, and it's not a matter of degree; it's not as if we were blended. We're strictly different experiences. So spatial proximity, and a coefficient of causal interrelationship, are not going to work as full solutions.

### Behavioral coherence

Behavioral coherence is another thing that people talk about, and I also think it doesn't work. Essentially, it conflates behavioral or functional unity with ontological unity. Just because an organism is behaving as if there were a unitary subject driving it doesn't mean that there is a unitary subject driving it. Why? Because, in the worst-case scenario, rather than a nervous system, you could have a lookup table. It could behave as if it were unified in a coherent way without actually being so. So behavioral coherence is not a solution to the binding problem in the least.

### The Mach principle

The Mach principle: this is a very niche theory, by Ken Mogi, the Sony qualia guy, whom I really like — I really love his book on ikigai — and who takes these ideas very seriously.[^mogi] His attempted solution to the binding problem is essentially this idea: a photon that goes from one star to another may take, from an external observer's point of view, millions of years, but from the frame of reference of the photon itself, it's instantaneous.[^photon-proper-time] So, in some sense, binding across time can be done by things that travel at the speed of light: the way different paths of things moving at that speed coalesce is capable of binding things that are distributed across time, and in that sense of creating internally complex unitary states.

I don't think it works, for the same reason that causality, or integrated causality, doesn't work: there is no boundary across time. By the Mach principle, we would also be, in a way, in exactly the same moment as the Big Bang — unless you count each interaction as its own moment. But then, in physics, most interactions are just going to be particles colliding with each other, and that's not enough information for a full conscious experience. And the moment we're talking about something at the level of neurons, I think the Mach principle just goes out the window, unfortunately. So I don't think it works, but I do really like it as a very creative approach, and it's very novel, as far as I can tell.

### Resonance

The last two: one is very close, and the last one is, I think, the actual solution. Number nine is resonance, and Steven Lehar talks about it. Again, let's say you took LSD and you're seeing your visual field tessellate: there is resonance between the various components of your visual field. Something we have pointed out at QRI — I don't believe others have figured this out when it comes to explaining meditation effects and psychedelic effects — is that there is a kind of **duality between temporal synchrony and spatial symmetry**. When you experience something perfectly symmetrical in your visual field, that entails that there are harmonic resonant waves that are actually synchronized. Temporal synchronization and spatial symmetry come together. So there is an interesting revival here: resonance and synchrony are very, very related, as possible solutions to the binding problem.

But what makes resonance especially tempting as a possible solution to the binding problem is that it gives you functional unity for free. When you have a system where A is resonant with B, which is resonant with C, you will have some degree of functional transitivity, where the entire system behaves as a unit. In fact, when two things are in resonance, then across the dimensions of resonance, if you modify one, you will see that modification reflected in the other. Not instantaneously, because nothing travels faster than the speed of light — and certainly not acoustic or mechanical vibrations. Forget it; this is not an instantaneous thing. But it can be fairly fast; it depends on the frequencies of oscillation. If the frequency of oscillation is really fast, resonance essentially, functionally, works as a glue: things that are in a state of resonance are functionally glued together. It actually works as a force — as if a rigid tube were connecting these components.

And I think that's the case with our experience. The components of our experience that are in a local binding connection are such that if you move one of them, the other moves as well. When I move this object — a Megaminx, a Rubik's-cube-like puzzle — the colors you're binding to these particular shapes, and to the overall construct of the Megaminx, are actually in a state of resonance with each other. I do believe that, and I think you can confirm it in the lab in a number of ways. That form of resonance is what allows you to very quickly compute, in a cross-modal fashion, where the entirety of the object is located and at what angle you're seeing it. Resonance actually makes the job of perception a lot easier, because the things that are meant to be connected with each other are properly connected.

Again, in an altered state of consciousness, that can break down, and you can have resonance between features of an experience that are not actually bound together in the world, or that there's any reason to bind in your mind. For example, if you're on LSD watching this, you may experience resonance between my eyes and an object behind me. They may create a symmetrical or vortex-type pattern. That will behave as a unit and have this glue property. But it's, in a sense, a maladaptive perception, because there's nothing in the physical world actually connecting them at that level. Still, I think resonance in the field of awareness is very obviously there, especially once you dig into psychophysics, visual illusions, and what happens in meditation and on psychedelics. Resonance is all over the place. It's very, very stunning that it's not currently a bigger component of modern neuroscience.

The problem with resonance, though, is that even though it does solve the binding problem — how these components of reality are, in some sense, connected to each other — it doesn't solve the boundary problem. Why? Because two things being in a state of resonance doesn't draw a natural boundary around them. You still need this mythical compiler that looks at the system and says: "Oh yeah, this and this and this are in resonance, so let's consider them a unit." But again, that would be the glider operation: something you're hallucinating, projecting into the world, that is not necessarily there.

So what is actually going on here? As far as I know, the same problem comes up for every theorist who proposes an electromagnetic theory of consciousness — for example, the cemi field theory by McFadden,[^cemi] or Susan Pockett, who identifies consciousness with local field potentials.[^pockett] I think the case is really strong that there is something really there. Just to give you the gist of it: it seems that there are a lot of unconscious processes in the brain. There's actually a lot of neural activity that doesn't generate conscious experiences — that doesn't paint any qualia on your experience — and the activity that does seems to be precisely the activity that generates local field potentials. As Steven Lehar would say, most neural activity is sandblasting the fields of electromagnetism. And your experience is those local field potentials stitched together into a crazy pattern of competing clusters of coherence, in a state of global, interconnected resonance. But local field potentials don't have an obvious boundary — or at least, you need more than just saying that local field potentials solve the binding problem. You still have the boundary problem.

## The solution: topological segmentation

So how do we actually solve it? Here's the solution, guys — here it is for all of you. The solution is **topological segmentation**. Here it is, roughly.

First of all, we start with a panpsychist account: what exists is the fields of physics, and they are fields of qualia. If you're not on board with that, there's not much we can do. I can tell you that I've searched through all possible — at least all humanly conceivable — theories of reality, and this is what seems to make the most sense and to satisfy as many of our constraints as possible. So imagine that the fields of physics are actually qualia, and that they start out unified. In some sense, the entirety of the fields of physics starts out as a natural unit — a super-experience, in a way, but not a mind. For the most part, it's not an intelligent experience. That doesn't arise automatically: you actually need a very complex process of natural selection to select intelligent patterns that are good at information processing, for survival and so on.

In an analogy with this balloon, the entire field is made of qualia — let's say, the yellow of the balloon. Well, maybe, maybe not. A topological segmentation is something that can arise naturally, as a direct effect of the laws of physics, without introducing new clauses. If you twist a balloon like this one, at some point it collapses and forms a pinch point, which actually generates a **global boundary**. We can define a global boundary as follows: if you're at a point in one of these fields, then to go to a point in the other field, you need to go through the pinch point. That necessity — that you *have* to go through the pinch point — is what makes it a global boundary.

![[balloon-topological-segmentation.svg|Twisting a balloon shrinks a ring around its middle to a single pinch point; every path from a point a in one segment to a point b in the other must cross it — a global boundary.]]

A **local boundary**, by contrast, would be a pinch that only partly closes the balloon — say, pressing it with your fingers so that it narrows without closing off. From any point in this field to any other point, you have the option of going through that pinch point, but it's not necessary; you're not forced to go through it. That would be a local boundary. And what I'm going to suggest is that local binding is actually generated by these local boundaries, and global binding is actually the result of global boundaries.

### Lessons from chemical bonding

Let me paint this picture more closely. There's a wonderful, super-classic book called *Valence*, by C. A. Coulson — no, not emotional valence; this is chemical valence. Not the same. How do molecules get formed? How do you get molecular bonding? That has obviously been a very big area of research for many years. You start out with very simple rules, such as the Lewis rules of molecular bonding: you have the orbitals, you have the octet rule, and that is the rule that makes it happen. But it seems very, very top-down. Where do those laws come from? It's not obvious that it should be the case. Instead, if you start out with the fields of physics — in quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation — and assume that the behavior of reality is actually ruled by the Schrödinger equation, and that it's actually a bunch of fields, then you can derive Lewis's rules as a good approximation to that.

But it doesn't always work, and there are all sorts of crazy exceptions — for example, pi bonds: the weird orbitals that arise when you have orbitals of the same polarity right next to each other, or the pi electrons of aromatic molecules. Very crazy stuff that looks like strong emergence. And it *would* be strong emergence if you had only an ontology of particles and forces. The pi electron would be a strongly emergent thing; it would arise out of nothing, as a new law that you have to invent — "the pi electron law." But if you have a fields-of-physics ontology and a Schrödinger equation, the pi electron arises totally naturally. It's just there, as a necessary consequence of that ontology.

Another beautiful thing about chemistry is that the actual orbitals that arise, and the resonant modes of a molecule, are precisely of the holistic-field-behavior kind. They are the fixed points of the possible resonant modes of all of the components of the molecule simultaneously — in other words, the fixed points of the dynamical system. As such, to derive what they are, you actually need to take the entire object into account simultaneously. This is not something that would arise naturally from point particles alone: it's inherently field-like behavior that molecules behave as units.

Now, just because they behave as units doesn't make them units, okay? But imagine — and this is something I don't know for sure; it's for the sake of illustration — that when you have orbitals like that, a topological boundary actually arises. If that is the case (and I'm not claiming it is; this is an example), then you have a beautiful correspondence between the creation of a new being — a new segment with a natural boundary — and something that behaves as a unit. That is the marriage between ontological emergence and functional units. It's not strong emergence, because you're not applying new laws out of the blue; but it is ontological emergence: the emergence of a new being, a new segment of the universe. And all of a sudden, this new object, this new segment, behaves as a unit.

With quantum orbitals, I would say that's still very much an open question. But when it comes to macroscopic objects like this balloon, it is absolutely the case that holistic behavior emerges. If I create this pinch point and make the balloon vibrate very fast, you will see that, because of the pinch point, this entire surface will have its own resonant modes, and it will behave as a unit — a subcomponent. You actually need that for a lot of things in physics. Take these bubbles, which I'm blowing through a straw into a glass of soapy water. This is not strong emergence. But if you want to analyze bubbles at the macroscopic level and simulate them — if you're in graphics or something like that — you need some kind of topological engine that tells you which face is connected to which face, at what intersection; whether three or four faces intersect at one single point; whether they share the same plane. There are so many topological considerations. Mathematically speaking, this would be what's called a CW complex, because you essentially have cells of different dimensions — 2D surfaces, lines, and points — and the entire system and its behavior really are predicated on the topological structure that arises. When you take one face of one of these bubbles and make it vibrate, you have to look at that segment and its resonant modes, and it will behave somewhat like a unit. Not 100% like a unit, because it's still connected to the rest of the structure, but it will have its own individual harmonic resonant modes.

So what I'm claiming is that the brain is such that its resonant modes actually pinch the electromagnetic field and generate these **topological pockets**. And the reason this was selected by natural selection — why it can be useful for an organism — is that by pinching the electromagnetic field in that way, all of a sudden you have the resonant modes of that pinch, which is holistic field behavior: something you can actually use for computation.

### How topological segmentation meets the desiderata

So let's review.

1. **We avoid strong emergence**, because we're just using the laws of physics. These topological segmentations are natural; as with the bubbles, you don't need a new law. However, you do get a new ontological structure: a new segment of the universe.
2. **We sidestep the hard problem entirely, via panpsychism.** This is obviously a rabbit hole, but, for example, David Chalmers has a paper called "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism."[^combination-problem] He explains how panpsychism overcomes some problems with the hard problem, but then you face the problem that there are no natural boundaries. As a response to that paper, I'm going to say that topological boundaries can be a wonderful candidate for actually solving the combination problem for panpsychism.[^tsc-2020]
3. **We avoid epiphenomenalism.** Why? Again, because by generating these topological boundaries, you segment the wave function in such a way that you get holistic field behavior. You actually get things behaving as a unit, and that can be used for computational and causal purposes, so it's not epiphenomenal. Your consciousness, and the boundaries of your consciousness, are actually relevant, because they define what is going to have harmonic resonant modes. So we are sidestepping epiphenomenalism, which is wonderful.
4. **And finally, it's compatible with physicalism.** I'm not going to say that I have the equations showing that the connectome-specific harmonic waves of the brain[^cshw] actually generate topological pinches in the electromagnetic field. No, I don't have that yet. But I will tell you that topological structures in the electromagnetic field are routine. They are all over the place. You're not going to find them described that way in physics textbooks, but if you look up the cohomology of the electromagnetic field, you will see a vast, vast literature specifically about this, which looks into things like the Hopf fibration, where regions of the electromagnetic field generate pockets in which the field lines get trapped. Because electromagnetic fields are inherently three-dimensional, these pockets are non-trivial. Sometimes they are eddies, but not eddies in the standard sense, like eddies of water: they're topological eddies. Even an antenna wouldn't be able to propagate signals very far if it didn't do a topological twist that generates these topological eddies. So topological segmentation in the fields of physics is routine; it's all over the place. It's the sort of thing advanced physicists have to take into account when modeling physical behavior.

So I'm not saying that I have the equations. I am saying that, so far, the story is completely compatible with physicalism. I think we just need to do the legwork — the mathematical legwork — to identify precisely what specific aspect of the cohomology of the electromagnetic field gets affected by neural activity. That's going to completely close the problem, and we will actually have the full solution. I'm very excited. I think it's possible. I think it's 100% within reach — definitely within our lifetimes.

## Implications

I'm going to finish off with some interesting implications of this. It may sound trivial: what can you really do with local boundaries? That's not a full experience. Well, I take very seriously the idea that the holistic field behavior of our experience uses properties of the electromagnetic field for causal effects that manifest, or project, into qualia space.

For example, when you feel very tense — I know this may sound ridiculous to some people, but no, seriously — when your kidney hurts, or you have back pain or something like that, people say that the field of their awareness feels knotted. That might be something like the balloon self-intersecting a little bit, which is tense. If you were to analyze the differential-geometry properties of the fields, you would say: "Wow, around here in my back — or in the part of my somatosensory cortex that represents my back — you see a lot of divergence and curl," which are differential-geometry properties of the electromagnetic field, and they affect each other in a very, very non-trivial way. So maybe one configuration like this is back pain, and lots of pinches would be a different type of pain. Being completely relaxed, or 5-MeO-DMT, is like getting rid of all of your internal boundaries, so that you have a smooth, very smooth field of awareness.

### DMT: knots and wormholes

But what else? The ways in which the field could self-intersect and generate these CW complexes are vast; I think there's a huge number of possibilities. Here's the first non-trivial example: you can knot yourself. If you knot yourself, then from the point of view of the surface of this field, it's very bizarre. The field self-intersects in one place, so it shares a boundary there, and in another place too; if we were to unfold it, it would be this weird thing. It's not only that you get a pinch: it's actually a wormhole. That's another of the crazy things. You see, I have a huge rant in here.

No: what DMT actually does is generate a gazillion local binding connections across your experiential field, which is much more like this. You're knotting — creating these knots in your experiential field, which are like wormholes. When you have local binding connections between components of your experience, it's as if there were wormholes between them. Essentially, the attention field lines teleport from one place to another. So in a DMT experience, your visual field becomes something intricate, like the weird, accordion-like, complex topological shape I've twisted this balloon into. It's so much more profound — so much deeper an alteration — than merely overexciting your visual cortex. No, no, no, you're just trivializing. You're not actually paying attention to the phenomena. You have to look at it. Look at it; pay attention.

Another, slightly more non-trivial example: you can do a double twist. Again, you're making these crazy wormholes of local binding connections within your own experience.

### Dissociative identity

Or, even crazier, you might have multiple topologically segmented surfaces — complexes — in your experience. What is this? This could be a dissociative identity. You did an unfortunate twist somewhere that actually created two topological surfaces within your brain, and all of a sudden you're actually two: there are two subjects of experience in your brain. And maybe one of them inflates a lot and the other one deflates, so one personality is mostly active and the other is mostly dormant, and maybe they flip-flop. Some of the craziest things that happen in brains are multiple personalities and dissociative disorders; it's very bizarre.
