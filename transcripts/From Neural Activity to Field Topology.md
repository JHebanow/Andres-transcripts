## 0. What's a coupling kernel?

The first thing I want to talk about is the **coupling kernel**.[^coupling-kernel] This came up when I was trying to find a way to interact with systems of coupled oscillators in a reliable, predictable way — without having to train sophisticated neural networks or control systems, and without hard-coding specific network geometries for particular control systems. I wanted a way of interfacing with a system of coupled oscillators that was natural and bottom-up, but also reliable and predictable.

What I ultimately arrived at — by coding dozens of systems of coupled oscillators and wiring them in different ways — was that the first thing with clear utility, the thing that clearly started to replicate some psychedelic phenomenology, was **Kuramoto oscillators**, geometrized. I'm not going to get into the mathematical details; they're not very complicated, but they're not necessary for this conceptual overview.

In the classic formulation of Kuramoto oscillators, you have an all-to-all relationship: every oscillator is connected to every other oscillator, and they're all trying to be in phase. When they're out of phase, there's an error. Think of metronomes on a table, tuned to slightly different natural frequencies, so they want to oscillate at slightly different speeds. But because they all sit on the same table — the same ground — there are tiny shared vibrations, and through those vibrations they pull and push each other. The more out of phase they are, the stronger the correcting mechanism realigning them. In this classic formulation they're all equally connected, and there's some fascinating mathematics you can show analytically: under certain convergence conditions, all the oscillators click together, dragged along by the mean phase of the ensemble. It's a very tractable system. It's not trying to accurately model any physical system of oscillators; it's more of a mathematical abstraction that just works nicely.

But in the realm of phenomenology we're talking about very complex networks — your visual cortex, your vasculature, your somatosensory cortex. You have many networks connected in different ways, and it's not the case that all oscillators are connected to each other. Instead there's some geometric embedding: all the oscillators live in some geometric space, and whether two oscillators are connected is a function of their distance. In the simple toy models used here, there might be a rule like: if two oscillators are within a certain distance, they're connected; if they're farther, they're not. That way you represent a geometric space in terms of connectivity between coupled oscillators.

What I stumbled upon by playing with these systems was that we can modulate the **coupling constant** between oscillators at different distances, and that way gain control over the overall emergent oscillations at the macroscopic level. Here's how it works. For any given oscillator on, say, a square lattice, there are four oscillators at distance one from it. So there's a lever whose value modulates a positive or negative coupling coefficient for oscillators at that distance. (For those who are curious, this is called **Manhattan distance**: you add up the distance along the x-axis and the y-axis independently. It's called Manhattan distance because it's the number of blocks you'd walk to get from one place to another on a city grid — you don't care about the diagonal, you care about streets plus avenues.) At distance two, the set of oscillators forms a diamond shape, with eight oscillators; there's a lever modulating the coupling constant to those eight. Then twelve oscillators at distance three, sixteen at distance four, and so on. So the central idea of a coupling kernel is a system of levers modulating the coupling constant of the oscillators at each of those Manhattan distances.

![[coupling-kernel-levers.svg]]

It doesn't have to be Manhattan distance — that's just for a square grid. In a 3D continuous space (and a lot of our simulations are like that) it can be Euclidean distance. It can also be distance in other geometries: on the surface of a sphere, or in hyperbolic space. This is very general. But for simplicity, in a square lattice, we're talking about x plus y — streets and avenues.
### Small-world networks

Before going further, I'll briefly explain what a **small-world network** is, because there's another lever we usually include. At the beginning of a simulation, for every oscillator we choose two other oscillators at random from the entire grid and wire them to it. The small-world lever modulates the coupling constant to those two random connections — effectively a random network layered on top.

![[small-world-random-wiring.svg]]

We call it "small-world" because of the well-known fact that the social distance between people is actually pretty small on average — between you and Queen Elizabeth, say (the classic example), or anybody in the world. Chances are you know somebody who knows somebody who knows that person; the number of hops usually doesn't exceed six. There's a quantity called the **diameter** of a graph: the pair of nodes whose shortest path is the longest — that longest shortest-path is the diameter. In a purely geometric network, where nodes connect only to neighbors within a certain distance, the diameter is very large, because you actually have to walk through the space. Maybe in medieval times, before long roads and long-distance travel, social distance between individuals was large. But not anymore: there was a threshold in social evolution where enough people were making long trips that it effectively added a random network on top of the geometric one. When you do that, the diameter shrinks and it becomes a small world. You don't need many random connections — there's a threshold percentage at which the diameter completely collapses and everybody is suddenly right next to everybody else.

So adding a small-world network is one important way of modulating the activity of the system: either scrambling the activity, when the small-world coupling coefficient is negative, or synchronizing everybody, when it's positive. You don't have to worry about it too much — it's a hack to significantly increase the state space of configurations accessible through the lever system. For now, let's focus on the geometric components: coupling one, two, three, and four.

## 1. Coupling kernels as neural-global bridge

Early on, one thing I did was apply a **discrete cosine transform** to this system, even just on a flat system. As you can see in the companion article, the discrete cosine transform is like a Fourier transform: it shows the extent to which waves of different spatial frequencies are present. (For context: a wave with a low spatial frequency is a very large wave; a wave with a high spatial frequency is a very intricate, detailed one.)

It turns out you can tune into **global resonant modes** of the entire system of coupled oscillators just by modulating the coefficients of the coupling kernel — the coupling of one, two, three, and four. This was very intuitive to me, but it turns out you can rigorously prove it using techniques from **spectral graph theory**, where you effectively apply filters on a graph for different distances. I won't bore you with the technicalities, but the various powers of the **Laplacian** of the system modulate the amplitude of different resonant modes at different spatial frequencies.

So we stumbled upon a way of modulating the amplitude of energy for waves of different spatial frequencies — which is mind-blowing, because systems of coupled oscillators are usually considered very complex, intractable, hard-to-predict dynamical systems. The fact that you can tune into their global resonant modes just by modulating the amplitude of a coupling kernel is fascinating. It gives you a handle with predictable, holistic, global resonance effects across the entire brain — or the entire graph, in this case — just by modulating levers. That was an important milestone for me in making sense of complex systems of coupled oscillators.

And it turns out I'm not the first to realize this. In November of last year, at a QRI work retreat in Tepoztlán, we encountered the work of **Marco Aqil**. I went to his thesis defense, and he presented at the Amsterdam QRI meetup — so, thank you very much, and that was awesome. His PhD thesis, which he defended last month, is called *Levels of Vision*.[^levels-of-vision] It touches on some of this, and it contains another key piece of the puzzle: **divisive normalization**, which I'll explain in a moment.

Marco developed a method called **CHAOSS**,[^chaoss] which is a way of analyzing the resonant modes of a brain by modulating something mathematically equivalent to a coupling kernel — although he doesn't call it exactly that. To me that was mind-blowing: in academia they're arriving at something quite similar from a very different perspective. From our perspective, we're taking phenomenology seriously — trying to replicate the features of exotic states of consciousness using graph theory and dynamical systems. In his case, they're not so much trying to replicate phenomenology; they're trying to replicate psychophysical and neuroscientific measurements, neuroimaging datasets — the objective, third-person scientific measurements of the brain. And all of a sudden that converges onto very similar ideas and models to QRI, which is effectively championing taking phenomenology seriously and bringing advanced meditators, physicists, mathematicians, and visual artists together to depict and make sense of exotic states of consciousness. So, CHAOSS converging on very similar ideas.

To recap: coupling kernels are a bridge between neural activity and global activity.

### Divisive normalization

The other very relevant thing from Marco is **divisive normalization**,[^divisive-normalization] a paradigm he has developed and furthered quite a bit (though he didn't invent it). The idea is that the probability for a given oscillator in the brain — populations of neurons, not individual neurons — to oscillate is a function of both the sensory inputs going into them and the activity in their surround. This is especially relevant in layered systems, where you have several layers of neurons with intra-layer (lateral) connections as well as connections between layers. Divisive normalization is a mathematical technique for explaining how the activity of one layer triggers activity in the next; you can repeat that over and over.

The really cool thing — without yet making precise claims connecting divisive-normalization parameters to the effects of specific substances (we want to get there, and we're crafting studies to eventually publish on it, but we're not there yet) — is that conceptually the picture starts to become crisp. You have this low-level "canonical computation," a method for making sense of how contextual activity in populations of neurons translates into things like traveling waves and dynamical behavior, which through something like a coupling kernel (or CHAOSS) can then be understood as global resonant modes of the entire system. Suddenly the brain starts to look more tractable. So I highly recommend diving into divisive normalization, CHAOSS, and Marco Aqil's work. He's building on work by **Morten Kringelbach** and **Selen Atasoy**,[^connectome-harmonics] and that whole tradition of making sense of the brain as a dynamical system with turbulent processes and resonant modes is starting to cash out into things that are very concrete, and maybe even insightful at the level of phenomenology.

## 2. Geometric constraints on coupling effects

The next point is more uniquely QRI: geometric constraints as modulators of coupling effects.

If you have a hierarchical system of coupled oscillators — like the visual cortex — a particular coupling kernel will have predictable effects, but those effects differ from what you'd get if the network topology and geometry were simpler, like a plain grid. The main point is that the network architecture and the coupling kernel *come together* to give rise to the emergent dynamic effects. A substance might be modulating the coupling kernel, but the effect it has in your consciousness depends on the network geometry it's acting on.

One important takeaway: a psychedelic effect — the effect of LSD, of mescaline, of DMT — may actually be very simple. Through divisive normalization it instantiates a coupling kernel, and that kernel affects different systems differently depending on their network architecture, in a predictable way. Consider LSD: it affects your visual phenomenology, your auditory phenomenology, your tactile phenomenology, and there's something similar across all of them. If you introspect, the bodily feeling on LSD has the same vibratory tone as what's happening in the visual field — there are differences, but also strong similarities. It would be very parsimonious — Occam's razor would be beautiful here — if we could explain that diversity of effects with one simple coupling kernel induced by the substance.

Ultimately it won't be *quite* that simple, because different systems have different divisive normalization and therefore different coupling kernels. But I do think that, to a first approximation, we'll be able to characterize LSD in terms of a coupling kernel in a cross-modal fashion — a characterization that works the same across different sensory modalities, and even across entirely different systems, like the cardiovascular system. One of the fascinating things I've learned recently is that the vascular system is very active, and it's not just the heart: there are smooth muscle cells modulating vasodilation and constriction. This is serotonin-mediated, with 5-HT1A and 5-HT2A as key receptors modulating some of that circuit-level activity, which would cash out in terms of divisive normalization and, as a consequence, coupling kernels. So we're developing a paradigm for making sense of how different types of waves might travel through your cardiovascular system as a function of taking a particular substance. To me this is mind-blowing — potentially massive explanatory power from very simple principles, which is exactly what we want in science.

## 3. Network geometry interactions and *Projective Intelligence*

But there's more. I recently made a video, and have written, about a concept we're calling **projective intelligence**.[^projective-intelligence] I recommend watching the video for more depth, though here it gets contextualized within our overall recent paradigm.

The overall idea is that a lot of what conscious intelligence is about is the interaction between fields of different dimensionalities and topologies. There's a constant drive in your conscious system to sync up and reduce the error signals between your visual field and your tactile field. When you see something and try to grab it, you're anticipating tactile sensations — a somatic feeling as a consequence of that movement. This is an insight that originally came from **Roger Thisdell**, **Wystan Bryan-Scott**, **Cube Flipper**, and **Asher Arataki** at the QRI-sponsored Canada retreat for researching meditation and exotic states of consciousness:[^pure-perception] almost always, visual sensations are somatically tracked. Part of what lets you have a visual working memory is that you anchor visual qualia and geometry to tactile sensations, and that may be essential for object permanence.

I've talked to people with autism, for example, who have difficulties with object permanence, and it might be because that connection is a little severed or broken — visual sensations aren't super well tracked by somatic sensations. That generates strange errors and deficiencies that are very hard to diagnose. I'd describe it as a failure mode of projective intelligence: visual sensations not being somatically tracked properly. A lot has to happen for the tracking to work: the anticipation of somatic sensations must be finely tuned to the visual sensations, and, on top of that, projective transformations in the visual field must correspond to projective transformations in the somatic field. As you move a cup around your visual field, the projective transformations in that field must, in some sense, correspond to projective transformations in the somatic field.

Very crucially, these fields have different dimensionalities. The visual field, to a first approximation, is two-dimensional — a flat surface. If you relax completely — in meditation, if you let go entirely in **jhāna** practice and get absorbed into infinite space, infinite consciousness, or universal consciousness *(thank you, Asher, for this t-shirt — a wonderful gift)* — your visual field actually becomes flattened. So embodiment, the feeling of being someone somewhere with depth to your visual field, requires some degree of somatic tension. The main thing I'm trying to communicate is that there's an extraordinarily deep connection between (a) the relationship between fields of different dimensionalities — two dimensions in the visual field, three in the somatic field — and their mutual projection or mapping, and (b) intelligence, conscious processing, and the embodiment of a multi-sensory world.

### Meditative cessations

The next insight, still part of the same point, is that the way these different fields talk to each other is by modulating the projection and the coupling kernel. If you want to dive very deep into this, I recommend our paper on **meditative cessations**.[^coupling-kernel] If you meditate a lot and go very deep into concentration, there are special moments where everything disappears (this also happens on high-dose 5-MeO-DMT). What we hypothesize is happening is that you're finding a projection from one of these fields onto another — and finding a coupling kernel in both fields — such that the waves in each interlock perfectly and look the same.

We have some beautiful simulations here: you can rotate a three-dimensional lattice representing the resonant modes of a three-dimensional system, like the somatic field, while also looking at a distance function that is two-dimensional, representing the geometry of the visual field. We show that there are particular projections — for example, looking at the cube from one of its corners until it looks like a hexagon — and particular combinations of coupling kernels in both the 3D and 2D spaces where, all of a sudden, it's impossible to tell whether the system is two- or three-dimensional. That, we think, is a very high-valence, very blissful state of consciousness that lacks prediction errors, and therefore has no information content: nothing is happening, in a sense, because everything is perfectly in sync. It's very difficult to find those states, because there's a combinatorial explosion of possible projections from one field onto another, and on top of that a combinatorial explosion of possible combinations of coupling kernels. So a cessation would be a really special configuration — the right coupling kernels and the right projection, such that the waves look the same in both.

I think that was an important insight I had last year, and we formally wrote it up. It keeps explaining a lot of things — even reactivations on 5-MeO-DMT, which might involve priming the system to enter configurations where there's the right projection and the right coupling kernel, so that the waves look the same across different fields.

This is mostly analyzing the visual and tactile systems, but of course there's also the auditory system, and other fields as well. If you go by Shinzen Young's carving of the state space of consciousness — qualia fields — there are at least six: the inner and outer components of *see*, *hear*, and *feel*. *Feel*, for example, has an outer component (tactile sensations) and an inner component (emotions). There's also scent — *ooh, rose Tulsi, scent qualia* — and all of these correspond to different dimensionalities and geometries, and would have different coupling kernels. So a true cessation requires a lot of fine-tuning. It's not just the visual and tactile fields, although those may be some of the principal components, some of the things you absolutely need to get right — the rest might be more the details.

## 4. Topological solution to the boundary problem

The next point starts to connect with metaphysics and with big solutions to problems in philosophy of mind: the **topological solution to the boundary problem**.[^boundary-problem] I won't go very deep, because I've talked at length about it — lots of articles and videos, and a peer-reviewed paper with Chris Percy as a collaborator.

If you start with a field view of panpsychism, where the universe is a gigantic field of consciousness, you have the boundary problem: if we're all one, all this gigantic field of consciousness, how come you are there and I'm here — different moments of experience, different perspectives? Aren't we all the same field? The solution is that the field is effectively something like a vector field, with arrows at every point. (There are many formulations and many ways of arriving at field topology; this is a relatively simple one that aligns with the electromagnetic field.) There are things you can do to the field that change its topology — in particular, with electric oscillations you can create vortices in the magnetic field. Vortices are just the simplest example of how to create a change in field topology; there are many other ways, and I'm not married to any particular one. I just think there's going to be some field-topology property. But vortices are a great entryway: this is falsifiable and maybe even physically plausible.

The core idea is like what happens on the Sun. You have huge flux tubes — plasma tubes — so the Sun isn't just a sphere; it has these protuberances. It's remarkable that when a solar flare, or what's called a coronal mass ejection, happens — when the Sun spews millions of tons of hot plasma into outer space — that is the result of a *macroscopic topological transformation*. This is significant, because most people think topology only matters at the microscopic level — atoms and molecules. But field topology has macroscopic, top-down effects at the scale of a coronal mass ejection, which is much larger than the planet Earth. Massive things. The way it happens: the flux tubes trap plasma, but when they touch, the magnetic field lines exchange partners, and that releases the trapped plasma — plasma that was **topologically confined** by the shape of the field.

That's a good example of the sort of thing I suspect is happening in the brain. Some deep part of it — perhaps the thalamus and the lateral geniculate nucleus together, maybe the thalamocortical loop, something deep and fundamental to being awake or dreaming, to being conscious as a unified moment of experience — has macroscopic neural oscillations that cause electric oscillations, which result in looping structures in the electromagnetic field. These looping structures are not epiphenomenal. The moment you make them and trap energy, you have resonant modes inside them, and you have holistic behavior — something that behaves as more than the sum of its parts. (Especially, as I'll touch on at the end, if you add a path-integral formulation of what's going on with light and electrons moving around in these pockets. But even without that, you have this emergent, holistic, more-than-the-sum-of-its-parts behavior, with objective boundaries that are causally significant and have computational properties.) That, we think, is what solves the boundary problem: these looping structures create an inside and an outside with an objective, clear boundary. It would be wonderful if this is the case, because it solves so many philosophical problems at once.

## 5. Electromagnetic field topology and its modulation

The next point is electromagnetic field-topology modulation. If something like the coupling kernel is modulating the way the electric activity of these oscillators behaves — the relative phase between them as a function of distance — then, as you can see in some of the simulations, you can use a coupling kernel not only to determine how sprinkly or how uniform the traveling waves and chaotic processes are, but also to modulate the field topology.

If you apply a center-surround, lateral-inhibition-type coupling kernel, you may get lots of vortices, or nested structures of vortices, that interact with each other and minimize energy — which is what physics is always trying to do — in a very exotic topology. Whereas if you make all the oscillators go up and down at the same time, then, as a consequence, you get just one gigantic vortex. Or sometimes not even a vortex: especially if you have a clear boundary, like a circle, and a macroscopic coupling kernel that goes up and down all at once, that can raise the internal topology and make it entirely flat — which, subjectively, may correspond to complete ego dissolution, or complete emptiness. There's no manufacturing of internal boundaries, perhaps associated with a full mystical experience — a "plus four" on the Shulgin scale.

So, to recap: you can modulate the electromagnetic field topology with a coupling kernel. All the pieces of the puzzle are starting to assemble. And importantly, the way a given kernel modulates the field topology depends on the geometry of the network — so there will be characteristic ways in which oscillations in the electromagnetic field tune into field topologies, which will differ between the somatosensory cortex and the visual field, even using the same kernel, all depending on the particular network geometry of these subsystems. And where it becomes really non-trivial and fascinating is how they couple to each other — how they project and map onto each other.

What we want to find out — and really write research on this year — is whether we can square the low-level effects of divisive normalization with an understanding of the network geometry and topology of different subsystems, in such a way that we can predict and generate the same visual and tactile phenomenology that people with 5-MeO-DMT experience actually report. This would be amazing, because it's the marriage of third-person neuroscience with taking phenomenology seriously in the first person — finally, one way of building a bridge. Even if the bridge turns out to be completely wrong, I still think it's very worth it; it's a milestone, a new way of thinking about how to tackle this problem. Even if we're wrong, I consider it an accomplishment — though it's next-level if we can actually make it work.

## 7. Coupling kernels and field topology

The next point is a brief observation: if the coupling kernel is tuning into a specific field topology, then the field topology will also have top-down effects. You're sculpting the field topology, and it sculpts back.

Think of a river. The laminar flow is, so to speak, the smooth flow of the electromagnetic field. If you put a bunch of rocks in the river and get foam and turbulence, you're affecting the topology of how the field lines — the laminar flow of the water — interact with each other, and that has top-down causal effects. If you keep the flow perfectly laminar and parallel, the river behaves a certain way, carving particular paths. If it's full of rocks and turbulent, the paths it generates are different — maybe it branches out more. (I'm not a geophysicist, but I think that's the sort of thing you observe: add turbulence, and the top-down effects on the river change.) And it feeds forward: the turbulence shapes how the river gets carved in the future, so you may discover more rocks that maintain the turbulence — or it may be self-defeating, with turbulence generating laminar flow, which generates turbulence, and so on.

So I strongly suspect that by modulating the field topology with a kernel, you then modulate the kernel with the field topology. This connects with the next level of what we call **neural annealing**, which we now also call **neural field annealing**. When you do a lot of jhāna meditation — very concentrated, blissful states — you're tuning not only into a macroscopic oscillatory dynamic but into a *field* dynamic, a wave function: ways for the field to evolve over time. And that has top-down effects. So kernels can be used to tune into field topologies that then affect the kernels themselves, because of things like Hebbian learning — neurons that fire together wire together. The more you play with a particular kernel, the more you create a kernel-consistent topology, which in turn reinforces that kernel. That might explain things like reactivation effects.

## 8. DMT vs 5-MeO-DMT effects

The next point is a deeper dive into DMT and 5-MeO-DMT. The main thing I want to drive home is that the sense of entities — the sense that DMT is an ecosystem of consciousnesses — may well be a side effect of increased topological complexity.

Within the boundary, the topological pocket that you are, if you have a bunch of vortices, they effectively function as different observers in the field. Ultimately there's just one observer — and maybe open individualism is true in a certain sense, that we're all one, all the field — but the way sensations get rendered depends on the number of anchor points, or pivots, within the topology of the field. Every vortex is a particular perspective for the rest of the field. The field requires these perspectives, these anchor points, to be able to observe itself in a way that breaks the symmetry.

Topological simplification, on the other hand, may create the powerful feeling of a superposition of all possible paths at once. I suspect that classic Buddhist awakening and 5-MeO-DMT are much more related to topological simplification, where there isn't an anchor point — no sense of self you can use to observe and break the symmetry of the field. If you can't break the symmetry, you just have a superposition of all possible points of view at the same time.

## 9. Paths and experience: the path integral of perspectives

This takes me to the last point: the **path integral**.

How does light find the shortest path? How does an electron find the shortest path — when you have a Tesla coil and put your finger close to it, how does it know to touch your finger? As Richard Feynman would put it, it's almost as if the electrons can smell their surroundings. It's very non-trivial and fascinating, and complex; I'll add a link in the description to a video that explains how this happens.[^path-integral-video] The parsimonious explanation is that electrons and light go through *every possible path at once*. It just so happens that most of them cancel each other out: for any given path you can find another path with equal amplitude that cancels it — except at the extremes, the shortest and longest paths (and saddle paths, which I won't get into). It's fascinating that light very often doesn't only go through the shortest path — it also goes through the *longest* path. You can observe this in a mirror room: if there's a longest path light can take, you'll see it take that path, which breaks a lot of intuitions.

A path integral, for those who haven't heard the term, means you're taking the integral over paths. An integral is like the area under a curve; here, instead of computing an area, you're computing the summation of all possible paths and their amplitudes.

I think this is also the way to explain one of the key causal roles of consciousness: in the current QRI model, a moment of experience is the superposition of all the allowed paths within a topological pocket. This is necessary to explain phenomenology. On LSD, for example, you may find yourself amid very complex fractal structures, and then you see them from a certain angle and everything collapses into a single point — meaning that, in the configuration space of neural activity, very complex fractal structures are right next to point-like states of consciousness. The only way I can make sense of this — especially taking phenomenal binding seriously, the fact that all these pieces of information are put together simultaneously — is that what it's like to be a particular person is the superposition of all possible paths within the pocket carved out by the oscillatory dynamics of, say, the electric field. Any symmetry-breaking within that pocket is essentially observed from every point within it.

There are a lot of configurations — especially fairly simple ones — where you get infinite lattices. Think about it: on the surface of a torus with one black spot somewhere, or two black spots (to make it clearer), if you look from a particular point you might say there's one black spot over there and another over there. But if what's actually happening is that you're the superposition of all possible points of view within the torus, you'll see an infinite grid — in some contexts, a lattice of points. Or think of what it's like to be a cube, viewed from the point of view of a path integral, over all possible perspectives within the cube. If the cube is perfectly symmetrical, it has only one corner, because you can't distinguish between the corners. As you approach a perfectly symmetrical cube, its multiple corners suddenly align and collapse into one. Likewise, on the surface of a torus, the moment you remove the imperfections — the tiny defects in the field — all of a sudden you just have nothingness, because where you used to have an infinite lattice of imperfections, of how it looks from every point of view at once, you remove that imperfection and *foom*, the whole thing collapses. Why? Because now every perspective looks the same.

Effectively, this might also be a way of arriving at something like the **Symmetry Theory of Valence** by Mike Johnson, in a rigorous, bottom-up way.[^stv] If the nature of the universe is this kind of maximum-entropy path-integral superposition, then achieving perfect symmetries entails a collapse of what it's like to be that thing, because every point of view within a perfectly symmetrical pocket looks exactly the same. So very advanced jhānas and peak 5-MeO-DMT experiences may be the result of simplifying the field topology to such an extent that all the perspectives within that pocket look exactly the same.

## Recap

That's the very big picture. Let me recap, because I know it's a lot.

- First, the **coupling kernel**: a way of controlling systems of coupled oscillators by modulating the coupling constant for oscillators at different distances. The coupling constant is, essentially: do you want to be in the same phase or the opposite phase — the same color or a different color?
- Then, using a coupling kernel, you can modulate whole-field **resonant modes**. That's the neural-to-global bridge, and you can show it rigorously — connecting, via something like divisive normalization, the low-level system architecture to global resonant modes. This might explain empirical data, like connectome-specific harmonic waves on psychedelics, where LSD produces higher amplitude in the high frequencies as well as more energy overall.
- Then, **geometric constraints on coupling effects**: the effect of a given coupling kernel is a function not only of the kernel but of the geometry and topology of the network it's affecting — a parsimonious way to account for the zoo of psychedelic effects.
- Then, **network-geometry interactions and projective intelligence**: a lot of conscious processing may be about how fields of different dimensionalities interact, couple, project, and map onto each other, with the coupling kernel modulated so prediction errors are minimized.
- Then, the **topological solution to the boundary problem**, connected to coupling kernels: modulating the kernels in particular geometries gives rise to global topological boundaries as well as local internal ones.
- This may account for the subjective differences between DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, and other psychedelics — maybe even ketamine. (I didn't touch on ketamine, but I suspect it's actually a *temporal* kernel. I won't go there — maybe in a future video.)
- And finally, to wrap up all these perspectives — literally — the **path integral of perspectives**. In *Principia Qualia*, Mike Johnson defined **qualia formalism**: the idea that there are mathematical objects that map onto moments of experience and their phenomenology.[^principia-qualia] It's a new hypothesis, by me and others at QRI, that the solution to qualia formalism is through path integrals: you have this field topology, and to interpret it as an experience, you superimpose all the possible paths within it. If you do that, you have enormous explanatory power, and you may finally be able to explain why substances that are so similar produce radically different, mystical-in-nature effects. Why does it feel like we're all one on 5-MeO-DMT? Why does it feel like an ecosystem of intelligences on DMT?

In summary: different substances produce changes in the low-level circuitry, which cause different coupling kernels, which tune into different global resonant modes, which modulate the field topology, which in turn gets processed through different path integrals — and then you get the phenomenology.

And the last thing I'll say is that the global effect — the change in topology — then feeds back causally. So the boundaries and experiences you have are, because of the path-integral nature, really *more than the sum of the parts*. It's not a metaphor. It's not like Conway's Game of Life or a cellular automaton, where you can squint and think of it as more than the sum of the parts even though the system is really still just accounted for by low-level interactions. No — when you're talking about path integrals, you truly have more than the sum of the parts, in a completely non-trivial way. And that, my friends, is why we are conscious: because we're providing non-trivial computational leverage on biological organisms for their survival. But hopefully we can break free from the replicator imperative and aim toward the values of consciousness. Team consciousness.
