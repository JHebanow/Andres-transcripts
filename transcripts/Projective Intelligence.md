# Projective Intelligence: How the Visual–Tactile Fields Use Projective Geometry for Signal Processing

Hello, hello, everybody — welcome back. Today we're going to talk about a fascinating paradigm for how to make sense of intelligence. It's a fairly new paradigm. It came out of some recent, very exciting research that the Qualia Research Institute conducted with a number of collaborators in Canada, which I'm going to talk a lot more about in the future. But one really awesome paradigm that came out of that work — and out of the last year of research as well — is **projective intelligence**.[^projective-intelligence]

## The qualia of the day: visual–tactile synesthetic representations

So let me get started with the qualia of the day, which is: synesthetic internal representations, especially of the visual/tactile domain. What is it like to have an internal experience, a representation, that is visual and tactile at the same time? Introspect on that. What is it like to have just pure vision? What is it like to have just pure tactile feeling? What is it like to have the confluence — the interface — between these two domains? I think this is a very rich question, and posed this way, I don't think we have all that much high-quality phenomenology on the topic.

I want to credit three collaborators for really pointing me in the direction of "hey, this is actually quite significant, and we can make some progress on it":

- **Roger Thisdell**, an advanced meditator who gives meditation classes and coaching;[^thisdell]
- **Asher Soryl**, an ethicist and phenomenologist;[^soryl]
- and **Cube Flipper**, a blogger and fantastic phenomenologist who is carrying the torch — effectively blending frameworks from Scott Alexander, the Qualia Research Institute, and Steven Lehar.[^cube-flipper]

These three collaborators all pointed out that, on a certain level, it is almost as if every visual sensation we have is subconsciously mapped onto somatic feelings, onto tactile sensations. And when this *doesn't* happen — that is the unusual circumstance. It is only very rarely, under very specialized circumstances, that you might experience pure vision. Roger Thisdell has talked about this in some of our research output, which I'll link in the description. But the extent to which this mapping is ubiquitous is something I don't think we realize.

Let me give you one example. It comes from Roger, actually — although I remember noticing it myself when I was a child. If I take this pen and bring it really close to your face — obviously you're watching this on a flat screen, so you'd have to imagine it in 3D. It doesn't really work if you do it to yourself, but if somebody else does it to you, you'll notice there are somatic sensations in the face: warpings of your facial structure, of your facial surface, of your sense of embodiment.

What's up with that? It's almost as if your body is getting you ready to react to whatever is about to happen. It is anticipating a probabilistic tactile sensation — your friend may not be that coordinated, and, well, this might end in disaster, to put it mildly. Probably not, if the person is sufficiently far away. But the point is that this sense of embodied risk is being actively represented in the tactile domain, even though the information is arriving from an entirely visual one.

## Working memory slots are visual and tactile

But this goes even deeper. When you reason about something, you are thinking in a somewhat visual way. This will depend from person to person — on which sense modality is dominant for them, on how they combine sense modalities, and on the degree to which they are deeply synesthetic or not. But in general, based on a lot of conversations with people about the phenomenology of their own thinking, I would argue that there is a sense in which you track your working memory slots — the contents of your working memory — in spatial regions, to some extent, that have visual reference but that are somatically tracked.

In other words: to keep several facts in mind, several features of a problem or a circumstance ("there was a car, there was a house, somebody was running, somebody was training for the Olympics") — to hold all of those in mind at the same time, you have to fragment yourself spatially and assign that information to different regions.

First of all, I would argue that this is, under the hood, a process of desynchronization: networks that were implementing the sense of continuity in the field become slightly desynchronized, and that allows the creation of internal boundaries — the ability to actually keep information separate. Working memory slots require some degree of internal fragmentation. But more specifically here, these working memory slots are visual and tactile in nature: somatically tracked, visually located. That confluence is computationally very relevant. So what's up with that?

## Concentration and the collapse of working memory slots

Something else I will point out is that if you go very deep into samādhi — concentration meditation — or, as it happens, take 5-MeO-DMT, which seems to produce a very similar, or at least highly related, phenomenon, the number of working memory slots you have will slowly collapse: from seven or so, to five, to three, to two. When you have only two working memory slots, that is the last moment at which you can really represent the sense of duality — a here and a there, a me and a you, a before and an after. And the final collapse of those two slots very much feels like two fragmentations of your visual-tactile domain syncing up with each other. They used to be slightly out of sync, and when they finally sync up, there is a confluence of somatic integrity, visual integrity, and the loss of working memory. They come together.

It doesn't mean that you lose all information, because in some sense you now have just one very large working memory slot. Depending on how deep you go into concentration, the qualities of the information contained in that slot will change: the deeper the concentration, the more symmetrical, the simpler, and the more abstract these representations have to be. The last things to go before collapsing completely might be things such as the feeling of space, or the feeling of consciousness, the witness, or even something like nothingness — the classic formless jhānas. That is what happens to the field when you are very, very, very concentrated and you don't have these internal partitions. The sense of the body is a whole, in a way.

## Vision as delayed, probabilistic touch

I'm going to suggest that every visual experience we have — save for some very strange exceptions — is in fact encoding, in the way it feels, a kind of probabilistic program for how interacting with whatever object you're seeing would give rise to tactile sensations. Even a cup, or a stuffed animal — this little lemur — carries a sense of anticipation of what it will feel like when you actually touch it. Or when I take a sip of water. In a sense, you can think of the visual sensations available to you as tactile sensations with a delay — and probabilistic ones at that: they may or may not happen. It is almost as if they encode a field of probability over future tactile events.

I think thinking about it in those terms is enormously generative, and potentially somewhat accurate, if not actually very accurate. Now, I'm not saying that visual qualia and tactile qualia are two sides of the same coin. No — they are quite distinguishable. But their interaction, I think, underlies so many, so many cognitive processes that it is very worth really thinking about.

## Different fields, different dimensionalities

One of the things that has evolved over the last year of thinking about this is that these different domains — the visual and the tactile — have different dimensionalities. And they have different projective properties. I think this is the case for any field of qualia in us. But visual and tactile is the most straightforward case, because they are the most obviously geometric. They are also very large: there is a lot of qualia in both of them for most people. So I think that interaction will end up being one of the principal components of whichever dimensionality interactions there are in a person's consciousness.

I want to point out a couple of things. I think the tactile domain is natively 3D. It is almost natively a volume — or, if you consider the energy body, a little cocoon around you, essentially an egg, in which there is a high density of tactile qualia representations.

The visual domain, on the other hand, you could argue is either 2D or 2.5D. I like to think of it as intrinsically 2.5D, because I think that has more explanatory power. But I think there is a case to be made that it is actually just 2D, and that the 2.5D-ness is synesthetic on an important level. Because once you introspect on the nature of depth perception, you'll notice that yes, it has something to do with integrating frames seen from slightly different perspectives — but notice also all the ways in which the sense of focusing, of tensing your eyes, of microsaccades, is so essential to setting up the depth. That seems like some kind of tactile operator applied to a flat screen. So there is a sense in which maybe the depth — the 2.5D-ness — actually comes from the tactile domain, or the tactile domain is at least an essential ingredient of it. But I can also see the case that no, you can have completely 2.5D visual experiences with no tactile qualia at all, in a pure-vision sense, as Roger Thisdell might describe.[^pure-perception] That's not that important, though. So for the time being, I'm just going to say the visual field is something like 2.5D or 2D, as the case may be, whereas the tactile field is 3D.

## Projection as a buggy interface

So what happens when you're having these synesthetic representations — when you're anticipating the softness you will experience when you actually pet the lemur? I think you are performing a series of projective transformations from the 3D tactile domain, squashing it into the 2.5D visual domain with a particular orientation: rotation, shear, maybe even reflection, or even heavier non-linear distortion, as may happen in some exotic states of consciousness. And then you are manipulating that representation in the visual field, in the 2.5D field, taking advantage of the affordances that the 2.5D field gives you for computational analysis — affordances that maybe aren't so straightforward or available in 3D.

At the same time, this also gives you all of the problems that two-dimensional or two-and-a-half-dimensional representations have. And especially when they're combined with 3D, there are a lot of bugs that can happen. It's a buggy interface.

And as I hope will become clear in what follows, the particular ways in which the visual and tactile fields interact may create enduring patterns of distortion that break the symmetry — and that also implement a kind of force field.

If you follow Rob Burbea and his conception of craving and clinging, he always associates them with a sense of body tension: when you're craving something, subtly, subconsciously, some part of you is actually tensing around it, anticipating that if you were to get what you want, that tension would be released.[^burbea] He advocates for trying to release it on your own instead — doing loving-kindness, or mindfulness, or something like that. But notice that craving is not something abstract. And various other kinds of bugs, I suppose — intrusive thoughts, for instance — also have a visual-tactile component. If you were to dissociate the two, you wouldn't have that type of phenomenology, or at least not the unpleasant quality of it.

## Why project at all? Projection as signal processing

But there are also amazing computational benefits to actually doing these interfaces. So let me explain.

Suppose you have a very complex pattern in 3D — a fractal structure, say, or a galaxy-like structure, with lots of dots and lots of connections and lots of dynamics. How do you make sense of it? Think even of the work of a data scientist. If you have an *n*-dimensional scatter plot with a lot of structure in it, how do you make sense of it? The first thing a lot of people do is principal component analysis — dimensionality reduction. "Okay, there's a lot in here. Let's just look at the main phenomenon, the main axes of variance. Let's re-project it in such a way that I capture some information about this higher-dimensional representation in the lower-dimensional representation, in a way that is not only degrading but also informative."

In other words, you take the fact that you are degrading information and turn it to your advantage: you degrade it in such a way that you are actually *processing* information. You're doing signal processing, effectively — filtering out some information, but, by doing it carefully, amplifying and making more visible some of the information that is there.

So I would argue that one of the key things we do is projective transformations on tactile sensations.

Now, shape rotation sounds like the cornerstone of all of this, if I say "projective transformations" — but it's really just a part of the mix. It's one of the ingredients. And I think overemphasizing shape rotation really misses out on a lot of the other projective effects that happen. There are reflections. There are glides. There are rotational transformations that get compounded with glides. (A **glide**, by the way, is when you reflect and translate at the same time, which causes an odd effect — "where is this coming from?" Clearly not from a standard mirror; only a very weird mirror would actually do that.) But you can do exactly this in terms of mapping tactile sensations to the visual domain.

So you're taking this very complex field of sensations and projecting it in order to amplify things like principal components. But what else can you amplify? You can amplify inherent symmetries that only exist along some of the dimensions of that higher-dimensional structure.

Here's one example. Imagine a bunch of people, or animals, who are in fact arranged in lines, but whose spacing is not regular. If you look at them from most angles, they will look randomly scattered — especially if they're getting in each other's way and there are occlusions. But then you turn around, and boom: clear lines, clear spaces.

That's an example where, by rotating — by doing a projective transformation on a very complex structure — you can discern that there is actually a lot of redundancy in it. You can compress it quite a bit. You can make lower-dimensional representations of it, because along some projections it is quite redundant. Hopefully this makes sense.

## Abstraction and reification

Now, we have to combine this with models of perception like those of Steven Lehar: **abstraction** and **reification**. What he says, essentially, is that whenever you see something with edges, there is a self-organizing principle he calls the **reverse grassfire algorithm**,[^grassfire] where you take the whole edge of the object, send a wave out from it, and see where it collides. If it collides in a very orderly way — I won't even say symmetrical; call it synchronized, or coherent — that indicates that there are symmetries along the boundary. So in some sense you can abstract the symmetries of the object in terms of how the waves collide at the center of all those boundaries.

Reification would then be where, let's say, some of the boundaries are missing, but there are still enough of them that you get that convergence of waves. And the brain, being a non-linear system, might amplify this with a non-linearity and send the waves back out, until you get an attractor where they bounce back and forth and reinforce whatever has that rhythm. As a consequence, you are able to complete the missing edges.

So what Steven Lehar says is that something like the Kanizsa illusion — where you have three Pac-Man shapes looking at each other and you see an illusory contour, an illusory boundary — is the confluence of abstraction and reification, with the reification being the actual showing-up of the boundary.[^kanizsa]

So you're projecting these very complex, high-dimensional objects — or, in the case of tactile sensations, maybe only 3D ones — and you're getting this clear, coherent interference of waves. If that happens, it indicates that there are symmetries in there; and more than that, through the process of reification, you may actually highlight those symmetries almost as if with a marker. "Oh, there's something that is almost a triangle there" — or almost a square, or a pentagon, or a cube.

And the moment you actually reify it — reify it in your projection, in this two-dimensional surface — then there's also a little bit of projective magic, which is that we clearly have the algorithms to rotate these things not only in the tactile domain but also in the visual domain.

So you start having all of these different reifications — of the gestalts present, the actual patterns, the redundancies, the compressions of the input — and then you can rotate them, and you can keep track of that information visually and synesthetically, so that as you rotate it, you are populating your representation of the higher-dimensional object with the symmetries of its lower-dimensional representations. And if you do it from every side, in some sense you are reconstructing pretty much all of the information, all of the symmetries, all of the redundancy that there is in that higher-dimensional space, simply by collecting the symmetries in its projections.

And I think that this is absolutely essential to how we cognize, very, very broadly. Whenever somebody is thinking — as long as they are a visual-tactile thinker, which I think most people are — they are going to be imagining the way in which they would synesthetically react to different circumstances, creating these probability maps of tactile sensations, and then rotating them and re-projecting them and squeezing them in order to see things in them.

Even, for example, planning how to score a goal, or how to throw the ball in basketball: that planning might involve some kind of pathfinding through this projective space, and then keeping track of the various projections that give you the information necessary to encode a particular move — the projections from which the view is very clear. All of those are projective transformations that involve abstraction and reification, and they're synesthetic. That is why I think this is so relevant and important. It's very ubiquitous.

## Meditative cessation

Now I want to conclude — and this is a much, much deeper topic, but I want to deliberate in relatively small bits, by my standards — on how this relates to something like meditative cessation: the complete disappearance of everything for a split second, in very, very deep, advanced states of concentration.

This is something we actually wrote a paper about. It's currently a preprint, which we submitted for an essay contest on pure consciousness. My collaborators Chris Percy and Bijan Fakhri and I worked on that together, asking: how can we apply some of these paradigms to make sense of something like meditative cessation?[^cessation-paper]

I'm going to say the following. I think Roger Thisdell is probably right that pleasure has something to do with the release of tension — that positive valence, in its energetic form, has something to do with the release of tension in a symmetrical way. And maybe, if there is no tension at all, then all you can have is this passive positive valence, which is the absence of a problem. And maybe, if you trust the Jains, that is intrinsically blissful in a certain sense — but it also carries no information.

So what I want to say is that when the vibrations in the tactile domain don't match the waves in the visual domain, those mismatches are precisely what determines the information content — at the very least of your visual-tactile domain, but more broadly: mismatches between the behavior of waves, between the behavior of things that have distances in different fields of qualia, encode information about your world simulation. And craving, effectively, would be one of these mismatches.

This is quite fascinating, because we could potentially reconstruct the phenomenology of something like the predictive coding or free energy paradigms just in terms of these projective mismatches. Why? Because the visual field is expecting your body to be doing something, and your tactile domain is in a different state. And it's not that computationally we don't *like* those mismatches — it's that their embodied manifestation is literally dissonant. There are literally waves in the visual field that are trying to sync up with waves in the tactile field, and because there is a mismatch, that mismatch is going to cause stress, which is a risk factor for strain. And that feels bad.

What these meditation masters will tell you is that cessation has just zero negative valence. Shinzen Young might say something like: relative to nirvana, everything hurts. A controversial statement — but I think there's a sense in which it's true. There's a sense in which I don't think it's true, but there's a sense in which it absolutely is true: in that all qualia is a kind of disturbance in the field, one that seeks some kind of resolution on a certain level. So pure cessation, the complete disappearance of everything, is the absence of any negativity at all. Quite obviously, in a sense.

How does that happen? Well, within the projective intelligence paradigm, two things must be true.

**First, the projection needs to be a very specific one.** Reasoning from first principles, it will have to have a lot of symmetries shared. In other words, you want to project the field in such a way that distances in the field correspond, as much as possible, to distances in its projection. Now, this is not enough, because in fact it is impossible to do this for every notion of distance at the same time. It *is* possible, however, if the number of vibrations that exist in both fields is restricted.

And here's a key difference between, let's say, complete unconsciousness and something like cessation. In cessation, I think, there actually *is* activity. There is coherent activity in the sense doors, in the various fields of qualia. There are things happening. But because of the way in which they are synchronized and aligned together, it doesn't feel like anything — which is quite different from there just being nothing, no activity at all, which also feels like nothing.

So the two criteria are: the projection has to be right — it has to be a very specific kind of projection, and ideally a very simple one. **And second, the waves that are present — the resonant modes present in both of those fields — must be such that the waves look the same in the original domain as in the projected domain.**

And we made simulations of this: visualizing the three-dimensional resonant modes of a system of coupled oscillators, then visualizing its projection, then visualizing the coupling of the resonant modes of the projection, and then looking for projections — looking, effectively, for combinations of resonant modes that make the waves look exactly the same.[^cessation-sims] And we showed this to something like four people who are really advanced meditators, and there was a lot of resonance with it. The response was something like: "Okay, this probably works — but it would have to be for *all* fields."

So there's a way in which this can happen for the tactile and visual fields; but when it also happens for the auditory, olfactory, gustatory, and cognitive fields — the mind sense, in the Buddhist ontology — when all of those have these relationships, the right projection and the right resonant modes, such that the waves look identical in all of those fields, then there is no information. There's nothing for you to report, because there are no discrepancies. And there are no prediction errors. There's no problem. Everything disappears. There's no frame of reference from which to notice a difference.

## Fabrication, DMT, and multiple projections

So this is just very exciting. Maybe the last thing that I will mention — and this is in the interest of sparking a little bit of your curiosity — is what happens with much more extreme fabrication practices, like fire kasiṇa, or, in the case of psychedelics, something more like DMT as opposed to 5-MeO-DMT.

5-MeO-DMT deconstructs; it collapses your working memory slots. DMT — and constructive, fabricating meditation techniques — will fragment you more. I've described DMT as an ecosystem, creating competing clusters of coherence.[^dmt-ecosystem] I think that's quite literal — but they are also competing clusters of visual-tactile synesthesia.

And here's the most mind-blowing thing of all, honestly: in some states of consciousness, you may actually have multiple mappings from the same tactile region to different regions of your visual field. People talk about being bilocated — being in two places at once. There's the esoteric interpretation: this is how you astral travel, or how you can teleport, or something like that. But no — just in terms of your world simulation, bilocation would make a lot of sense. It's a possible failure mode of these projective operations. Very buggy.

If there's a lot of energy, if the garbage collector is broken, so to speak, you may accumulate a number of projections. And there's the tracer effect too — remember, on psychedelics everything lasts longer. So you move your hand around and you see copies of your hand, and each of those frames, each of those copies, may in a sense carry that synesthetic mapping.

So some really fascinating, crazy mathematics comes out of this. Because if you have two tactile domains being projected onto the same two-dimensional field — guess what happens? The waves in the visual field will allow those two tactile representations to talk to each other. You could hallucinate shaking your own hand, so to speak. Even though your hands are separated, because you're taking your visual field as a shortcut, you may actually hallucinate touching your hands together — or touching your right hand with itself.

And this can get really non-trivial, very complex. If you have multiple of these representations, then on top of that, not only will there be shortcuts, but each of them will have an internal representation of the visual field reflected through this mapping.

## Indra's net and exotic geometries

I think these are the ingredients for **Indra's net**, where everything reflects everything else. I've talked about Indra's net from the point of view of abstraction and reification and the free energy principle,[^indras-net-fep] but I think this is another key ingredient: that it is this synesthetic mapping going haywire, when the branching factor of the projections is more than one, or something like that. We can probably formulate it in a fairly straightforward way along those lines, once we have the very, very crisp formalism. And Indra's net does tend to have very exotic dimensionalities.

Consider: if you're having multiple projections of your tactile domain in the visual field, each of them will feel like a different world. But they are different versions of the same world. In fact, they are the same world, just being projected in different ways.

And the thing, too, is that the layers of projections, the complexity of the projections, and the non-linearity of the projections all increase drastically on DMT. So something quite distorted and weird-looking in one region of the field, and something very, very different in another, may in fact both be projections of the same part of your body. And because they are so contorted and twisted along higher dimensions, the geodesics — the possible communication pathways between these projections — will be quite exotic. So you make tiny changes in this whole dynamic, and the full set of shortest paths from every region of your field to every other region changes completely.

And I would argue — I'm actually pretty sure of this — that with this sort of system you can essentially tune into hyperbolic topologies, even three-dimensional hyperbolic topologies: things like knot complements. Because you're twisting this effectively three-dimensional space, adding wormholes to it, and completely changing what the web of shortest possible paths is. And so light in that space is going to do all kinds of crazy, completely bonkers things: standing wave patterns, finding shortest paths and geodesics across these very, very intricate, fractaling three-dimensional spaces. And for the life of me, I can't imagine that not being computationally really significant. We just don't know how to use it effectively.

And that's about it for today. Thank you for tuning in, and I'll talk to you another time, on another topic. Infinite bliss, everybody — take care!
