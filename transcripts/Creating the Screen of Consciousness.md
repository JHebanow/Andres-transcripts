## Why the screen of consciousness is a hard problem

*(Anyway, we're already ten minutes into the video and we haven't talked about the screen of consciousness — but so it goes.)*

Serious ethical considerations aside, let's talk about the screen of consciousness.

The first thing to mention is that this is a very tough problem. Part of the reason it's tough — though definitely not the main reason — is that people don't even realize that it's a problem.

Here's the thing. A lot of people think of the unity of consciousness in terms of functional unity, functional organization. Someone might say: it's a mystery, or a puzzle, how the brain is able to put together tactile information and visual information in order to create a unified moment of experience that contains both. And then they might say, well, that's actually not a mystery, because you can train a multimodal model with artificial neural networks that has a tactile field and a visual field, with a network integrating both streams of information and an output that takes both feeds into account.

That is what I would call **functional integration**. And I would argue that artificial intelligence systems have really extraordinary functional integration, and that neuroscience has found a lot of hints for how the brain would be able to functionally integrate information. But that is not what the question is about. Nor is it what the phenomenal binding problem is about — and the phenomenal binding problem is a precondition for even understanding what I'm talking about when it comes to the screen of consciousness problem.

We're talking about something much more radical and ontological. I'm asking you — and not everybody is actually able to do this — to realize that in a moment of experience you have a unified visual field, a unified tactile field, and a unified multimodal experience that incorporates all of this information at once. This is more than just *behaving* as if the information were integrated. Only a hardcore behaviorist would believe that behaving as if information is integrated somehow makes that information actually integrated into a unitary moment of experience.

What we are really pursuing here is: what kind of ontology for reality would even be able to accommodate something that is unified, that contains many pieces of information at once, that has one subject all across that one experience, and that has causal effects such that the total is more than the sum of the parts? And I mean that in the most non-trivial way possible: a system where, if you were able to change any part of it, the behavior of the whole changes in some sense instantly — as opposed to being delayed while the information propagates.

The word "instant" here is hard to qualify, because the ground of meaning we are trying to point at is **phenomenal simultaneity**, which is itself a rabbit hole. Why? Because there is a sense of the passage of time *within* a moment of experience. The moment of experience — the information that is "simultaneously" presenting itself — also contains axes of subjective temporal change. This is what phenomenologists call the **specious present**:[^specious-present] the moment of experience is almost a four-dimensional hologram, with one of the dimensions being time, and with soft tracers in both directions. You have a little sense of what's going to happen in the future — anticipation — and you also have a sense of what happened in the past, but you don't identify with it; you keep it as a representation. Likewise for the future: you understand that it's an anticipation.

Now, on heavy doses of psychedelics, that arrow — which we call the **pseudo-time arrow**, because it isn't the physical arrow of time but the arrow of time within a moment of experience, which is representational and phenomenological — can loop around. You may end up having a very strange phenomenology we call **time loops**, where it feels like the future and the past wrap around and you're trapped in an eternally repeating mini-cycle, or pocket universe. An even more extreme manifestation of changes to the pseudo-time arrow involves the collapse of future and past into the present moment, something we call **moments of eternity**, which usually happen on pretty high doses of psychedelics — although they're more likely to happen on 5-MeO-DMT, and also during meditation.

## Attention, awareness, and the tracer

When I say the information is presented simultaneously, this is tricky, because people will say: not really — I'm darting my attention around, and I'm only really paying attention to one bit of information at a time.

There are two problems with that. First, the feeling of moving around and refocusing your attention is itself happening within phenomenal time. When you move your attention from one place to another, on arriving at the new place you still have a tracer effect of where you were before. Without that tracer, the new focus of your attention would have nothing to contextualize it. The very meaning of your experience unfolds through this superposition of the tracer of your immediate phenomenal past together with your present; there is a phenomenal integration between those two elements that gives rise to the current meaning.

Second, anything you pay attention to is contextualized not only by the immediate past but also by the surrounding present. This is why, at the Qualia Research Institute — and in mainstream neuroscience and cognitive science too — we distinguish between **attention** and **awareness**. Attention is the fairly sequential concentration of qualia resources to disambiguate stimuli, find connections, and bind together different pieces of information. Awareness is the context within which that happens. If attention existed without the awareness that gives it its context, it would be meaningless. Imagine your experience being "here's a one, here's a zero, here's a one, here's a zero," versus "here's a one within a sea of zeros that contextualizes it." Those are very different.

Moreover, exotic states of consciousness and meditation indicate that this sequential, laser-like or flashlight-like conception of attention is a special case of something more general. If you take LSD at a certain dosage, there's a fragmentation of attention where you may have two flashlights of attention coexisting simultaneously. And DMT in particular can fragment your experience into lots of different competing clusters of coherence, each with its own flashlight of attention. This is neither parallel nor sequential. It's something stranger — a superposition of parallelism and sequentiality.

So the question is not how you can create a system that functionally behaves as if it were able to integrate these pieces of information, because that's a solved problem. It's a different type of problem. The question is: how does the *experience* of it come into place? How does it arise?

If somebody believes that maybe it's an illusion — the eliminativist camp — I would say they're at a kind of level minus one, where they've denied the very problem they're trying to solve, or think that denying it is the answer. For the most part, talking with eliminativists is, in my experience, a huge waste of time. *(Sorry, sorry, sorry, guys.)* Usually there are introspective deficits involved, or a really extreme need for closure or social validation, or a fear of being perceived as gullible for believing in mysterious properties of consciousness.

But no, dude — be honest with yourself. Recognize that consciousness has qualia, has simultaneous attention and awareness, has these very strange properties like the simultaneous presentation of seriality and parallelism. And sure, if it's an illusion, you still have to explain how that illusion works. But I don't think it's an illusion. I think it's actually a hint about how reality works fundamentally, and about what kind of thing reality is.

## How do you get a point of view out of atoms and forces?

Getting to the object level: how do you get a point of view out of a universe that is, let's say, made of atoms and forces? Or out of a universe that is a cellular automaton, where you are essentially updating ones and zeros based on their immediate neighbors, as in Conway's Game of Life?

I'll mention that even in Conway's Game of Life, you do have a non-trivial, mysterious mechanism for binding, because each square is in some sense aware of all of the squares around it — that simultaneity. What's up with that? What is implementing that? That itself is not just ones and zeros interacting.

More than that, you have the problem that there is nowhere in such a universe where many pieces of information exist all at once with a clean boundary, in such a way that the contents inside that boundary behave as a unit in a non-trivial way. With a cellular automaton you can always decompose what is happening into the various squares — whether it's a glider, a little creature moving around, or a really complex computer that you implement in the automaton. Any segmentation you choose within that cellular automaton is your own choice. It's arbitrary. It's observer-dependent.

You could imagine people with different cognitive styles looking at a cellular automaton and drawing very different conclusions about what is going on and what kind of beings are being implemented there. If you have a very laser-like attentional system, you might say: yes, gliders are real, but anything larger isn't. If you're a very holistic, big-picture thinker, you might say: no, only the very complex recursive processes you see when you zoom way out in Conway's Game of Life have any gestalt properties, whereas at the level of a glider it's not even real, or not quite real yet. But it's observer-dependent.

And any of those partitions — any way you choose to segment out the squares within a cellular automaton — is epiphenomenal. It doesn't add anything to the computation. It doesn't change the computation. The computation continues to be just these tiny cells, these tiny local environments.

## A more general conception of computation

So the first thing I want to say is that my conception of computation is more general. It's not a Turing machine. It's not a standard cellular automaton. I'm talking about a conception of computation where the inputs, the intermediate steps, and the outputs are all allowed to be unified physical systems that have holistic properties — that behave as units, that are unitary in some sense, that contain many pieces of information all at once, and that you can probe in various ways.

For me, the general conception of computation would be a computer where the inputs can be soap bubbles, or quantum coherent objects, or dynamical systems. And the intermediate steps involve things like: I'm going to shine a laser on this object from this perspective, or I'm going to collapse it from that perspective; I'm going to have these two objects resonate with each other and then measure the Fourier decomposition of the vibration of the system as a whole. All of those are holistic computing.

Of course, if you think of the universe as a cellular automaton like Conway's Game of Life, all of that can still be simulated with a computer of the first, boring, Turing machine kind. However, in physics that is just not the case. In real physics you have all of these situations where, in order to understand the next step or the next configuration in a system, you need to take the entire system into account all at once. You can't segment it into subcomponents and compute those independently, because they all affect each other. There's an all-to-all relationship in those systems — in particular in quantum mechanics, where you have path integrals, where you take into account all of the possible paths and interactions a system could have, and then you have the superposition of all of those, and then a probability function comes out of that superposition.

That type of system is much more of the computational type that I am proposing: one where inputs, intermediate steps, and outputs can be unitary, ontologically unified, holistic objects that interact with each other and produce non-trivial computation along the way.

So that's one thing: generalized computing. But then, even when you have generalized computing, you still have the question of how you get the specific phenomenology that humans have. How is it that you can have this perspective on a visual field where it feels like there's a homunculus, or a camera, and you are a camera looking at a screen?

## An illusion is still something to explain

Here's the thing. It's very common in spiritual circles and psychedelic circles for people to rightly point out that the screen of consciousness is an illusion. They might say: I took 600 micrograms of acid and it dissolved, so I know it's an illusion — why care about it?

Similarly, along the path of awakening, especially when it comes to the dimension of increased sensory clarity — and 5-MeO-DMT in particular really goes there quite a bit — it's very common to go through a stage (Frank Yang calls it the screen of God[^screen-of-god]) where the screen of consciousness becomes even more crisp: it really feels like you're looking at a 4K, super high-definition screen from a specific point of view. It looks hyper-real. People get poetic in those states: oh gosh, I've cleaned the doors of perception.

But then they go further, and they might say: yes, but then I became centerless, and I broke down the sense of the observer, and now every sensation is exactly what arises. They might say something like: I used to believe that sensations were being perceived by a central observer, but now they just exist in whatever way they exist, without a perceiver, without a witness.

Well, yes. I actually do think the screen of consciousness is illusory in that sense. But I mean illusory not in the sense of *not real, and therefore not important to explain*. I mean it in the sense of it being a **special case** of possible unified moments of experience. And therefore it's something that any theory of consciousness also has to account for.

It's the same as with physics. You could say: liquid water is an illusion, because I heated it up and it turned into gas. Yes — that's an important insight, especially if you're talking to people who have only ever seen liquid water, and then you tell them it can also be ice, it can turn into something solid. But if you want a theory of physics that explains the whole thing, you also have to explain how the liquid water happens. That's part of what you have to explain. In fact, now you have to explain *more*.

The advantage, though, is that you're no longer stuck with an ontology in which water is an irreducible, impossible-to-break-down primary element of reality. You start to realize that water is an emergent property of something more fundamental. The screen of consciousness is like that: the sense of there being a screen, with you as a camera behind it, is something that the field of consciousness — quantum fields, the topology of electromagnetic fields, whatever ontology we want to use — can give rise to. And we have to explain that.

## The screen is a collapsing volume, not a flat plane

So first of all, this is very difficult. Let's think through how you could do it.

To have the experience of a screen — which is not just a two-dimensional flat space, but something more interesting — is really to have the rendering of a process in which a 3D volume collapses into a point. The screen of consciousness is something like this: you have the dome of experience, and it is slowly collapsing towards the observer. The observer is thereby able to notice the differences, the distances, that exist between itself and the various perceptions.

One important insight here is that the consistency of the experience can be pointed at by saying that there is a projective dynamic that gives rise to a consistent metric: you can locate places in this three-dimensional, or 2.5-dimensional, space, and you know intuitively how far they are from each other, and you understand that behavior. But how does this present itself not as an abstract metric, in which different objects have different distances to each other, but as an actual embodied geometric space? In philosophy of mind, this is called the spatiality of phenomenology problem. Integrated information theory has some ideas for how to tackle it.[^spatiality] But it's a real, real problem.

The real phenomenology, in a sense, is richer: you have to take into account the fact that we have two eyes to get the full richness of the depth map. It's more like two overlapping observer points that know their distance from each other and can triangulate how far different elements within experience are, by the difference in the projection onto those two points.

But it's full of artifacts. If you put your finger pretty close to your face, it becomes double; or if you focus on the finger, what's behind it becomes double. I would say this doubling is in some sense the building block of the sense of depth. The degree to which things double, or become fuzzy — which also has to do with the focusing distance of your eyes — provides the clues used to create this metric, this shared environment where everything knows how far it is from everything else.

## Psychedelics: one eye becomes many

What I will say is that DMT especially, but psychedelics in general, tend to fragment this. There's a sense in which the experience of high-dose LSD can be understood as a field of sensations that are now being perceived by many eyes rather than just one eye.

Intuitively, one way of elaborating on this: on psychedelics, you move your hand around and you get tracer effects. Well, on high doses of psychedelics it stops being just that sensations last longer, and becomes more that the entire world simulation overlaps with itself. So you have the entire world simulation, with its actual depth structure, overlapping with itself, and you have different perspectives on the same metric space all overlapping with each other. And because there's also noise, there are drifting effects that can turn into a complex web of new kinds of distances, which may effectively emulate the behavior of higher-dimensional spaces in some ways — which is a whole other topic, very important and also very related to the screen of consciousness, but even more non-trivial.

## Frames of reference, and who reads the frame

Here's a key argument for why this is so hard in a normal Euclidean universe with atoms and forces.

In physics — in special relativity and general relativity — the core proposition is that the laws of physics look the same from every inertial frame of reference. An inertial frame of reference is usually defined as a grid where you assume that every line, every segment, measures the same: a 1×1×1×1 lattice, where the last dimension is time, so there are clocks at each of the vertices. To measure distances, or the plane of simultaneity, you look at how long it takes for signals to arrive from a particular source at each of the lattice points within that frame of reference.

Now, here's the thing. If your cube is a 1×1×1×1 cube and you're using it to triangulate distances, that's all well and good. However, you need something that is able to integrate the information about when these signals arrive. And who is doing that? Another cube? The problem is that you eventually run into an infinite recursion problem. It's exactly the same as with people who complain about the Cartesian theater model of consciousness: okay, you have a homunculus inside that is perceiving the screen — but who is perceiving what the homunculus is perceiving? Is it another little guy inside the little guy? The same goes for frames of reference.

Conceptually, the way this ends up working mathematically and formally is that you swallow the frame of reference into each point. You equip a point with a metric, and you measure its curvature with parallel transport: you look at what happens if you move a vector around and come back to the same point, and how much it changes direction. You are essentially equipping every point with a lot of pieces of information. We're not talking about a mathematical point anymore, because a mathematical point has zero information.

But then the question is: where does that data structure exist? Reality wouldn't just be a collection of points related in a certain way, because every point also needs to carry the information about its immediate environment and have its metric tensor embedded within it — which is, again, something that has multiple pieces of information at once.

Again and again we have this issue of where you find these multiple pieces of information at once in standard models of reality. And as far as I can tell, in most conventional models that is swept under the rug, because you end up absorbing those costs — billing for them — at the point of interpretation. It's the theoretician, the person thinking about the theory, who is integrating that information. But the model itself doesn't have that information integrated, if that makes sense.

### The camera, the aperture, and the screen

To make it starker: imagine you have this 1×1×1 frame of reference, and you want to know where and when something happened. Let's say a star somewhere outside the lattice explodes. When the plasma reaches the different corners of the cube is what you can use to triangulate where and when that event is relative to this frame of reference.

But we have the problem: who is witnessing this frame of reference? Who is integrating these pieces of information in order to arrive at the conclusion that the star is over there, in that direction?

Well, assume that it's a camera. You have an aperture, and then you have a screen behind it. The information from each of the corners goes through that aperture and gets projected onto the screen of that camera — onto all of the sensors behind it. I think intuitively a lot of people actually think of the screen of consciousness as just this: we have some mechanism inside us that provides an aperture, and then we're measuring a screen behind it where the rays arrive, and when they arrive, and that is how we figure out a frame of reference through which to relate everything to everything.

There is a fundamental problem here, which is: how does the information in the camera get integrated? And who is integrating that information? And how do you get, again, this property of many pieces of information all at once that behave as a unit and have unitary phenomenal properties?

The argument for why this metaphor is misguided — or at least definitely very far from complete — is fairly simple. There are really two options.

**Option one: the information is aggregated in the aperture** — the tiny hole the light must pass through. The problem here is that if the aperture were a point, then, as we said, points have no information; they're zero-dimensional, and cannot integrate many pieces of information at once. So it has to be an entire circle, an actual area. And there's a deep physical reason why it has to be more than a point, too: the diffraction limit. If something is too small, there are wavelengths that can't go through it without getting distorted, so if you want to measure wavelengths of a certain size, you require an aperture of a certain size. So the aperture is something extended in space, and also extended in time.

And then you have the question: who is to say that *that* is a meaningful object, one that hangs together as a unified moment of experience containing all of that information? Why not half of the aperture? Or half of the aperture plus a chunk of something outside it? Again, this is like the cellular automaton scenario, where how you choose to partition the physical system depends on each observer and each observer's choices and predilections. So you're not going to get very far with the aperture as the mechanism of binding, the mechanism that lets you have a screen of consciousness.

**Option two: the screen.** But the screen has another problem. Sure, things arrive at different sensors on the screen — but how do they become combined afterwards? And if you say that somebody else is observing the screen, you have the same problem: now you need the aperture or the screen of *that* observer, and it becomes an infinite recursion problem again. In other words, for a camera to be the thing that allows you to have a screen of consciousness, it itself requires another camera to interpret the content of the camera, and then another camera to interpret the content of that camera. It's ultimately a non-starter.

I have no idea if this is landing for most of you, and I don't think I'm doing that good a job of explaining it — but I do think this is absolutely a very real problem. ## Topological segmentation

Now let me give you a sketch of how I do think about this.

First, I think there is a mechanism in the universe that allows for actual **topological segmentation** of regions of a field of consciousness, such that when you segment a region out, it actually has holistic properties.

I've used this metaphor a lot in the past, but let me remind you of it. Imagine the field of consciousness is a balloon. If you twist a balloon from two ends, you eventually get two balloons that share a pinch point. What has happened is a topological segmentation: you collapsed a whole perimeter into just one point. That's a dimensional collapse. It's a difference in degree that cashes out into a difference in kind — it used to be a sphere, and now it's two spheres connected by a point. It's a topologically different object.

So if you want to go from a point on one lobe to a point on the other lobe, you are forced to go through that bottleneck, that zero-dimensional bottleneck. There's a very non-trivial sense in which the information on one side of the original sphere gets lost when it goes through that point and arrives on the other side. In effect, even though there may be one object ontologically, informationally and causally there is an absolute segmentation.

![[balloon-topological-segmentation.svg|Twisting a balloon until two lobes share a single pinch point]]

A more comprehensive view of the field of consciousness would equip the field with vectors and curvature and a lot of other properties, and topological segmentation would be more like the topology of a **vector field**. There you have things such as cycles: if you follow the arrows of the vector field, there's a precise place where you're trapped in a circle, and if you go outside it you start to spiral out, and if you go inside it you start to spiral in. That's one of those cases where this whole region goes off towards infinity, and that whole region goes off towards infinity, but this one region stays the same and forms a closed circuit.

I'm going to suggest that a moment of experience is something like that: one of these closed circuits of reality. And that other parts of the universe diverge without closure, without satisfaction, so to speak.

## The brain as a non-linear optical computer: the cavity grid

I am going to suggest that the whole apparatus of the brain is something like a non-linear optical computer that takes these holistic objects and operates on them with each other. It does this in a number of ways; one of them is with **beam splitters**.

So you have a hologram — an almost three-dimensional, holistic superposition of waves — and what it's like to be that is the superposition of all possible paths within it. Again, that has the right kind of shape for the ontology: a superposition of oneness and of many pieces of information, both simultaneously. It's closed, but there's also internal richness and detail. And it's not just statically there; it's a dynamic system where all of the paths are simultaneously present. I think something of that shape is probably closer to what consciousness will turn out to be like.

The core building block, or core idea, is this. Picture a very large grid — let's start with a two-dimensional one — where each cell of the grid is a compartment, and the mirrors separating the compartments are **beam splitters**: semi-transparent, so some of the light bounces back and some of it goes through. On top of that, you have fiber optic wires that let you take images of these compartments, move them elsewhere, and operate on them further.

So think about it this way. You have these different compartments, and each of them has a barrier that is a beam splitter — part of the light goes through, part reflects. Think of it as a funhouse mirror, or an **Indra's net** type of system. What happens when you shine light into one of the compartments?

What will happen is that inside every one of these compartments there is going to be something like a superposition of what it is like to be inside all of the other compartments. And the central compartment might have more of a centeredness quality, where it is at the center of the hurricane: there's an infinitely repeating fractal around it, but this one is the center of the fractal. It does have a special place, and it's also special from a PageRank perspective — meaning that if you do a random walk in this system, the higher probability mass, or the largest light amplitude, will be in the center.[^random-walk] So that's the most likely one.

Now imagine that what it's like to be the light in that system is the superposition of all of the possible paths. When you're in one of the compartments, if that compartment were perfectly mirrored, what would happen is that all the paths would start to look the same. The standing wave pattern — the main mode within that compartment — is symmetrical: rotationally symmetrical if it's a sphere; or, if it's a hexagon, symmetrical in other ways, with reflection and rotation in six ways. And so all of those perspectives cancel out and merge with each other.

### What is it like to be a cube?

Let me give you a quick example. One of the classical thoughts I have is: what is it like to be a cube, from this path-integral perspective? Well, it's the superposition of what it's like to be at one corner, plus what it's like to be at the next corner, plus the next, and so on. And in practice this would be a strange object in which there is effectively just one corner — because the cube looks the same from each of those corners.[^cube-orbifold] So when there are many perspectives within an object from which the object looks the same to itself, all of those collapse into the same perspective.

The reason I started to think about this in this way is that on high-dose psychedelics you have this very strange property where you may be experiencing a really complex, intricate fractal with lots of mirror reflections — and then you look at it from a certain internal perspective, and the whole thing collapses into just one point.

Meaning: consciousness — or at least our type of consciousness — is not like a painting, where the distance between two images is measured by how many pixels you need to flip to get from one image to the other. Because here we have a case where a super complex fractal is right next to this point — this pointer experience.

So it's much more along the lines of the configuration space of two mirrors that are parallel to each other. If you're slightly off, you'll see lots of reflections, but they'll have a shape: something trumpet-like or tunnel-like, that becomes parabolic and diverges. But the moment you get it perfectly aligned — especially if the distance is zero — the whole thing collapses, and it actually just feels like an infinite space without any distortions.

![[two-mirror-collapse.svg|Two round mirrors: the tunnel of reflections runs straight when they are parallel and bends when they are tilted; seen from above, the reflections close up at 60 degrees and converge on a point as the mirrors approach]]

I'm going to suggest something like that: we have all of these different compartments and they're all reflecting each other, and what it's like to be you is a superposition of all of those compartments simultaneously. And when there's some kind of closure or symmetry, they all look the same, and so it feels like there's nothing — nothing happening.

So in this model, **cessation** happens when the internal content inside each of the compartments is identical, which can happen in a bunch of different ways with this type of optical system. And on the other hand, the states of consciousness that have a lot of information are generated by the *differences* in what it looks like to be each of those different compartments.

## The full picture: two sides, and a hierarchy of grids

The full version of what I'm applying is not just these layers. To begin with, you actually have two of these grids, and then you have tubes connecting the two sides to each other, which effectively produces stereoscopy between them. And then on top of that you have multiple layers of those, each with grids of different sizes. By the time you go to one of the very large ones, there are only a few compartments in it. And then there's a largest one, which is just one big cavity.

![[grid-hierarchy.svg|Two stacks of discs, from many small compartments to a single cavity, joined by tubes]]

So what I'm going to suggest is that what it's like to be you is, in a sense, what it's like to be the optical components — the microtubule resonances in each of the neurons — where the neurons are organized in this grid-like fashion, plus there are two hemispheres, plus it's hierarchical. I'm hypothesizing that when you superimpose the perspective, the shape, the standing wave pattern of all of those, it actually looks like a screen of consciousness.

Now, I still have to demonstrate this, and I plan on doing a bunch of simulations. But I think it's a very promising idea, and I think I can actually get the 2.5D nature of experience out of those principles.

## One and many

I think that's it for now. I also want to say that very few people actually realize the very strange property of consciousness that it's not just one and it's not many, but the superposition of one and many. And I think that reality ends up being made of building blocks that are like that, both internally and externally.

I see reality so differently than I used to. If we go back to when I was a kid, at first I thought there are souls, and there's matter and electrons — I almost had a dualist perspective. Then I became a materialist, and then an idealist, in that I thought it's all consciousness. I briefly even considered things like solipsism and other perspectives. But implicitly in all of those views I always had a set-theoretic understanding of the makeup of the universe.

It wasn't until I learned about path integrals in the context of *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* that I realized: hold on a second, reality is very strange. To see how an electron will move from one place to another, you need to compute the superposition of all of the possible paths, including how the electron may interact with its own other paths. What kind of object is that? And who is doing that computation? It's very, very mysterious.

But one property that stands out is that it's this strange superposition of oneness and multiplicity: reality fragments into all of its possibilities, every option at once, and then it collapses back through some kind of bottleneck where many of those paths add up to something meaningful, but most of them cancel each other out. I think that absolutely happens, and that it has explanatory power when it comes to exotic states of consciousness — and that something like the geometric-looking screen of consciousness that we're used to is really just a very special case of all of this.
