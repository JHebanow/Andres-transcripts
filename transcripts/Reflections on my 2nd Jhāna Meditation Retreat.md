# Reflections on my 2nd (Jhāna Meditation) Retreat: From Formless Love to Antivortex Defabrication

## The qualia of the day: kinesthetic feedback

Hello, hello everybody! Welcome back! The qualia of the day is a lasso string shooter, a little toy that spins a loop of string really fast. To me it symbolizes **feedback**: when the string spins really fast, it actually becomes kind of stationary, and if you move it around, it takes a while for the movement to propagate through the entire system — even though the string itself is moving faster than the waves in the string. It's a really fun gadget to play with. There are much better videos out there showing the tricks you can do with it; apparently cats really have a blast with it. The reason it is the qualia of the day is that it's an excellent visual-kinesthetic metaphor for a lot of the phenomenology of jhāna practice.

## My second jhāna retreat

Today we're going to talk about my second jhāna retreat: insights, openings, takeaways, learnings, suggestions, and recommendations for anybody out there who is interested in pursuing this. For the record, I'm not an expert on this by any means. I do think a lot — a lot — about consciousness, the nature of reality, physics, and philosophy. I also meditate, but I'm not an adept meditator. This is actually only my third retreat; I've been taking meditation more seriously for the last three years or so. I'll tell you a little bit about my daily practice: why I do it, why I enjoy it, and what I'm trying to get out of it.

A very brief recap. I made a video called *Buddhist Annealing*, with insights from the first two-week meditation retreat I did, which followed Shinzen Young's advice: cultivating equanimity and the seven factors of awakening. There's also a Qualia Computing article about it.[^buddhist-annealing] That was in 2021. It was very healing, but it was definitely not jhānic in any explicit or emergent way. Equanimity, as we will see, has many flavors, and I think the flavor of equanimity I was cultivating there was not a jhānic equanimity.

Then last November — so relatively recently — I did a two-week meditation retreat at home following Rob Burbea's *Practising the Jhānas* lecture series.[^practising-the-jhanas] The original retreat took place over the course of three weeks, so I compressed the lectures a little: I ended up listening to them at a higher rate than the people who actually did that retreat. That's fine. I was very motivated and very disciplined on that retreat. I timed it every day: I meditated on average eight hours a day, sometimes a little more, sometimes a little less.

This time it was only a one-week retreat: technically nine days, but in practice seven, because what I'm doing right now is the very end of those nine days, and on the first day I still had a couple of tasks to do. So in practice it was seven days completely focused on this jhāna retreat, with a little practice on the other days as well, but not as much. And I took it much more lightly. I think that's what felt right — what I sensed would actually give me the most openings. Rob Burbea talks about how, in a jhāna retreat, you need to let the day breathe, as it were. Counting the hours, I estimate that on a typical day of this retreat I meditated between four and six hours. This was much lighter. Still, I continue to be impressed with the difference it really makes to do this rigorously, as opposed to just meditating once in a while. Even a practice of 20 minutes a day can be very helpful and healing, but it's just not the same dose. The first retreat was the first time I experienced the first jhāna, and it was a powerful opening experience. It was really intense. So these are seriously intense altered states of consciousness. But unlike DMT, they are, by the very nature of what you're cultivating, extremely high-valence and very pure in their valence. As we will see, as you go up through the jhānas they become even more pure in their valence. But even the first jhāna is so much more pure than the typical body pleasure we are used to in everyday life, or even with other substances.

That said, I have never done IV heroin or methamphetamine, so I don't know if those feel equivalent to the first jhāna. I suspect they might be in the same ballpark. I would love to talk to people who have tried both. I would not recommend trying that just to get the comparison, because it might ruin your life! But it would be an interesting thing to know. Compared with the high of alcohol or THC, or weak euphoriants — not comparable. The first jhāna is a lot, a lot better than those.

That said, as I've explained before, I think it is very common for people to meditate, experience some **pīti** — the very pleasant body sensation that is an ingredient for getting to the first jhāna — and then assume that that was it, that that was the first jhāna. Sometimes they'll say, "Oh yeah, I experienced tons of pleasure in meditation, but then I realized it wasn't that big of a deal." In particular, the first one becomes more subtle — perhaps higher-valence, but less dramatic.

## How Rob Burbea teaches the jhānas

What I'm going to do now is give you some of the core ideas of how Rob Burbea teaches the jhānas, so that even if you don't listen to him you have something to go off of. I would still highly recommend that at some point you do something like this: spend a couple of weeks just listening to those lectures and practicing. It's life-changing, it's profound. I really recommend it if you can afford it — if you have the time, the possibility, and the motivation. But even through the lens of Andrés, or the lens of QRI, it makes sense to go through some of these ideas.

### Sensitivity

Right before the retreat I made a video about metta, loving-kindness,[^metta-video] in which I talked about **sensitivity, responsivity, and attunement**. Very quickly: one of the things Rob Burbea really encourages is to increase the sensitivity of your **energy body**. This is a phenomenological construct, or phenomenological reality. It's like the concept of attention: we don't need to make strong claims about its ontological status in order to use it. We can use the concept of attention, and likewise we can use the concept of the energy body.

The idea of a lot of these practices is to breathe into the energy body. You breathe in and out, and, for example, when you breathe in you imagine that you're energizing it. The energy body is not just your felt sense of the body; it's like a cocoon around you. When you get really absorbed into it, the physical body boundaries dissolve — or they're just not there; they may not even dissolve. The feeling of dissolution may not arise; you may just skip the boundaries, and all of a sudden you're in a spacious-awareness state with a lot of sensibility. Use that as the thermometer for what is going on. You say a loving-kindness phrase, for example, and then you notice how that affects the energy body; or you make an attentional movement and notice how that affects it. Training that feedback system sensitizes you, so that you have a large surface area of your phenomenological space to play with.

### Responsivity

The second one is **responsivity**. This was very, very powerful to learn, because it is not how meditation is taught in general. In general, meditation tends to be taught in a very formulaic way: "Do this, this, and this, and that will arise." In practice, the first step works, the second step doesn't really work, and the teacher just tells you, "Keep practicing; eventually it's going to open up." Rob Burbea has a very different approach here. He essentially suggests: if something is not working, try something else. Really think of it as fine-tuning a very complex system. You're shaking it this way and nothing happens — what if you shake it this way? What about this way? What if you turn it around? At the macro level this may be something like: "I've been practicing for an hour and I actually don't feel very good; the hindrances are attacking me. Maybe I should go for a walk." That's one possible response. At the micro level it's much more subtle: what micro-adjustments in posture, in the quality of attention, or in the way I'm spreading awareness will make my experience feel better in the moment? That's the direction for the jhānas.

You need a lot of creativity here, and there are a lot of things you can do. When you're trying to get the pīti to spread, for example, you can imagine a tube connecting the different parts of your body so that the pīti can move around freely. You can shake the pīti a little bit, or softly add a wave of attention around it. There's a whole set of attentional techniques. You can imagine that attention is a receiver as opposed to an emitter. The typical way we think of attention is that you're emitting waves and seeing what comes back; there's a very active component of sending energy and scanning. A different mode of attention is completely receptive: you're just there, trying to receive. Then you can play — it's a combinatorial explosion — with, say, focusing on your stomach and receiving sensations in your stomach, or receiving sensations in the stomach at the same time as you're emitting sensations from your nose. I'm just giving you some random possibilities; in reality there's an enormous set of them. Another example: if you have discomfort in your body, one of the things Rob Burbea suggests is to imagine that metta is being emitted from it. It's not a natural move to make. Usually you might want to send love to that part; here you imagine that the part that hurts is itself sending love. It's a different thing. It may not work, but it may work. So the answer is to try it — try it all — develop an intuition for what works in what context and in what state, and then do it and let it unfold.

### Attunement

The third big suggestion is **attunement**: you have to develop sensitivity to different frequencies. Here is where it sounds very woo, but no — again, this is a phenomenological reality: different sensations have different frequencies. This is also something we take very seriously at QRI. See Steven Lehar's *Harmonic Gestalt*, which you can find online and on YouTube as well.[^harmonic-gestalt] Essentially, larger phenomenological constructs vibrate at lower frequencies, and smaller phenomenological constructs vibrate at higher frequencies. The DMT space is higher-frequency than the LSD space. Pīti, body pleasure, is lower-frequency than sukha, happiness. All of that is absolutely true, and you can verify it for yourself as you develop this sensitivity for tuning. Even when you're tuning into a particular part of your body, you're actually sensitizing yourself to find what frequency to tune into. There's also a duality between vibratory spectrum and shape, so in some sense tuning into a shape is the same as tuning into a set of frequencies.

All of that is super helpful. I bet a master signal-processing engineer with lots of experience with 3D audio — and I mean not a fake one, not somebody who just studied electrical engineering, but somebody who actually plays with the thing on a daily basis and develops an intuition — would have a leg up in learning tricks for tuning into different components of their experience and giving rise to jhānas. That's something to consider. Attunement is a whole topic; it could be a very long video on its own. I just mention it because it's a key ingredient here.

### SASSIE

Rob Burbea also talks about an acronym, **SASSIE**:

- **S** is for *spread*. You want to spread the pīti, or the happiness, or the peacefulness, depending on which jhāna you're working on. Once you've fully spread it, you're done; that one has an end.
- **A** is for *absorption*, and there's no end to it. It's really a vector: how absorbed you can be. Even very advanced meditators will tell you there's no end to it. I suppose there's probably an end — maybe cessation, the unfabricated state, the ninth jhāna, is full absorption. I don't know. Maybe; probably. But absorption is a vector, and in general, in pretty much every state, you can be more absorbed: you can embody the pīti more and become it more and more. The powerful opening of the first jhāna on my first retreat, for example, came when I was really playing with the absorption metric, when I was able to really absorb into the pīti. That was not an ordinary thing; it's a very strange thing that happens when you can do it.
- **SS** is for *suffused* and *sustained*. You can sustain the state for longer. Absorption is instantaneous — how much you're actually into it right now — whereas sustaining is about for how long. You can play with that too; it's also a vector.
- **I** is for *intensity*. He actually doesn't emphasize intensity; he doesn't take it very seriously or consider it that important. I suspect intensity is quite important from the point of view of energy and annealing, so ratcheting up intensity is probably very significant for some purposes. But it's part of the acronym.
- **E** is for *enjoy*. The idea is that what you're trying to do is ultimately a very mundane thing: find a way of seeing and interacting with your phenomenal world that maximizes enjoyment. Very hedonistic!

Now, this is not hedonistic in the typical, traditional way of selfish pleasure-seeking, because it takes a lot of work and play and is demanding. It's not the same as going to a spa. Meditating is exhausting; it takes effort. On top of that, the states you're cultivating tend to be very selfless. If you're using loving-kindness, you're really trying to get rid of things such as greed, hatred, and delusion, and other qualities of experience that typically emerge when somebody is just a pleasure-seeking junkie, like a meth addict. This is a completely different dimension — in some ways the opposite dimension. But reminding yourself that, on a moment-to-moment basis, what you're doing is tuning into enjoyment is powerful. I think most people actually have some hang-ups around it. Either they feel judged, or they feel that others will judge them, or they have blockages, like traumatic memories or unpleasant sensations. Whenever joy comes up, they may fear it's just going to go away anyway, so why care about it? If you ask somebody in the street, "When you've felt very joyful, what did that actually feel like? Could you concentrate on it?", most people will have no idea what you're talking about. It's a very subtle thing, but a very real and powerful thing. Telling yourself that you're there for enjoyment, and for a really deep sense of fulfillment, causes a positive psychological change moment after moment. The more you do it, the more you learn to love and accept yourself, and to accept that you are worthy of enjoyment, just as everybody else is. That's wonderful and beautiful. So that's part of the ingredients.

### Opening the mode of attention

I'd also mention a little more about **modes of attention**. As you progress into more refined states of consciousness, the range of modes of attention really expands. We tend to have a very narrow mode of attention. I suspect there are subconscious processes narrowing it down; it's not that it has to be that way on its own, automatically, because of the laws of physics or something. One of the important practices is to open up your awareness, constantly, to the whole body, to the whole energy-body space. It will close constantly, and you open it up again and again and again. It's an unusual mode of attention, and apparently it takes a long while for it to stick. There are people who've done insight and jhāna practice for years, and that's how they experience the world all the time: they're always spreading their attention fully throughout their body, and they have this beautiful ease in the body and sense of embodiment. They're very in touch with their emotions, and open-hearted. That's one of the effects this has. But probably because of some subconscious processes and habitual patterns, it's not automatic at all.

### Wim Hof breathing and daily practice

The last thing I'll say in this category is that I also mixed in Wim Hof breathing exercises. I find them very helpful, and mildly psychedelic. Wim Hof breathing doesn't take you to Waiting Room levels of DMT or anything like that, but it might take you to something similar to a threshold-level DMT experience, which you can then use as a source of pīti. You decide to experience it as a very positive energy source: when you're retaining your breath, or breathing again, and you're getting all these vibrations in the body, you turn them in a jhānic way, towards pleasure, well-being, and a sense of wholesome harmony. That really helps. I did not have that tool on the first retreat, and to be honest I think it would have been helpful. I don't know why Rob Burbea doesn't talk about breathing exercises. Maybe they're bad for you — I honestly don't know. Maybe they kill brain cells! I suspect they actually just make you more resilient. There are some studies showing that people who do a lot of Wim Hof breathing are better able to handle stress — not only of the psychological kind; they're also better able to resist diseases like E. coli and things like that.[^wim-hof-study] I don't know how legitimate that research is, but the point is it's probably not going to kill you, and it might reduce your stress a little bit. For the purpose of jhāna practice, though, the main benefit is that it's a way to play with the **energy parameter**: moving along these psychedelic axes without taking any substance. I found it very helpful.

My typical daily meditation practice is actually 10 minutes of Wim Hof breathing, followed by 10 minutes of metta, followed by 10 minutes of compassion practice, often guided with music. I'll share some links, or make a playlist, for what kind of music and what kind of meditation to do.[^practice-links] That's a solid half hour of very engaged, very energized, and very pleasant meditation, and every day it actually does something. Again, a retreat is a much, much higher dose, and it takes you to completely, completely different places — "real territory", as Daniel Ingram might say.

## Qualia Mastery

Now I'm going to move on to my relationship with desire: what it is I'm trying to achieve here. It's ongoing, but it's something I annealed on the first jhāna retreat: the concept of **Qualia Mastery**. There is a culture around powers and meditation — think of *Avatar*, not the James Cameron movie but the cartoon — in which they are something that makes you really special, a decorator of the self. It's like you're getting an award. Interestingly, that mentality is very counterproductive for developing the jhānas. That's something wonderful about the jhānas: if somebody is actually accessing real territory there, chances are — unless they're sociopathic; there are probably some exceptions — they've had to develop a relationship with their self-image and with desire that is healthier and less self-obsessed. That really came to the fore. I do have a competitive mindset about a lot of things: I want to be the first one to solve a particular problem, or something like that. So of course: "Yes, I've got to develop the jhānas!" Subconscious processes like that got really attenuated on the first retreat. Instead, what I annealed to, which feels so much better, so much more wholesome and sustainable, is what I call Qualia Mastery.

Qualia Mastery has three elements:

1. **It's for the sake of all beings.** You're developing Qualia Mastery so that you can actually help everybody. I think understanding consciousness is going to be enormously relevant for the present, and especially for the future, where things are going to get really weird.
2. **To experience the mystery of consciousness directly**: to be a participant in this wonderful play of form, not satisfied with knowing things intellectually but also having first-hand experience of them. In Mary's room, you want to see the red; you don't want to learn about red in a neuroscience book. The same goes for the jhānas, or ideally for arbitrary exotic states of consciousness.
3. **The love of knowledge.** Here is where it's quite different from something like Zen practice: one of Qualia Mastery's three pillars is actually understanding. I'll get to some of those insights towards the end. In the video about my first retreat, I talked about the brain as a non-linear optical computer.[^first-retreat] That is my current best model; it's where QRI has been heading, and all of its paradigms start to fit together within that view. A lot of the things I experienced make much more sense when you think of the brain that way, as opposed to just a neural network, or a predictive processing system, or something narrower like that without implementation details. Anyway, we want to know the truth. We want to know how consciousness relates to physics, mathematics, chemistry, and algorithms, and we want to know it all. That is part of the drive.

But that would be a very unhealthy drive if it weren't balanced by, first, loving-kindness — really wanting to help all sentient beings — and second, having skin in the game: actually wanting to be a participant in it. Otherwise you're not really studying or understanding it deeply.

So that is Qualia Mastery. It's a lot of the impetus, the desire, that keeps me wanting to continue exploring these spaces. Of course I recommend you engage with Qualia Mastery, but you don't have to. Ultimately you'll come up with something that really resonates with the core of your being — that will probably take some meditation, to sensitize yourself to what you actually resonate with — and it might be a different formulation, a different conceptual framework.

## Mastery of the jhānas and the meta-jhāna

Moving on: Rob Burbea promotes the idea of **mastery of the jhānas**. Jhāna Mastery would be a subset of Qualia Mastery, but I think a very important component. He says there is enormous power in intention and in the conceptual framework. I love that about Rob Burbea, because so many meditation teachers say, "I don't care about the conceptual mind; bare attention, or pure consciousness, is the thing" — or the non-thing, but the non-thing that matters. Rob Burbea says no: psychological processes and a conceptual understanding are absolutely essential, so that you can contextualize what you're doing. If you're working on the sixth jhāna, the reason you're doing it is not just the sixth jhāna; there's an entire context that puts it in place. That gives you a lot of motivation and allows you so many more moves.

One of the key things I experienced — even more so than deepening into a particular frequency, a particular mode of vibration or jhānic quality; much more so — was integrating the whole conceptual framework of Qualia Mastery and Jhāna Mastery. In particular, there is a kind of consciousness that emerges once you've dipped into several of these frequencies, which is aware of the reality of all of those frequencies at once. It has a flavor of its own. It wouldn't exist — you wouldn't be able to get in touch with it — without a conceptual framework tying the various states of consciousness together. That might actually be one of the most valuable things. Having the consciousness of the possibility of the four formless jhānas, having been in all of them, is a gift. It's precious. It's something else, and it doesn't arise until you've contrasted and compared them and seen all of them, at least to some extent. It deepens over time as well. I probably have a very weak version of it, but I've already noticed some profound effects on perception: the ultimate is not just this one, nor that one; together they paint a picture, and I can savor the feeling of sensing the entire picture, and value that.

So that is a takeaway from this retreat, and I recommend you look into it: develop a sensibility for this **meta-jhāna** — not *metta* with a double t, but meta in the sense of above, beyond, or recursively on top of. The meta-jhāna would be the jhāna of the awareness of the eight jhānas, or something like that. It's a very, very special, very, very valuable, precious state of consciousness.

## Pleasure as a trail: the ant colony metaphor

The next item. I noticed that a lot of what allows you to make sense of these states of consciousness is what Rob Burbea calls — actually, I think this is the technical term — the **nimitta**. The primary nimitta is the primary thing you're paying attention to. In the first jhāna, the primary nimitta is pīti, bodily pleasure. In the second jhāna, the primary nimitta is sukha, happiness. In the seventh jhāna, the primary nimitta is the sense of nothingness, and so on. But then there are **secondary nimittas**, in the background, which are supportive. I will also explain how that relates to non-linear wave computing and non-linear optical computing; it makes sense from that framework too, and I don't know if it makes sense in any other framework.

Say the secondary nimitta is something like "I'm noticing the enjoyment; I'm noticing the peace." If you absorb yourself into nothingness, but in the background there is this sense of joy around it — "Wow, this is peaceful; this is different from every other sensation; this is restful" — that pleasure functions as a kind of glue that ties it together and leaves a trace. The metaphor that came to mind as I was noticing this process over and over again was that doing these meditation practices is like being a gigantic **ant colony**, where every moment of experience is one ant at a particular moment. Whenever an ant finds the nectar — the pot of honey: the seventh jhāna, the third jhāna, whatever it may be — it leaves a trace around it. Not only is that state itself very high-valence, which is knowable only in the state and not anywhere else, but you're also leaving a trace around it that tells the organism, "Hmm, there is positive valence around here." That slowly builds chains of paths, gradients of valence, with which you can tie and tether these states together. You're forming a meta-structure. Every highly positive state of consciousness is usually very pure and almost memoryless; it has extreme symmetry, so you can't really encode much information there. But its surroundings — slightly less pure, but close — also become suffused with positive valence that you *can* remember. And you do this over and over. The space is large, and that's why it takes so long — in this model, at least.

You're like an ant tracing all of these paths. Many times it gets completely lost: it's somewhere else, it's in Mordor, nowhere close to a jhāna. And what you learn about those paths is how to get out of them quickly; that's also part of what you're learning. Especially if you reward yourself: "Wait a second, I've been ruminating for the last 30 minutes without realizing I'm supposed to be meditating." If, instead of scolding yourself, you feel good about noticing it, that gives you a little trace, a positive valence gradient that will accelerate the process of getting out of it next time. So again: kindness and love to yourself — and consciousness, and emptiness, and all of that — because you don't want to get stuck in patterns of self-judgment. That's antithetical to the whole project. It's a similar process, but much clearer, around the purer, high-valence jhānic states: you're constructing a set of paths, and over time the mind develops grooves as you travel to the nectar more and more times, as it were. Probably an advanced meditator just has direct paths from all of the mettas; Jhāna Mastery is essentially that. Maybe it forms the sacred shape of the Kabbalah or something like that. That would be funny.

So that is the ant-search-algorithm metaphor that occurred to me.[^ant-colony] Maybe, if you take that algorithm into account, it would be an effective way of accelerating meditation with EEG and neurofeedback. I'll talk to Stephen Zerfas[^zerfas] and see what he thinks about this; he's working on something along those lines.

## Why nothingness opened up

Moving on: nothingness. Why did I have openings into the realm of nothingness? Here is an important component. Rob Burbea mentions in the jhāna retreat that if you've done a lot of insight practice, it's actually quite frequent for the fourth jhāna to open up to you even before the first, second, or third. Why? Because insight meditation — deconstructive practice — often gives rise to a lot of peacefulness, a lot of equanimity. ## Undoing knots: vortices and antivortices

An insight that came from this was that if you want to go very deep in that direction, you will need to undo a lot of energetic knots that have been constructed throughout your life. In Buddhist lingo these would probably be the saṃskāras, or conditioned existence — I'm not entirely sure. But I think that, at a very literal physical level, a lot of the information content of your experience is in the form of internal **topological knots**.

Here's the thing. Say you have a wave — a sound at a particular frequency. If it's coherent, just a single sinusoid at a particular phase, can you get rid of that sound? Yes: you can get rid of it by adding the inverse wave to it. If you have a more complex sound that is decomposable into a set of oscillatory, coherent frequencies — meaning their phases are not changing — you can still cancel it out by adding the inverse wave. When the phases of the frequencies change over time, it's more difficult, especially if there's no pattern to it. If you know in advance what the waveform is going to be, then yes, you can always cancel it out. If it's chaotic, noisy, then no: there's going to be a noise residue. At some point, the only thing you can really do to get rid of a particular noise is to add white noise to smooth it out and make it more regular in frequency space. The point I'm trying to get at is that simple patterns are easier to cancel out. So in order to access the realm of nothingness, you require a very high level of coherence and concentration, so that you actually have a very simple state of consciousness. Once you learn that simple wave, you can add its inverse wave and flatten it out.

That's a good metaphor, but it's not entirely the case. I think the reality is a little more interesting. Let me explain what I mean.

I think these are not just simple waves you need to cancel; what you need to cancel are topological structures. How do you do that? You can look up online a **vortex** versus an **antivortex**. Different shapes inherently have what's called a winding number. The hairy ball theorem, for example, states that if you try to comb a sphere covered in hair, there will always be at least one place where a hair is sticking out. If the sphere is full of vectors — a vector field — and you try to comb it, there will always be one vector sticking out. On a torus, that is not the case: you can actually comb a torus. That gives you what's called the winding number: how many vortices a particular space has.[^hairy-ball] In this context, the winding number of a space would be the minimum number of vortices it can have. In another context, if you can modify the field however you want and you're not constrained by initial conditions, you can make a plane with a lot of vortices. But if you fix the winding number and want to add another vortex, what you will need to add in addition is an antivortex.

The thumbnail of this video will hopefully be a picture that cleanly contrasts a vortex and an antivortex. A vortex is spinning. An antivortex is strange: it's like two streams that collide and then go their separate ways. It's the opposite of a vortex, and if you put the two together, they merge: there's a critical moment when you get a singularity, then they cancel each other out, and there's no more vortex and no more antivortex.

![[fig-vortex-antivortex.svg|A vortex (winding number +1) and an antivortex (−1); a vortex–antivortex pair approaches, merges, and cancels, leaving a uniform field]]

The whole **topological approach to the boundary problem** suggests that your consciousness right now is a topological pocket of the fields of physics, which would be a kind of vortex.[^boundary-problem] There are many different kinds of vortex — you need a specialized topological mathematical language to talk about all of them — and they have different antivortices as well. So I think that when you're meditating on the formless jhānas, a lot of what you're doing is this. To review, the formless jhānas are:

- **The fifth jhāna**, the first formless jhāna: the sphere of **infinite space**. You merge with pure space; you become space; spacious awareness is all there is.
- **The sixth jhāna**: **infinite consciousness**. You're just very, very aware that you're aware right now, that you're conscious right now — now, now — very wide awake, intense. It happens on LSD more or less commonly in a dirty way, as I'll explain in a second — not a pure way — but you can definitely get hints of it on LSD: "Wow, this is happening." In jhāna practice it's a very, very pure state.
- **The seventh jhāna**: **nothingness**. You're not landing on any construct, and the primary nimitta is the feeling of not landing on any construct.
- **The eighth jhāna** is a lot weirder: **neither perception nor non-perception**. I'm not sure I've experienced that in any way. Maybe in some states of consciousness, but not something I can access in jhāna practice.
- **The ninth jhāna** is the **unfabricated**, which might actually be the complete cancellation.

With the topological approach, what's happening is this. Something like the realm of nothingness is where you just become a torus, or something like that: you're now a smooth field, with no internal content. Maybe deeper still, neither perception nor non-perception is where you've already cancelled that out with another antivortex, and now you're not a torus — maybe you're a circle. The idea is that the full ninth jhāna would be where you find the final antivortex that undoes the primary topological separation that turned you into a pocket of the fields of physics. Then you're just gone. What there is, is just the fields of physics, without any boundaries anymore — at least not within that region.

An absolutely trippy way of thinking, but very helpful. Once this occurred to me, accessing those states became even easier. It's like jhāna practice informed by the antivortex model: you're scanning for what kind of sensation will undo a particular knot inside you. You're aware that there are anti-knots. If you got knotted in the way you are, clearly there was a sequence of steps that led you to it, so there's got to be a way to undo that knot. The question then is how to do it in the most efficient way possible: what is the shortest path towards the unmanifested, the absolute? I thought that was a very interesting, very helpful insight, or way of seeing this process.

### Nothingness as no-thing-ness

This takes me to nothingness as **no-thing-ness**. During those experiences it really felt that a lot of how we perceive involves an algorithm related to ray marching and to Leharian perception, from Steven Lehar. The world-sheet from the hyperbolic geometry of DMT experiences is also related to that,[^world-sheet] and so is manifold learning from machine learning. They're all different facets of the same process — well, not really, but they're very related. When you look at an object, you're reaching out with attention and touching it all around, and there's a moment at which — click — it becomes a thing. It gets thingified. That involves some kind of topological closure of the sheets of attention you're using to envelop it. I noticed that when I was close to the realm of nothingness, that would stop happening. I would notice the sheet of attention, but it just wouldn't close. That's a move I was learning to make: not to close it, just to let it be, and then it dissipates. There is something it feels like to be that, and I suspect it's very related to some kind of emptiness realization. I would have to talk to several Buddhist teachers to know exactly how it relates to emptiness realizations. But when you're not closing these sheets of attention and you're hanging out in these open spaces, it's quite wonderful; the valence is very sublime.

It also triggers a lot of compassion, because you don't see people in the normal way. I think we normally do quite a few layers of closures to construct a person, and as we construct the person, we construct their vibe and the ways in which they might be difficult or problematic; a lot of things get built up. When you're not constructing people that way, they're almost half-faded. They're these open sets that don't close, and they feel like part of the universal stream of qualia. All of a sudden you don't feel them as separate, independent, self-existing entities with characteristics and essences; you feel them as part of the flow of the universe, and it's all one flow. So it's very difficult to feel any emotion other than metta towards these unclosed structures — and towards yourself as well. The same thing happens with your future, present, and past self when you conceptualize it that way. If you do that a lot — and I plan to practice it more — it has an after-effect on perception: after coming out of that state, when you look at something it feels like a thing and not a thing at the same time, an in-between. I think that has positive effects. You feel less trapped in things in general, because you're not thingifying. I'm sure there's some loss of subtle high-valence characteristics that come with closure, so this is not a panacea, or just a plainly better state, but I suspect it would bring so much relief in a lot of contexts. Something to ponder.

## The jhānas and psychedelics

The last two things I'll mention are a comparison with psychedelics and other drugs, and how all of this relates to non-linear optical computing as a paradigm for making sense of states of consciousness — qualia computing more broadly.

### 2C-B and THC

First, comparisons with psychedelics. **2C-B** might give you a really good hint of pīti and hedonic tone: a strong, vibratory, electric, strobing pīti happens on 2C-B. I don't know of anybody who's done the experiment, but I wonder if 2C-B might accelerate the development of pīti a little — probably towards the beginning of a jhāna retreat. Except that 2C-B does have a bit of a comedown and an after-effect, so I'm not sure it would be that good an idea. But it might be, especially for somebody who doesn't experience pīti at all, who is very anhedonic. I wonder if 2C-B might help.

**THC**, I think, is related to the jhānas only very tangentially. It's very dissociating for a lot of people, and very conceptual for a lot of people; in particular, it causes a lot of feedback in the working-memory slots. So I'm not sure it's very compatible with the jhānas. But if you're the kind of person who experiences a pleasant body high from it, maybe it might help kickstart pīti, especially at very tiny doses. I'm pretty ambivalent about its utility.

### LSD and psilocybin

**LSD and psilocybin.** I believe there was one study,[^psilocybin-retreat] and I do know a couple of people who've taken psilocybin at the end of long retreats; they said it was really synergistic with the meditative state. It would be really interesting to interview them, or ask them to write a trip report about it. It makes sense to me that it would be synergistic, because both LSD and psilocybin are fairly clean energizers of frequencies. My current estimate is between 15 and 20 hertz of stroboscopic and replay effects, which you can read more about in the post on modeling psychedelic tracers with QRI's tracer tool.[^tracer-tool] You can measure these things — how many strobes per second you're getting on LSD. That also happens in the body sense: you get intensified feedback effects and annealing effects. At the peak of a good LSD state — or maybe LSD plus phenibut, or something that smooths out the vibrations — there's probably a very big, good opening for cultivating strong pīti and strong sukha. But it's very chaotic, so I suspect it would mostly be helpful for people fairly early on, as a way of jittering away from their current attractor state.

One note here on high-dose LSD — which I don't really recommend; I don't recommend psychedelics in general, they can be very, very unpleasant. It is actually very common for advanced meditators to say that LSD or psilocybin gave them a mystical experience, and that that's what led them to take something like Buddhism, meditation, or yoga seriously. I can see that. On high doses of LSD you do sometimes experience things that I suspect are essentially a really high-energy version of the sixth jhāna: becoming universal consciousness, rainbow consciousness. Sometimes you experience a huge source of consciousness with rainbows all around it, and as you become it, it feels like you're one with everything — you're the ultimate consciousness, super-consciousness. I think that territory is extremely related to the jhānas. The problem is that it's very chaotic, and there are very negative versions of it, and I don't know how to induce those states reliably. But if you're somebody who responds really well to LSD, I suspect it would probably synergize with a jhāna retreat. I don't know when it would be best to time it — at the beginning, at the end, in the middle. We would have to do a lot of research, hopefully in the future — ethical, legal, and all that — but it seems promising to me.

Before DMT, I'll mention that with all of these psychedelics there would still be **dirty jhānas**. Part of your consciousness may center on, say, the sixth jhāna, but there's a lot of other chaotic stuff and decorations going on, which may be really difficult to get rid of; the annealing process often doesn't get rid of all of them. Maybe with the right combination of drugs — I don't know. But in general these would be glimpses more than the real deal, as it were.

### DMT

**DMT**: higher-frequency, estimated at around 30 hertz, fairly neutral — so that's good, good energization — but lots of lateral inhibition. The problem with DMT is that all of the patterns you see tend to be very wavy, and it's very difficult to experience a very smooth space. Allegedly you can, but it takes a lot of practice, so I'm not sure it's a direct path in any way. As a consequence, yes, you can experience facets of the first four jhānas on DMT, but they're embedded in a matrix of a lot of other things. For example, I've heard a long-term meditator say that before achieving the jhānas, what he experienced on a DMT breakthrough was an alien coming out of a spaceship. The alien had all kinds of crazy energies, but that wasn't the point. The point was that the energy of the space inside the spaceship was filled with what he later realized was third-jhāna consciousness. I suspect this is a fabrication of your mind, but we can play with the idea that these are entities from another dimension whose spaceships are inherently always filled with third-jhāna peacefulness. That would be really cool — it would be really cool if that were a consciousness technology you could create. I don't know. The point is that on DMT, if you do experience jhānic factors, they will be embedded within a context that has a lot of other things. So I'm very skeptical that you can use DMT specifically to accelerate jhāna practice. I'm sure there are a lot of things for which DMT is very useful, though — especially for understanding consciousness and its computational role. There's a lot more I will say about that in the future.

### Ketamine

**Ketamine** is relevant insofar as it tends to produce these unknotting processes. I think high doses of ketamine are wild and can actually lead to something akin to the formless jhānas. Often there will be dirty mixtures of formless jhānas — say, a lot of spaciousness and a little nothingness, or a blob of slowed-down spatiotemporal qualia that feels like a blob of negentropy; maybe that's a mixture of the fifth and seventh jhānas. So there will be weird mixtures. The other problem, though, is that they're **off-key**. They're off-key because, at least according to QRI's models, what dissociatives do is slow down the speed of wave propagation in the nervous system. As a consequence you get a desynchronization from the predictive coding hierarchies, which leads to the resonant modes of a ketamine-infused nervous system — different resonant modes from those of your normal, everyday nervous system. So they can be helpful. A profound ketamine experience — a K-hole, so to speak, though I don't like that term; it trivializes it, and ketamine can be really profound — can give you a glimpse of the quality. But you should know that the precise vibratory frequencies of the jhānas of your natural state of consciousness will be different from the vibratory frequencies of the pseudo-jhānas on ketamine. I think that's what makes them limiting: you won't really be able to train them.

But there is this unknotting process. I think ketamine therapy — occasional use, at relatively high doses, in a proper setting — can be very therapeutic for helping people move on from things in life, because it can undo a lot of knots. It's a kind of psychic surgery, and they end up in a state of consciousness with a much lower winding number. Recall that the winding number of your state of consciousness is how many vortices — how many topological knots — it has. So a post-ketamine state of consciousness might have a much lower winding number, which in general is going to feel better. Not always: there might be some loss of positive valence if the knots were finely calibrated and consonant with each other. But if you're pursuing ketamine therapy, presumably that's not the case.

### 5-MeO-DMT

Finally, **5-MeO-DMT**. Now we're talking. 5-MeO-DMT probably gives rise to fairly clean formless-jhāna states of consciousness. Again, I suspect it slightly changes the speed of wave propagation, so these are not exactly the natural jhānas you would experience — but probably very, very close. And don't listen to me; listen to Shinzen Young. Shinzen Young is an 80-year-old Zen master, recognized by the community and by other Zen masters as fully awakened, all the way there.[^shinzen] Of all the substances he's tried — and he tried a bunch, especially back in the day — he more recently tried 5-MeO-DMT, and he says it gives you the same kind of ego death and communion with the unfabricated, the absolute, as something like a hardcore 100-day tantric practice of extreme asceticism, which he experienced in the past. Again, this is a very, very strong experience. Don't take it lightly. It's basically like giving birth: a very serious thing that you're doing to yourself. This is not going to the bar. This is not even going to France. This is a profound thing. The sort of protocol I would suggest — again, I'm not really recommending it; this is what I would suggest for a research project in this area — would be to slowly attune yourself to it over the course of a week or so. The problem is that it's very shocking to the organism, especially if you don't do it slowly and adjust over time. My current understanding is that if you just jump right in, first you will panic. The ego will panic. It really hurts the ego: "Oh my gosh — all these projects, all these attachments I have in life, the very substance of my existence, are revealed to be a dream of infinite consciousness." I actually think that's illusory — I think this lifetime is very significant, and I don't buy into that kind of dualism — but in the moment, that's what it profoundly feels like. It's shocking. So not only is your ego going to die, you're going to suffer all the way through; and on top of that, you won't even be able to remember it properly, because you haven't created the network of positive-valence tethers to it. All of the meditators I know who can access the formless jhānas say they're delightful, but an acquired taste. That is why I am very, very hopeful about 5-MeO-DMT as an aid to meditation — say, in a long meditation retreat, titrated. Maybe we can cut the amount of meditation you need in order to access the formless jhānas by a lot, maybe by 80 or 90%. But it has to be done properly. This is not child's play. This is serious — very serious stuff.

## The brain as a non-linear optical computer

Finally, non-linear optical computing — just a couple of reflections. I think a lot of what you can experience on these retreats makes no sense under current neuroscience paradigms — predictive coding a little bit, connectome-specific harmonic waves a little bit. The same goes for psychedelics. Especially high-dose DMT: if you can experience four-dimensional objects and hyperbolic geometry, that throws out the window the notion that all hallucinogens do is activate the resonant modes of the visual system. That's obviously not all they're doing, because that would only lead to Euclidean shapes — the hard-coded predictive-coding composition. That's not what happens: you get absolutely insane, multi-dimensional contraptions. At QRI we're working on formalizing this and proving that this is the case; we should investigate it very carefully. The point I'm making is that if you actually care about reality, if you take consciousness seriously, you will have to go beyond current neuroscience paradigms and beyond current philosophy of mind, look at the thing, and try to make sense of what is going on.

The only framework I have that can actually make sense of all of this is the idea that the brain is a **non-linear optical computer**. There's probably more to this — this is probably just scratching the surface — but consciousness is probably an eigenmode of the electromagnetic field in the brain that creates a topological pocket, such that it's mathematically equivalent to photons moving inside it and creating a standing-wave pattern. And because of the non-linear optical properties, when the energy level reaches a certain threshold, the waves stop going through each other and start bouncing off each other. On DMT at low doses, for example, you get two-dimensional resonant modes, like a wall becoming tessellated. At higher doses you get three-dimensional resonant modes: much higher energy reaches the non-linearities, so the waves are actually bouncing off each other. Then there's a whole process of energy dissipation, in which the symmetrical shapes are energy sinks; I've covered all of that extensively throughout my career at QRI over the last however-many years. At even higher doses — and here is where it actually challenges neuroscientific paradigms — you start experiencing hyperbolic structures with a proper hyperbolic metric, or higher-dimensional structures. That makes sense from the point of view of coupled oscillators in a non-linear optical system generating implicit higher dimensions. I don't know how it could make sense otherwise.

The point being that, in this model, **attention** is where the waves are concentrating, and **awareness** is what they bounce off of. Whenever you find a place in your consciousness to which you're attending, there's a corresponding oscillatory complement: the field of awareness that is paying attention to it. When you get into very concentrated states of consciousness, what's happening is that there's a non-linear bundle at the center — or it could be spread out; there are many configurations, and the center is one example — such that the periphery bounces off the center, and you get to a stable eigenmode. When the waves arrive at the center, they can be in focus or out of focus, in phase or out of phase, or you can have a more diffuse structure. This is very weird, but think of adding lasers into quartz until the waves start to bounce off each other: there are states of consciousness in which the distinction between awareness and attention is not quite clear. On top of that — and this is where it gets really bizarre, though I think it's consistent with physics — the entirety of the experience is what it feels like to be every point in that experience, together with the information every point in that experience has access to, at the same time, as in a superposition. Very weird, I know — very, very weird. But this is the only way I can explain the jhānas, the only way I can explain high-dose LSD. It's going to take years to formalize this properly, but I think this is the right direction.

The formless jhānic states of consciousness are states in which the field is homogeneous, and therefore symmetrical: every point looks the same, and the entire field looks the same from every point. Then the topology of the field determines whether the lines concentrate at a point. That might be, for example, the sixth jhāna, the realm of infinite consciousness: the entire field is observing itself; the lines concentrate at one point, and that point gathers information about the entire field. In the formless jhāna of nothingness, by contrast, the lines never concentrate; they're completely parallel, so there's no focal point — like a series of lenses moving light around in such a way that it never focuses, which would be a possible state of a non-linear optical system. And something like the unmanifested, the ninth jhāna, would be where the field is completely quiet: it has cancelled out all of its oscillations, and on top of that it's topologically open, or something like that.

Now, the brain also has a predictive-coding neural mesh, with its own hierarchical structure. But the whole idea here is that the field surrounds all of that neural network, and the consciousness you're having right now is the result of how the neural network has learned to tune different properties of the field. So when you're meditating, in a sense you're changing the neural weights and seeing how that affects the shape of the field. Over time you learn to control the field in new ways, so that you can access, for example, the fully unfocused state or the fully focused state. I think that has a lot of explanatory power.

I'll also add that the brain is a bit more complicated, because you have the amygdala, the prefrontal cortex, the cerebellum, the homunculi. But the main point here is that the amygdala may provide something like the **style sheet** for the entire field, adding the fine-level consonance and dissonance — especially dissonance and noise — vibratory components. Think of an old cathode-ray TV, in which a beam of electrons is moved around in a raster scan to paint a picture, and inside it there's a little component that uses magnets to control where the beam moves. I think of the amygdala as something like that: it controls the quality of attention. Say attention wants to move here. If you're in a very anxious, very antsy state of consciousness, the amygdala will function as something that vibrates in a slightly chaotic way, so whatever you pay attention to will be slightly modified in an unpleasant, low-valence way, and slowly you'll entrain that pattern onto the **HTML of experience** — onto the sensory doors, as it were. What you're doing with the jhānas is learning to control that, so that the quality of attention is always very soft, symmetrical, homogeneous, and regular. On top of that you also have the pleasure centers, which — and this comes from Mike Johnson's *Principia Qualia* — allow the whole brain to enter into resonance: a kind of shortcut that enables large-scale synchronization.[^pleasure-centers]

So there are a lot of components; it's a complicated system. Overall, though, there are these odd states of consciousness to explain: there's global binding, so you're simultaneously aware of all of these different components, and yet you're paying attention to only some of them; the shape of attention can be modified; if you add attention to itself in a certain way — convolve it on top of itself — sometimes it disappears; and if you add an inverse version of your consciousness, you become nothingness. All of these strange effects, to me, only make sense in light of something like a non-linear optical paradigm. That's why I put a lot of stock in it.

### Attention heads and synchronization

I think that's about it. Oh — one last thing about that paradigm. From the previous retreat, it became pretty clear to me that we actually have something like seven **attention heads**.[^first-retreat] The way you access, for example, the first jhāna is by synchronizing the sensory doors enough that they acquire the proper vibe. Pīti has a vibratory quality such that wherever the attention heads settle, they will be vibrating in exactly the same way. And when they're vibrating the same way, the attention heads collapse into just one. As a consequence, that gives rise to **ekaggata**, single-pointed attention. It's not actually a single point — it could be spread out spatially — but the idea is that there's one prominent thing. I think that's why there's a threshold effect: you need a threshold of pīti in order to cohere, all of a sudden, into the first jhāna.

A very simple metaphor: if you have 10 pendulum clocks on a wall and they start desynchronized, over time they share little vibrations through the wall and all get in sync. If you have 10,000 clocks on a wall, they will never synchronize. Their natural state is that you sometimes get pockets of synchrony, or traveling waves of synchrony, but they never synchronize all at once. Now, if you start adding connections between them, there will be two phase transitions. In the first, you get **competing clusters of coherence**. I think that's something like what happens on DMT: your brain becomes more interconnected than normal, but not enough to become fully coherent, so you get competing clusters. Every clock belongs to one cluster, but no single cluster contains all of them. If you add even more connections, there's another phase transition in which the whole system coheres into one big synchrony. I think that's what happens on 5-MeO-DMT, but it's also what's happening in the jhānas. I think the natural network topology of, say, the prefrontal cortex is such that its attractor state is competing clusters of coherence. Once you synchronize the sensory doors — the HTML of experience — enough, and the amygdala is quieted down or very steady, the attention heads can pivot off the coherence in the sensory doors to achieve a functionally higher degree of interconnectivity, and as a consequence they synchronize. Then you get synchronization of both the sensory doors and the attention heads. That, I think, is the jhānic state of consciousness.
