## The qualia of the day: Indra's Net

First, the **qualia of the day** is **Indra's net**.[^indras-net]

It is said in various Eastern philosophies that Indra, a very powerful god, has this net hanging from the ceiling in one of the palaces in the realms of the gods. Each of the nodes of the net is a multi-dimensional gem — a beautiful, beautiful gem — and the net is infinite. Completely infinite. What happens is that each of those gems contains a reflection, a tiny reflection, of every other gem. And then within the reflection of every other gem, you find an even tinier reflection of every other gem, and so on ad infinitum. Of course, if this were physically realized, it would hit a limit, because photons are quantized ultimately — so there's not going to be infinite resolution when you observe it and try to find the world in a grain of sand, so to speak. But it's a very interesting and powerful metaphor. It's used methodologically to talk about the interdependence of everything on everything else, in Buddhism and even Hinduism.

That's a powerful vision. Imagine that everything in the world actually stands in this inter-net relationship to everything else, and everything implies everything else. Even a piece of trash, or a piece of foam — *this was a random thing that came in a package of essential oils* — is interdependent with absolutely everything else. More than that, it's the final product of its backward light cone, which contains such a huge amount of history, all the way down to the cosmic background radiation. Everything is interdependent with everything else.

Now, that's a mind-blowing idea. But what's the use of it? Recently there's this fascinating Dharma teacher, **Rob Burbea** — I've been consuming a lot of his lectures. He has a book called *Seeing That Frees*.[^burbea] One of the things he talks about is how you can interpret insight practice in Buddhist meditation — as opposed to, say, concentration practice or morality practice — as a set of *ways of seeing* the world that reduce suffering, that diminish **dukkha**, the unpleasant qualities of experience. For example, being hyper-aware, or being in the moment, or perceiving things as empty of inherent existence. When you apply those ways of seeing even to unpleasant low-level body sensations, for a number of people in a number of circumstances, it can greatly reduce their suffering. It doesn't always work, and it's really interesting to ask why it works and under what circumstances. But as a general frame, I really like it. It's very pragmatic. These deep, quote-unquote esoteric views of the world — interdependence, dependent origination, emptiness, and all of that — are not necessarily useful because they're fundamentally true. Maybe, as the Buddha might say, that's ultimately unknowable. But internalizing those ways of seeing your experience lessens suffering. It lets you drop a lot of the baggage you carry around that causes internal dissonances, and it cleans your experience.

A powerful experience of Indra's net is similar — though it actually depends on how you interpret it, because there's also a **spiritual materialism** risk to it.[^spiritual-materialism] If you have a mind-blowing meditation or LSD state of everything reflecting everything else, and you think, "Oh my gosh, I'm enlightened, I'm one with everything," now maybe you have *even more* baggage, because you've formed a new self-conception out of it. But there's also the flip side: actually understanding the interdependence of everything with everything else can let you lessen the amount of fabrications you engage with — especially mindless fabrications — because you recognize their empty essence and their co-arising with everything else. In a sense, you can treat them by modifying the conditions that give rise to them. So Indra's net is not only a mind-blowing experience; it's also potentially very pragmatically useful, in that it might lessen your suffering to some extent.

Importantly, I also think it's very valuable to study as an experience in itself. Different experiences have different degrees of how much they are like Indra's net. At QRI we essentially assume indirect realism about perception.[^indirect-realism] So when you're having an experience of Indra's net, I don't think you're literally contacting the rest of the cosmos and seeing how everything reflects everything else. I think what's going on is that you're instantiating a state of consciousness that has this fascinating property: every part of that state of consciousness, in some tricky but non-trivial and very real sense, is actually reflecting every other part of the experience. And that is what I will explain with the free energy principle. Exciting, isn't it?

I should also remark that, because of the **symmetry theory of valence**, most Indra's-net experiences tend to be pretty fractal and highly detailed — but above all, very consonant and very symmetrical.[^stv] So they tend to be extremely blissful. A lot of people may experience Indra's net and be fascinated by it without recognizing where the valence comes from. They may think the valence comes from literally becoming embedded into the fabric of reality. But that's a case of the **tyranny of the intentional object**: reading too much into the intentional content of the experience.[^tyranny] Instead, what I'd advise you to do is pay attention to the way those patterns are consonant or dissonant with one another. The claim is that blissful Indra's-net experiences, for the most part, are hyper-harmonious — they minimize dissonance in a very, very deep way.

So that's the qualia of the day. Highly recommended. You can get it with meditation; it's very common on DMT, as we'll talk about; and it may be freeing in some deep sense that I think is very valuable.

### A one-true-ontologist idea

The free energy principle is one of those really high-level, cool ideas that, as you munch on it, you start to see everywhere. I'd compare it with a couple of other things in that category — very high-level interpretations of reality that make you reinterpret everything, that give you a completely new lens.

One of them is evolution. The idea that whenever you have reproduction with selection and variation, you have the ingredients for evolution. That's a fascinating, clever idea — synthesizing evolution down to just those three principles. And once you have them, you start seeing evolution everywhere: not only biological evolution, but all over the place — in Conway's Game of Life, in fundamental particles. But you can distill it even further and say that's maybe a special case of something deeper: the *survival of the stable*. Survival of the stable is a meta-principle from which you can derive the three principles of evolution. Sure, there are biological organisms — but if a rock is really stable, it will be around. What exists is what is good at being stable in some way. Something could be very variable, a dynamic system, but construed as the state space of the entire system, it could be a very stable construct. Biological organisms are like that: very dynamic, but carrying some stable metadata that is what is actually immortal. Our genes are immortal over the generations, even if the individual isn't. In that sense, survival of the stable is a meta-principle.

I'd also say Bayesian epistemology is one of these high-level concepts that lets you reinterpret so many things. Once you understand that the brain is receiving information from its environment in a time-series fashion — there's massive parallelism, but there's also time; we're embedded in time, so the information we get happens over time — then whenever you have that data structure and you're trying to minimize prediction errors, it follows from the math that you're going to be doing Bayesian inference. There's no way around it; it's a hard constraint. It doesn't matter whether the brain is made of neurons, or of honey and bees, or of the population of China. If it's making accurate predictions about time-series data structures, it is going to be making Bayesian inference. Now, Bayesian inference lives at the computational level. You still need to dig deeper into the algorithmic and implementation levels, as I've discussed elsewhere (see the digital sentience video for a deep discussion). The computational level doesn't tell you the full story, but it is a constraint — a boundary condition upon which everything else has to fit. Nervous systems do Bayesian inference, period. They have to; otherwise they wouldn't be able to make accurate predictions.

Some people think the free energy principle brings all of these high-level paradigms together into an even bigger, higher-level paradigm, which is crazy. At QRI we have a lot of respect in general for thinkers who are what Mike calls "one true ontologists" — who take one principle or one set of ideas and try to apply it to all of reality and see how far it goes. Maybe it doesn't apply to all of reality, but in the process of trying to apply it everywhere, you discover a lot, and you may be able to massively compress a lot of highly detailed information about reality into just one principle. The free energy principle may do that.

### What the principle says

Briefly stated — and I'll go into more detail — it says that any dynamic system actually capable of surviving, actually able to sustain itself, must be able to minimize the entropy it's receiving from its environment. So you have a little cat, and if it always goes toward the absolutely most chaotic environments possible, it's going to die — it's going to go to the oven, or wherever. *(Bad images, but the point stands.)* If you maximize chaos, that chaos is going to eat you; it's going to shake you apart. So systems that do survive, that are stable — as I was saying, this is the generator for the theory of evolution — are those able to minimize the amount of unexpected entropy the environment imposes on them.

From that, through a chain of inferences, you can arrive at the implication that dynamic systems that survive in the universe must, on some level, have an internal model or representation of their environment, such that they can anticipate what's going to happen to them given the input they receive, and thereby avoid states of very high entropy. It's kind of crazy, because this may apply to a lot of counterintuitive systems. One is the brain, where we intuitively understand that yes, we do have an inner model of the environment. But this may also apply to things like a whirlpool, or a tornado, or convection currents. Not that a convection current explicitly contains a picture of its environment inside it — but implicitly, it must be doing some level of computational processing that makes it maximally adapted to its environment.

There are various schools within the free energy principle literature about whether the representations are explicit or implicit, and maybe that depends on the particular system. Most people would generally agree that at least the brain is making explicit representations — we're not just convection currents; there's something more explicitly computational going on. But at the same time, the brain must be satisfying this principle: if it's going to survive, it has to be able to predict its environment.

### Variational free energy

Now, the actual term "free energy" is an information-theoretic construct, not a physical one — although, as I'll mention in a moment, there might be an isomorphism between the two in some circumstances. It bounds evidence for a model of data. Here's the thing: if you had complete information about the world and the inner latent states — the latent parameters that give rise to what's happening in the world — you could compute how surprising a particular set of inputs is. In that case we'd be talking about the *surprise-minimization principle* rather than the free energy principle. So why the free energy principle instead? And what is this free energy?

It's an information-theoretic construct that appears in machine learning and statistics. (In the *Deep Learning* textbook, for instance, the definition of variational free energy appears on page 625.[^deep-learning-book]) The point is that variational free energy provides an upper bound on how surprising the stimuli are, given your generative model of the world. I know these are a lot of words, and to do the topic justice would take many hours and a detailed presentation with definitions and all the math. But what you need to know right now is that variational free energy is an upper bound on the *true* surprise a particular stimulus generates — where "surprise" means the model says these are very unlikely events. This construct, the negative log-surprise, gives the total surprisal of the stimuli, and that is what you should be minimizing. You don't have access to it; it's intractable. So instead you minimize free energy, which is an upper bound that is tractable and computable, and which, in machine learning, is used routinely to optimize inferential learning in probabilistic graphical models. Apologies for all the jargon — but the takeaway is that this is a clever, tractable way of minimizing how surprising the stimuli from the environment are.

### The dark-room objection

Someone might object: if you're minimizing surprise, why not just go to a dark room? That's the quote-unquote dark-room interpretation. In a dark room everything is infinitely predictable — you know exactly what you're going to get — so in principle it should make you feel great, because you're minimizing prediction errors.

Well, not quite, because the model we have of the world is dynamic, and the things expected in that model are *sequences* of events. There's no local maximum where a homogeneous, perfectly symmetrical state is maximally expected — your model actually goes against it. You don't expect to wake up in the morning and see nothing and have no sensory input; that's an incredibly surprising state. So going into a dark room, becoming super thirsty, not eating, and so on drastically *increases* your surprise and your free energy. Minimizing free energy actually entails doing a lot of very simple, quotidian, everyday things that restrict the range of states you find yourself in — because you're trying to minimize the entropy of the density of states you can inhabit internally, which maximizes your chances of continuing to exist indefinitely. And if you suddenly found yourself in a dark room, on a low level you might think it's very unsurprising because it's constant — but at the same time, you'd be making the inference that you're probably in huge danger. So it doesn't minimize free energy after all.

### Active inference and the precision of priors

The other fascinating thing here is active inference. There are two ways to minimize free energy.

First, you can change your model. You're getting something very surprising; to minimize the free energy it generates, you can change your model so that what you're experiencing becomes less surprising. But this requires a balance between the *accuracy* and the *complexity* of the model, because you shouldn't overfit. There's only so far a free-energy-minimizing system will go in adjusting its model to the immediate information, because it's also taking into account all the previous information — which shouldn't be discarded if it's an embodied entity that's going to survive. New information could be garbage, so you can't update too heavily on it. This brings up the precision of the high-level priors of the model: very surprising things may not warrant deeply updating the model, precisely because you have so much past evidence that the input you're getting is likely spurious. As a corollary, you can spin up an additional model of the probability that data is noisy or faulty, and model under what circumstances data becomes faulty — and *that* becomes the way you minimize free energy, rather than instantly updating your model.

Second, you can act on the world so that later inputs become more expected. If you're close to a hot stove, or in a very chaotic bar where a bar fight is about to break out — a bunch of drunk people, very chaotic, generating a ton of free energy for you — one thing you can do to minimize it is start walking and get out of the bar. Outside, it's a far more predictable environment. So action can also be a method to reduce free energy.

In the end, what you get with this theory is a beautiful interpretation of dynamic systems that survive, including humans: in a balanced way, they modify their internal models *and* act on the world so that there are fewer prediction errors. And what we're doing right now is doing that implicitly, on some level.

### Markov blankets and predictive coding

Another thing to mention is the concept of the **Markov blanket**, which comes from probabilistic graphical models. (You should look it up if you're curious.) You can define a barrier between an inside and an outside: for a given set of nodes, you can look at all the nodes that, if you were to fix them, would lock in the information that the internal nodes can get from their environment. The Markov blanket is the outer shell of a particular set of nodes in a probabilistic graphical model, and in that sense it generates an inside and an outside. What the free energy principle entails is that whenever you have a Markov blanket modeling a dynamic system, the free energy principle will apply to that Markov blanket.

In something like Bayesian predictive-coding models, where you have a hierarchy of models going from very low-level sensory data up to very high-level notions of the self, there's a kind of quasi-concentric-shells configuration, where each layer tries to predict, anticipate, and modify the one right underneath it. The process of model-building and world-building — creating the world-simulation you inhabit — is what arises out of the tug-of-war between all these shells trying to predict each other. The end result is a hierarchical model that encodes information about the world in a more and more refined and abstract way as you go inward.

![[fig-predictive-coding-markov-shells.svg]]

Of course, there's a huge debate about whether a model like that actually fits what's going on in the brain. To some degree you can model the layers of the cortex hierarchically — V1, V2, and so on — but the brain is really interconnected; there are connections between layer one and layer five, and it's all very mixed up. So identifying the Markov blankets of the brain's neural networks is not as easy as just dividing into layers. There's more complication to it. And down the line, at QRI we actually believe that consciousness *is* a holistic field behavior — or rather, utilizes holistic field behavior, and that's the reason it was selected and recruited by natural selection for computation. So consciousness might be inherently extremely interconnected by its very nature. Within a moment of experience, there may not be an easy — or even conceivably possible — way of drawing Markov blankets. That might run deep against some of the core assumptions of the free energy principle. At the same time, I do think it's quite possible to make proper Markov blankets *within* your world-simulation, and that alone lets us use the free energy principle as a lens. We'll recognize it's an imperfect lens — because these are partly imperfect Markov blankets, with leakage of information between inside and outside — but it can still be insightful.

## Applying the principle to psychedelics: REBUS

So that's the free energy principle. Now let's apply it to psychedelics.

The first thing to mention is **REBUS** — Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics — a paper by Robin Carhart-Harris and Karl Friston.[^rebus] What they argue is that the net effect of psychedelics on your world-model is a *weakening of the precision of high-level priors*. Those very high-level priors — the innermost layers of the Markov blankets — become less strong. They don't impose on the other layers as much; they're reduced. As such, this allows bottom-up information to propagate across the hierarchy and give rise to bigger belief updates. That's a fascinating idea.

They justify it based on neurophysiology. In particular, they examine the hypothesis that deep pyramidal neurons encode these very high-level priors, and that the 5-HT2A receptor is a way in which these neurons can be sensitized. Because they can then receive stimulation from lower layers of the hierarchy, sensitizing them gives rise to more straightforward information propagation between the lower and higher layers. There's also speculation that the priors are implemented through patterns of synchrony in those top-layer neurons, and that this sensitization and bottom-up propagation gives rise to their desynchronization. So rather than a coherent story imparting a synchronized top-level prior, you get competing patterns of synchrony, all trying to make sense of the world and of each other — an anarchy. Robin Carhart-Harris talks about it as **the anarchic brain**: all these different clusters and components of the model giving rise to bottom-up updates on the belief network.

### An aside on Feyerabend

In terms of the aesthetics of science, this is pretty cool. There's a fascinating book by Paul Feyerabend — *(I don't know how to pronounce his name)* — called *Against Method*, which argues that the idyllic picture of the scientific method as a recipe you just follow doesn't work.[^feyerabend] A really good quip from that book is the idea that facts dictate theory as much as theory dictates facts — or, put the other way for stronger effect, theory dictates what facts *are* as much as facts dictate what theory is. This is why paradigms take such a long time to advance in science. Whenever a paradigm explains a lot of information — as good scientific paradigms do — and you bring up a new study that provides strong counter-evidence, the typical response is to reinterpret those facts so they fit the theory, rather than re-examine the theory, which is a much bigger enterprise. Plus there are vested interests in academia, which I won't get into. But basically: facts dictate theory as much as theory dictates facts.

With REBUS, the really cool thing is that you can imagine this happening in your own brain. You're interpreting the world through your narrow lenses, and maybe by the age of 30 those lenses are pretty crystallized. If they're bad models of the world, it might be good to revise them — to allow low-level prediction errors to actually propagate all the way upward so that you update your model. That's the ideal therapeutic use of psychedelics, given this reading: you let people get unstuck, preventing the default mode network — or whatever story we want to tell — from imposing its high-level priors with very high precision everywhere, and instead being open to updating those priors based on new information, even information you've stored inside. In the REBUS paper they also note it's not only prediction errors from the environment; it's also a sensitization to oscillations in the limbic system. Emotions you didn't want to face, things that were "off" about certain situations that you were in denial about because your high-level priors made them hard to perceive — all of a sudden they become readable. They propagate in the network, which presumably could have a lot of therapeutic benefits in many circumstances.

### The limits of REBUS, and SEBUS

REBUS is cool, and I think it explains a lot, but it's actually very narrow. It doesn't go into the truly insane stuff that happens on psychedelics — the hyperbolic-geometry-of-DMT-experiences type of stuff: **world-sheet** bending, 3D hyperbolic spaces, hyperbolic resonant modes, space groups appearing in your visual field. There's a lot that REBUS doesn't touch, because that's not what it's trying to explain. But maybe if we pay attention to those crazy aspects, we can still apply the free energy principle and get some good insights — which is what I'm about to do.

A couple of final things about REBUS. There's also a fun and great commentary paper by Adam Safron called **SEBUS** — Strengthened Beliefs Under Psychedelics.[^sebus] He makes the point that a lot of what happens on psychedelics is more toward the *strengthening* of beliefs than the weakening of them — a lot of the side effects of psychedelics involve believing weird stuff. So what's going on there? He suggests that yes, psychedelics do weaken (or sensitize) top-level priors to prediction errors, but they may also *strengthen* medium-level priors. That might give rise to particular hallucinations and expectations about the input: the DeepDream paradigm, where something that kind of looks like a dog suddenly congeals into a dog. That feels like you're imposing your high-level model onto the world. So the REBUS story doesn't make sense of everything. But it also doesn't seem very parsimonious to say "REBUS and *also* SEBUS." There are these competing forces, and the story is probably pretty complicated.

I'd also point people to Anil Seth's lab and the work of Michael Schartner — *(apologies if I'm mispronouncing his surname)*.[^seth-schartner] They're doing fascinating work creating biologically plausible neural-network architectures, disrupting them, and using the generative models that come from them. They get essentially a generalized and more biologically plausible version of DeepDream. That's great research. And the literature is still waiting for a grand unification that makes sense of REBUS, SEBUS, and these predictive-coding paradigms.

### Evidential adequacy

For a theory to be adequate, there's a criterion of evidential adequacy. You don't only need to explain the DeepDream effects and the belief updates you experience — you also need to explain machine elves. You need to explain tracer phenomenology, enhanced valence, the crazy harmony that happens in some of those states, the annealing effects, the dissolution of the self into the rest of the cosmos, resonance between people. There's a ton to explain.

It's very similar to how some theories of consciousness claim to have an answer to consciousness, and yet, if you read into them, you recognize they're not even addressing the real problem. Any scientific theory of consciousness must be able to explain:

- the *existence* of consciousness;
- the *causal properties* of consciousness — why was it recruited by natural selection?;
- the *binding problem* — how is it possible that we experience unified moments of experience, even though neurons are spatially distributed?; and
- the *full range of qualia patterns* — what is the periodic table of possible qualia values and patterns, and the way they can be interrelated? What patterns can be bound with one another?

You need to explain all of that. And as far as I can tell, REBUS definitely doesn't go that far. As for the free energy principle: if you have a certain kind of mind — and in particular if you're very dismissive of qualia — I think you can squint really hard and get a glimpse of the free energy principle as a theory of consciousness. I would claim, no: it's a theory of *dynamic systems*. It's not addressing consciousness directly. But given that a conscious system is a dynamic system, you'll see a lot of free-energy-principle effects in a conscious system. I hope that difference makes sense — it's very tricky. I'm not saying you can explain consciousness with the free energy principle. You can't; it's not a theory of consciousness. But because consciousness is a complex dynamic system, you'll find the free energy principle appearing in it all over the place. It's like flipping it 180 degrees, while still highly valuing the free energy principle. It's just very important to note that it's not a theory of consciousness.

## Three new applications of the free energy principle to consciousness

Let's get into the three new applications.

First of all, the free energy principle is a *computational* theory. It deals with probabilistic graphical models; it's something very natural in machine learning. It's not a theory of physics, although it's trying to connect those principles with physics. What we're doing at QRI is getting an *implementation-level* account of what the brain is doing, and based on that implementation, inferring what kinds of algorithms can exist. The computational-level account I was describing — the Bayesian brain — provides hard constraints on the computational side. We're exploring the implementation level, and they'll meet in the middle: given the implementation, what algorithms are possible and likely; and given the computational side, what algorithms are necessary. It's a kind of forward-chaining and back-chaining, and hopefully we get a good insight in the middle.

We model a moment of experience as a **resonance network**. Again, this is not a full theory of consciousness, as I explained, but it does address the binding problem, at least at a functional level. When you have a resonance network where each node is a metronome — and it could be a more-than-one-dimensional metronome, for example a square with various vibrational modes — resonance can be transitive in the sense of locking the system into a coherent state. But the frequencies of resonance don't need to be transitive between nodes. If node A is in resonance with B, and B with C, then A and C don't need to be in resonance with one another; they're only implicitly influencing each other through their influence on B. On their own, they're not intrinsically in resonance. So there's this fascinating non-transitivity of the frequencies of resonance. And what we're claiming is that what the brain is fundamentally doing is trying to minimize dissonance.

### 1. Dissonance-minimizing resonance networks

From that, I'd claim we can derive the free energy principle as an implication of this implementation. Again, it's not that the free energy principle is fundamentally explaining how consciousness works here; rather, this particular implementation naturally gives rise to a free-energy reading. It goes as follows.

In this system, when the layers are in dissonance with one another, that encodes a prediction error. Making an accurate prediction entails that one layer can synchronize with the components of the layer below. When that doesn't happen — when a layer propagates information that the further, higher layer is not predicting — that generates an out-of-phase interaction, and that is an instance of dissonance. That's one component.

Second, there may be an inherent *complexity cost*: very complex models tend to give rise to dissonance, because there are a lot of interrelationships. So in trying to minimize dissonance, you get a balance between model complexity and prediction errors. The weight on either could be programmable — by genetics, or by the particular details of the resonance network. Some of those configurations will be very adaptive, because they hit precisely that balance: minimizing the range of internal possible states (minimizing that entropy) while also minimizing the entropy coming from the environment (because they make correct predictions). And this happens recursively in each of the layers — each of the concentric shells of the Markov blanket, so to speak.

The fascinating thing is that we can interpret **neural annealing** — the paradigm we've talked about, which Mike Johnson has an article about — as a way of getting out of a *local minimum of dissonance*.[^neural-annealing] You're stuck in a given configuration; with neural annealing, you inject a ton of energy into the system, and in that elevated, higher-energy state the barrier for exploring different states goes down. It weakens the constraints, and as the system cools down it can settle into a much less dissonant configuration. As a consequence, that process might give rise to generally better predictions about the environment. Not always, though — you could anneal something that's just too simple. With the wrong dose in the wrong setting, you may anneal something too simple. For example, something like 5-MeO-DMT may generate such global hypercoherence that all the information gets lost. In that super-symmetrical state, you anneal to a model of the world where the expected thing is actually the dark room — this infinite void. The first time you experience the infinite void it's incredibly surprising, but the more you experience it, the more natural it feels — the more it feels like ground-based, basement-reality experience. You can read this as an annealing process updating your model of the world such that now, minimizing free energy requires you to take into account the existence of the void.

That's a cool lens. In the REBUS paradigm, by contrast, annealing — and they do talk about simulated annealing — has much more to do with things like message passing and belief propagation as algorithms in probabilistic graphical models: when you weaken the constraints there, you get an annealing effect, and as you strengthen them again, you may end up with a more accurate model.

In a recent talk at the Oxford Psychedelic Society, I hypothesized — and this also comes from some conversations with Enrique Bojorquez, a volunteer at QRI — that there might be a very deep duality here between what's happening in the resonance network and what's happening in the belief network.[^cognitive-dissonance] For example, when you experience information you don't like, it literally feels dissonant. Cognitive dissonance would be actual physical dissonance going on in the brain. We're actually interested at QRI in testing this, and we think it's feasible to publish a paper on "cognitive dissonance is physiological dissonance." Resisting information also has this embodied component — you're, in a sense, preventing the information from propagating. And practices of equanimity, where you learn to relax deeply and just embrace whatever happens — what you're doing there, because of the duality, is *allowing information to propagate*. You're not being in denial. So even patterns of muscular tension would have implications for how you're dealing with information in the belief network. This is a super rich, super rich connection.

### 2. Bayesian energy sinks and symmetry

The second application is a synthesis of Mike's **symmetry theory of homeostatic regulation** and the hyperbolic geometry of DMT experiences.[^sthr]

Mike hypothesizes that very symmetrical harmonics in the nervous system are signals of health, because they let you do a very quick diagnosis. Whenever there's symmetry breaking, that usually indicates damage. You may not be able to sequentially examine every part of your body and compare it with an inner template — that might be computationally intractable — but detecting deviations from symmetry might be very straightforward. It's like a Fourier decomposition: you look at how harmonious the Fourier analysis is, and that tells you how symmetrical the state is, how much it's deviating from homeostasis. It's a deep idea, and it's an algorithmic-level account that can be hooked up to the free energy principle.

In the hyperbolic geometry of DMT experiences, I talked about how DMT literally energizes your experience.[^hyperbolic-dmt] Here I'm talking about physical energy — with a panpsychist background assumption that fields of physics are fields of qualia, which you don't need to buy into for this to make sense; you could also read total energy as average neural activity, or intensity of electric potential. On DMT you have higher-energy experiences, and this manifests as very bright colors, or a very curved world-sheet (the depth map of your experience can become very curved), or super-intense high-energy resonance, or even a very loud sound. On DMT, the energy chaotically transitions from one modality to another. Sometimes it concentrates — there are reports of people saying the energy concentrated in their visual field and they experienced the most intense blue they could possibly experience. That's one high-energy configuration. But for the most part, the energy is distributed across many modalities.

I also talked about **Bayesian energy sinks**: what you *recognize* lowers the energy. You have these chaotic, crazy patterns — an excited world-sheet — and then you see the corner of a table, and that becomes a nucleation point that propagates the shape of the table and condenses it. There's a condensation of the excited world-sheet into a semantically meaningful shape. As a result, the things that actually survive in a DMT state are the highest-likelihood ones — given how crazy the state is, whatever could possibly make sense of it. That imports a lot: narrative and information from previous trips, what you've read online — Alan Watts, Terence McKenna — all of that becomes high-level priors that give rise to condensations of the world-sheet. This is largely why I think there's a ton of confirmation bias on DMT: what you've learned and what you can recognize will condense as a world-sheet. But it's a fabrication, I'd claim — you're subliminally choosing it, because you could also condense something different. It's sometimes difficult to resist, because these interpretations have a momentum of their own; they gather energy and are very difficult to stop once they get started. Which is why I think it's very important to develop a very, very, very good aesthetic — a very high-level philosophical aesthetic — so that the things you recognize in the world-sheet are actually beautiful things, cooperate-cooperate equilibria between the sub-agents of your experience.

To synthesize Bayesian energy sinks with the symmetry theory of homeostatic regulation: very similar to a soap bubble, the *other* thing that condenses the energy of the world-sheet is symmetrical configurations. In a soap bubble, any configuration besides the perfectly spherical one has potential energy; that potential energy immediately translates into kinetic energy, making the bubble bounce, and because it's bouncing it interfaces with the surrounding gases and radiates its energy. A soap bubble radiates its energy until it reaches its local minimum of energy — which, in this case, is actually the global minimum for that shape: the perfectly symmetrical spherical configuration. Likewise, on DMT, the shapes you experience are local minima of energy, because they radiate energy to the environment. This has to do with holistic field behavior and the fact that the phenomenal objects in your experience literally radiate waves of energy. *(That's a topic for another time.)* I don't mean waves of energy from outside reaching at you — it's all in your world-model. Internally, in your own world-simulation, there are waves of energy that the objects radiate, and there's a lot of very deep material about that. On DMT, objects will do this; they radiate their energy, and so they condense into their lowest-energy configurations, which for the most part bring up elements of symmetry.

So the typical DMT experience is a hybrid of the two: it simultaneously has a lot of symmetries *and* recognizable semantic content — because that just happened to be the way physical energy was being minimized.

### 3. Indra's Net

I promised I'd explain Indra's net with the free energy principle on psychedelics and meditation, so that's what I'll conclude with. Bear with me.

Remember that in previous videos I've talked about how DMT can be modeled as *competing patterns of coherence*, and how this gives rise to an evolutionary process. Think of Reddit's **r/place** — that one-million-by-one-million canvas where people could place a pixel every ten minutes or so. Obviously you needed to collaborate with others to paint the whole thing, and the dynamics that emerged were really cool. Some people were in the low-entropy "let's make everything black" or "let's make everything white" camp; others were "no, let's make a Pikachu" or "let's make the flag of Australia." The point is that there's a competition of patterns, and the stronger a pattern gets, the more it can signal, the more visible it becomes, and the more people it can attract. So there's a rich-get-richer dynamic, and therefore a power-law distribution of how popular different patterns are.

The same happens on DMT. One frame for analyzing DMT is that these competing patterns of coherence fill up your experience with the contents of your subconscious — what your subconscious can recognize and finds emotionally salient — while also mixing in the affordances of highly symmetrical configurations as energy-minimizing configurations. So the crazy stuff you see on DMT is basically the balance between the forces of the competing clusters of coherence.

Sometimes a cluster of coherence can win — it can be really savvy at minimizing its free energy. When it does, you may have an alien-contact experience where the whole trip is you standing right next to a lady, or a mantis, and it's scolding you for something you did, or praising you, or telling you you're going to be the king of the universe. It's telling you a story that is very captivating. Being captivating, and being consistent with what you know, is one of the ways these quasi-entities — these emergent phenomena on DMT — get you. That's how they attract your attention: they build things that simplify your model of the world or make things make sense, and that is very alluring. There's a feeling of super intelligence. And yes, a DMT state is, in some sense, super intelligence — because it's finding, at an extremely fast pace, connections you weren't able to find before, and that could be computationally very meaningful.

At the same time, because of the chaotic nature of the state and the coherence across the layers of the hierarchy, there are a lot of *forced belief updates*. Unfortunately, when you experience these DMT beings, they force you to update your beliefs toward them being real, outside of your own existence. Once you understand everything I've talked about, you'll be able to notice this: the way it manifests is as literal dissonance. If you rebel against the updates a DMT machine elf is giving you, it will feel difficult — it will feel like resisting, and that resistance leads to dissonance. It's going to be unpleasant. Which is why I think it's so important, from the very beginning of the DMT experience, to always hold the uppermost, most elevated aesthetic you have, such that the evolutionary selection pressures for the patterns give rise to cooperate-cooperate equilibria. When that happens, it can give rise to Indra's net.

And what is Indra's net here? Okay, we're coming to a close — everything is munching together to get to this core insight I'm about to deliver. The competing clusters of coherence, in order to be good at surviving, must be able to model their environment. And they must be as simple as they can be, so that there aren't chaotic elements within them that would destabilize them and shake them apart. Now imagine that the entire field of experience is following these principles, following this gradient, and that the clusters are more or less similar and consonant with each other. When you have those conditions — and you can maximize their probability by taking DMT in a really good state of mind and holding a really good aesthetic in place — each of the competing clusters of coherence will generate a model of its environment, such that when it receives the waves of energy, it can match them with internal waves of energy simultaneously, reinforcing the specific pattern that it is.

And because fractality and self-similarity are also a way of minimizing complexity, what can literally happen is that you'll have these competing patterns of coherence, each of which literally contains a tiny reflection of every other one, scaled downward to the appropriate scale. And within each of them, you'll have a smaller one. It's not going to go infinitely — there's no space, and the frequencies are not infinite; the frequency has a maximum value, so the bands of the waves of energy as they radiate cannot be infinitely thin. So there isn't infinite complexity, but there can be several layers of mutual modeling. And because the simplest configuration is when all the competing clusters of coherence are identical, when you set up the conditions right, the gradient moves toward literally every cluster of coherence being a fractal reflection of every other cluster of coherence. That way they can predict each other perfectly, they don't lose their shape, they minimize energy, they make perfect predictions — a completely stable state. And it's high-valence, because it's all consonant. And that's Indra's net.
