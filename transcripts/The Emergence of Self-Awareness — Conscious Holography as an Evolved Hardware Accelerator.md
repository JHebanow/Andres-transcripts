## Wolfram and computational irreducibility

Wolfram is very interesting within that picture because his main conceptual contribution, I would argue — obviously he has contributed in many other ways, and there's a huge brand behind him and a lot of fascinating research — is this concept of **computational irreducibility**.[^irreducibility] From very simple rules, like in cellular automata, you sometimes get specific combinations of rules — Rule 30, for example — that give rise to endless complexity. And you cannot actually predict it. You have to compute it. You have to actually go through the process of seeing how the rules add up together and do as many steps as it needs, in order to see: okay, will this square be black or white? You actually have to run the computation. You cannot simplify it, for deep reasons.

And he hypothesizes that at the grandest of scales, the universe is like that. The universe is an irreducible computation, as it were, and so much complexity and so many asymmetries emerge out of that.

But then also biology. He was talking about how you should think of the sort of computations that biology does not as a well-crafted computer that has been designed in a top-down fashion, but really as a bunch of molecules and physical fields.

Anyway, Wolfram says that biology stumbles upon these computationally irreducible patterns, and then there's an intelligent process that figures out how to use them, to instantiate a broader self-replicating pattern that involves a reproductive cycle — and also to model the environment and model the self, etc.

So he essentially thinks that it's going to be quite difficult to create crisp, transparent interpretations of what biology does, because a lot of biology is based on these computationally irreducible structures and loops and patterns. As a consequence, it sounded like he's in some sense somewhat pessimistic about the potential for transparency or interpretability in biological systems. That doesn't mean that advanced AGI cannot be interpretable. It's just that it's very likely that if you're constructing such systems in a bottom-up way — for example, using neural networks, especially very complex multi-layer neural networks — chances are those systems are going to give rise, in a very bottom-up way, to computationally irreducible patterns on the one hand, and then recruit them, use them as building blocks.

So it's not that biology is a building made of clear bricks, all of them the same size, all of them well placed. No: the building blocks of biology are more like, oh, why does this do what it does? Well, it does what it does because it's a computationally irreducible pattern, but there's a higher-level process that has figured out how to use that and recruited it for building a wall, or a door, or a mechanism that connects doors, and things like that.

## David Pearce's four questions

Foremost among them, I very much like the breakdown of David Pearce.[^pearce] You have to be able to explain:

1. **Why does consciousness exist**, number one.
2. **Its causal properties.** In what way are we able to talk about our own consciousness? In what way is it not epiphenomenal — actually doing something that evolution might find useful?
3. **The binding problem.** How is it possible that a world presumably made of atoms and forces can simultaneously package a region of itself such that all of it contributes to a unified moment of experience? Why am I here and you're there? If we're all part of the same field of physics, as it were, why are there these divisions?
4. **The palette problem.** Where do all of the textures of qualia come from? What is their interrelationship, and why? Why is there phenomenal color? Why is there synesthesia — why is it possible to combine tactile sensations and smells, for example, and what are the rules behind those combinations? Palette in the sense of: what is the range of paints you have in order to make a painting, if you see what I mean? Or the notes in a musical instrument that build up towards the composition.

In particular, I think the combination of the binding problem and the causal problem is very insightful. I think the unity of experience is part of what makes it causally efficacious: the fact that you have an integrated moment of experience, and that you're seemingly instantiating a geometric or pseudo-geometric screen of consciousness that is itself being utilized for computation.

## Holography between fields of different dimensionalities

Now, here's where the rubber meets the road. I am going to hypothesize that fundamentally, the reason you're conscious is that these moments of experience, these integrated information streams, are facilitating the communication between fields of different dimensionalities, or even just different geometries.

In particular, I like to use the following example. The tactile field is sort of three-dimensional — there's a three-dimensional sensory input. And the visual field is two-dimensional: you have a two-dimensional retina. Of course, you have two eyes, which provide the potential for depth perception, so in some sense the visual field is 2.5-D. But the actual sense of embodiment of looking at a window and having the sense that if I walk in that direction I'm going to be able to touch the window — you anticipate tactile sensations through projective transformations of visual qualia. So there's a deep synesthesia.

In fact, one of the key insights from the Canada retreat comes from Asher Arataki, Wystan Bryan-Scott, Roger Thisdell, and Cube Flipper. Cube Flipper, by the way, has touched upon holographic properties of consciousness and also recently posted about the path integral formulation of moments of experience,[^path-integrals] which I recommend checking out and reading — very much QRI perspectives, Smooth Brains perspectives, as it were. It's a bit of a rabbit hole.

But the point is that this crowd, within the context of a QRI research retreat, arrived at the insight that all visual sensations are somatically tracked, and that it is an anomaly when you have pure vision — vision that is not encoding predictions of tactile sensations in potentiality. Roger Thisdell wrote an article called "On Pure Perception," which you can find on heart.qri.org, where he talks at length about this.[^pure-perception] So it's actually unusual to have a visual experience that doesn't have a tactile component. In some sense, all visual sensations carry within them counterfactuals about how you would feel the world if you were to approach it in certain ways. Again, take the cup of tea I'm holding: my visualization of it entails that if I put my hand here, I'm going to feel warmth. That's part of the visual encoding.

So all of this is to say that I think the unity of consciousness — the quasi-geometric, holographic, many-pieces-of-information-all-at-once quality of experience — is fundamentally working as a hardware accelerator for increasing the speed of communication and the coherence between sensory streams of different dimensionalities. This is a very non-trivial insight, and I think it has far-reaching consequences and implications.

### Evidence from cessation

I think it's also well supported from various points of view. In particular, it is well supported from the perspective of deep cessation — when you blank out of consciousness, deep in meditation, in what some people call the ninth jhāna.[^cessation] At least based on interviews and on talking to people who can experience this reliably, there seems to be a projection of the sensory fields on top of each other in such a way that waves traveling in each of the sensory fields look identical: a wave moving across the tactile field is indistinguishable from that wave moving in the visual field.

Whereas normally — 99.999% of the time — there are small phase lags between different sensory fields. So part of the information that you're encoding in your visual field is the way in which the visual field is disagreeing with the tactile field. Your world simulation is encoding that gap: the gap between how far away the things I'm looking at are, and the number of steps I would have to take in order to actually feel them tactilely, and vice versa.

So all of this is to say, I strongly suspect that one of the key hardware-accelerator properties of unified moments of experience — and therefore the causal properties of consciousness — has to do with these holographic geometric mappings between different dimensionalities.

## The research program

Now, this is fascinating and testable. In fact, I'm currently writing and developing a proposal for a research program around this. The core idea is as follows. I'm not going to share the core implementation details quite yet; this is more of a high-level "let's get excited and start thinking about this," because I think it's really profound and significant and walking in the right direction, expecting to be able to touch gold, as it were.

### Coupled oscillators and the free energy principle

The main idea is this: look at what happens when you have a hierarchical system of coupled oscillators. You can find examples of these in, for example, "From Neural Activity to Field Topology," a Qualia Computing post.[^coupling-kernels] It's fairly speculative, but it has a lot of dense conceptual frameworks that I think are very helpful for making sense of consciousness. One of the things I visualize in that post is hierarchical systems of coupled oscillators, such as **Kuramoto** oscillators. And something you can do with that is show, in a very hands-on way, the free energy principle from a computational perspective, embedded in a computational system.

In particular, say you have a video stream being fed to a layer of coupled oscillators, and that layer of coupled oscillators has additional hierarchical systems of coupled oscillators coupled to it. Here you actually have a clean expression of the free energy principle. How so? Because you can compute the free energy as a function of how many out-of-phase interactions there are between the coupled oscillators across the entire system. And this divides into two big categories.

The first is the phase difference between the input stream — the actual sensory information — and the first layer of oscillators. What that encodes is **prediction error**: how well the system is able to predict its environment.

The second component is out-of-phase interactions *within* the system, which effectively work as a kind of **regularization**, meaning it tries to keep the system's model complexity as low as possible. Because if you just have the one layer, and it's tightly coupled with sensory input but not tightly coupled with itself, then trivially you might be able to minimize prediction error. But how do you minimize prediction error that way? Well, you predict exactly what you just saw. The point of minimizing prediction error is that you provide a cross-validated model — in other words, a model that generalizes. You have to actually pick up the patterns, so that you don't only predict the current state but predict future states. You can anticipate what's going to happen. You have an appropriate model complexity for the task at hand. And that is what the hierarchical system of oscillators allows you to do: it picks up the relevant patterns that do carry over time, so that you can anticipate future patterns and minimize prediction error that way.

Okay, so this is even just one dimensionality. You can do that for each of the eyes: you have the retina as a system of coupled oscillators, and then you have the whole optic tract and then the visual cortex. And yes, that's a complex hierarchical system of coupled oscillators, with skip connections and a bunch of things that complicate it a little bit. But the essence is the same.

And what I'm going to suggest here is that high **valence** is naturally the composite score of how many out-of-phase interactions there are within that entire system. Very high valence in this context entails that you're synced up internally and you're also synced up externally: you're able to anticipate the next input, but also you're able to anticipate yourself. You have nested Markov blankets that can predict each other. It's a little bit more complex than that, but to a first approximation that will do for now.

### The central core as a multiplexer

Now, I don't think you really need consciousness for that. I don't think that is the essence of consciousness per se — at least not when you're referring to second-order perspectival, self-aware, unified moments of experience.

Where it gets interesting is when you have multiples of these hierarchical systems of coupled oscillators that meet at a **central core**. And the central core functions as a kind of multiplexer for the fields of different dimensionalities. So the surface area for the tactile field might be 3-D, potentially — or let's say even 1-D, for audio — whereas the area of the visual field might be 2-D. And so, say in the thalamus, where all of the sensory streams are getting combined, that is where a moment of experience becomes more than just a series of coupled oscillators minimizing their phase differences. It's something more interesting. I think that is where a hologram would make sense.

And a hologram here has all sorts of fascinating properties. Being able to balance between the frequency-domain properties of the input and the spatial-domain properties of the input is in and of itself a kind of regularization, and reduces model complexity. If you have an autoencoder and the central layer is actually a hologram, you're forcing it to contain both frequency and spatial information in a balanced way — where a purely spatial data structure costs a lot, and a purely frequency-based data structure also costs a lot, and a hologram is a very nice in-between: the harmonic mean, as it were, of spatial information and frequency information.

What's more, by combining the different dimensionalities, and then encoding valence in terms of — or rather interpreting valence as — the aggregate phase difference of the model with itself: there, I think, is where you get something really significant.

### Temporal depth

I was thinking about this earlier and didn't quite explicitly say it, but your world simulation also has **temporal depth**. The temporal depth is contained within each moment of experience, meaning that you have a kind of escalator or revolving door, where information about the future — what you're predicting the future states to be — is stored at the edge of the system of coupled oscillators, and is slowly advancing in just one direction until it hits the phenomenal present, and then it moves to the phenomenal past. The prediction error is computed relative to the senses in the phenomenal present; however, you're constantly updating and maintaining information about your predictions of the immediate future. I think that's necessary.

### Growing a self-model from the bottom up

Essentially what I want to do is set up the organism, as it were — the simulation of the systems of coupled oscillators — embedded in a world with, for example, food and other systems like it, and then demonstrate that organically, within some architectural constraints, you end up having this central core behave in a way that is functionally holographic. In particular, you can probe it and see: here is the phenomenal self; this pattern of oscillation is actually representing the organism as a whole, and that pattern of oscillation is embedded within a bubble, a kind of diorama-like simulation that represents its environment.

And as the organism moves around, the internal simulation — which, again, is completely bottom-up; it emerges out of the architecture and out of trying to minimize prediction errors and internal inconsistencies — will naturally give you this sense of self and environment within that internal pocket, this coherence operator that bridges together different sense modalities. That in and of itself, I think, would be pretty awesome. And then something we can do is, through evolutionary algorithms and gradient descent — adjusting weights and modifying the architecture — study and explore under what conditions you actually get this emergent self-modeling, how accurate it is, and whether it's evolutionarily adaptive or not.

### The kicker: simulating the accelerator

Well, here's the kicker. What I want to do is take that central model and hook it up to something like a model of non-linear optics. Unless we actually use non-linear optics, which I don't plan to do for the time being, you need to cheat a little bit. Not really — it's not strictly speaking cheating, but it is cheating from the perspective of the other organisms that have access to the same substrate.

Why? Think of it this way: imagine simulating organisms with quantum mechanical properties. If you're simulating on a classical computer, you will have to slow down the whole simulation and say, hey, hold on a second, I'm still doing the quantum computation over here — because it takes longer to do on a classical computer. However, from the point of view of the causal network observable by other organisms within that simulation, it would legitimately look like a quantum mechanical object. Does that make sense? You're slowing everything down so that you can keep up appearances.

In some cases you might have to slow everything down a lot. If the quantum chip is very large, then the amount of time it will take you to simulate the purported quantum behavior within that pocket might grow exponentially. But we can cap it. We can say, okay, you can only allow up to five qubits or something like that. And then what we can show is that naturally, organisms that utilize those artificial speedups are better at surviving — because from the point of view of the environment in which they're embedded, they're actually faster and more efficient.

## Why this is not the IIT animats project

I will conclude with just one tiny reflection, which is that it is very important not to confuse this with, for example, the Animats project within IIT, with Giulio Tononi and others.[^animats] What they were showing is that in evolutionary simulations where you have tiny organisms that use neural networks for navigating an environment, one of the things they showed is that as the simulation progresses, the organisms express more integrated information. So in that way, they're trying to show how integrated information is evolutionarily adaptive — you kind of expect it to emerge just out of evolutionary systems.

This is conceptually adjacent, but there's a key differentiator.

From my perspective, integrated information theory, at the end of the day — when it is built on top of an ontologically neutral substrate that doesn't have these natural quantum speedups in and of itself — is actually **epiphenomenal**. Integrated information is just a measure that you defined, such that at some scale you will say, oh, there's a causal bottleneck. But the truth of it, deep down, is that it's still just a cellular automaton, and fully local, and there's nothing unusual happening computationally. So it's really an abstraction, and it's observer-dependent. It doesn't truly have causal effects — even though the whole point of IIT is to say that consciousness is wherever you see something that is more than the sum of its parts in its causal properties. But the math of it just doesn't give you that. It gives you something else: it gives you "hey, what is a good compression of that?" Controversial. I don't want to fight anybody, but that is my current understanding of the paradigm.

Whereas what I'm proposing here is actually radically different. I'm saying, no, it's not about information metadata, or about ways of seeing the system where, from a certain scale, the causal effects look like more than the sum of the parts. We see something more fundamental. We see how the organism chooses to recruit physics — this energy minimization function that has perspectival applications, and that has this coherence-operator application, but that is not in and of itself the coherence between fields, and is not in and of itself second-order perspectival representation.

In fact, what it is more fundamentally is this **holographic compression** with a clear boundary, where the total effectively functions as more than the sum of the parts. And it's not a metaphor; it's literal, in the same way that a quantum entangled object effectively functions in a way that is holistic. You need to take into account all of the entangled particles in order to be able to predict the next step. Likewise, in moments of experience, you need to take into account the entire experience in order to know what you're going to do, how you're going to behave, and in what way your neural networks are actually sampling that internal simulation for information processing.

But my money is on physicalist idealism, where in effect life and consciousness are recruiting physical properties that effectively function as computational accelerators. And I think a project like this would be able to show this in a very crisp way.
