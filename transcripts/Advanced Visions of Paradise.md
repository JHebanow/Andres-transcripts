## The qualia of the day: pure land consciousness

Before I get into it, the qualia of the day is pure land consciousness. What is pure land consciousness? I should first introduce you to the six Buddhist realms, which are one way of breaking down the possible attractors of consciousness. If you were to map the transition probabilities between different states of consciousness, and the limit cycles or attractors that emerge, this might be a good lens for interpreting those attractors. I highly recommend the book *Opening the Heart of Compassion*.[^opening-the-heart] It's a wonderful, wonderful overview of the six Buddhist realms and of the ways in which they can be extremely sneaky, manifesting in how we think and how we feel.

Whenever you are in one of these Buddhist realms, you tend to be completely stuck in it, because the frames you use to make state transitions come from that realm. In a sense, the realms are closed rooms. At times they seem to have a deep insight into the entirety of consciousness and the universe, but in some interesting way they might be a bit of an echo chamber. I will talk about these six Buddhist realms as I go through the various visions of paradise.

It is worth pointing out that in maybe 99.999% of their states of consciousness, human beings are in one of these six Buddhist realms. But you can also be outside of them. You can actually be free: you can be in a state of consciousness that is not actively engaging in activity related to any of these realms, and that is instead able to perceive them as options — as a menu of consciousness — and to notice their pros and cons objectively, without getting wrapped up in, seduced by, or embedded within any of those spaces. That is a wonderful place to be, because it's free, it's unbiased, and it's ultimately capable of really telling what is good and bad about each of them. Roughly speaking, that would maybe be the state space of experience of bodhisattvas and very advanced meditators. Some exotic states of consciousness might have that flavor too: for example, 5-MeO-DMT, and maybe to some extent MDMA or things of that sort, may give you a temporary glimpse of what lies outside the six Buddhist realms.

To some extent, a lot of what I'm going to talk about will be best absorbed — will be most useful to you — if you understand it as the work of people who have been trying to figure out how to actually gain control over states of consciousness, rather than be completely wrapped up in any of these limit cycles.

## Aesthetics: Eros, Psyche, and Logos

I'll also mention that a vision of paradise requires an aesthetic. If you want to carry out a vision of paradise, you need to coordinate with other people. You need a shared, fast, parallel, decentralized, very efficient way of tagging the events or situations that happen, so that you can quickly get a sense of whether they're compatible with the overall picture — the aesthetic. So when we reason about the future, about what could be a good utopia, so to speak, we're doing things on multiple levels. On the one hand, you're using an ontology: a sense of what is real, what is good, and what frameworks are adequate for understanding what is good and what is real. But you're also cultivating an aesthetic: figuring out a way to feel about images, concepts, and situations. And it settles down into a coherent structure that we might describe as a self-perpetuating aesthetic.

Now, "aesthetic" is very loosely defined for the most part. I have an article on eight models of art[^harmonic-society] that I recommend reading, to show you how tricky it actually is to know what an aesthetic fundamentally is. But I really like the work of Rob Burbea, a great meditation teacher and Dharma teacher whose work I've been reading and consuming recently.[^soulmaking] He describes an aesthetic as follows: in the simplest formulation, it has at least three components.

The first one is **Eros**. Eros is related to sexual energy and arousal, but it's not quite that; it's a generalization of it. It's passion: the desire to get closer to the images, sensations, and archetypes you're attracted to. Cultivating an aesthetic involves cultivating that passion towards those archetypes. The archetypes could be low-level or very high-level. They could be very abstract things — justice, beauty, intelligence, and things of that sort — or something much more basic, like a walk on the beach. Maybe that, or pictures of nature, is a key part of your aesthetic. In either case, though, for it to qualify as an aesthetic you require strong passion: a beautiful, resonant reverb of experience when you evoke those images, so that you can savor them. That's one part.

Another part is what you might describe as **Psyche**: the collection of images, the archetypes, the things you're attracted to. There's a dialogue between Eros and Psyche. And finally, you also need **Logos**, the overarching conceptual framework that makes sense of the Psyche. Take particular ideas — even something very, very simple, like: what is an atom? Your conception of it is very different depending on how you have filled in the details of reality — your model of reality. An atom is a very different entity for a particle physicist who knows quantum field theory than for somebody in elementary school who just learned about atoms and maybe hasn't even been taught that there are electrons and protons and quarks and so on. The Logos allows you to organize these images, pictures, and concepts within an overarching framework. An aesthetic is the simultaneous coexistence of Eros, Psyche, and Logos, together painting a picture of what the good is.

Ultimately, this gets implemented with hedonic tone — with valence, with "vibing", in quotes. Cultivating an aesthetic is cultivating a way of vibing in which particular situations and images give the soul, so to speak, a beautiful, resonant, reverb quality.

### Why disagreements are often aesthetic

That bears on many of the disagreements that happen between people. At the intellectual level, two people may share ideas, concepts, and background assumptions. But if they've been cultivating very different aesthetics for many years, something one of them says — something factual, something true — may not be possible to absorb within the other's aesthetic: within their semantic web, their Logos, or the kind of Eros they have been cultivating. Even if you know intellectually that it's true or correct, if it doesn't fit within the web of harmony and consonance between the various elements of your aesthetic, it's going to be dissonant and unpleasant. I think it's quite interesting that a lot of the time, when people disagree, they actually agree on the object level. It's just that their aesthetics are different, and they don't know that, and it's not obvious. Also, because of naive realism, whenever you experience something dissonant, it feels inherently bad. So when somebody says things that go against your aesthetic, it might feel fundamentally very unpleasant. In that sense, it's involuntary. It's involuntary.

I would definitely suggest equanimity for this talk, if not in general: notice any discomfort with whatever I might say. Perhaps you agree with it at the object level, but you may say something like, "Well, it doesn't quite check out." My claim is that this is actually because it's causing a little bit of dissonance with your implicit aesthetic. Most people have implicit aesthetics; they don't have a theory of their aesthetic laid out, and for the most part they haven't gone that far. So that's a super important thing.

## Negative versus positive visions of the future

One last thing before we go through the camps: there's a key difference between **negative** and **positive visions of the future**. There's a bit of a play on words here, because when somebody asks "What is your negative view of the future?" you imagine they mean which dystopias you think are likely. No — I'm using "negative" here in the sense of testing for a disease and the result coming back negative, meaning you don't have it. So a negative view of the future is one where you say: paradise should not have X; paradise shouldn't be X. X might be intense suffering. It shouldn't be a totalitarian state. It shouldn't have mind control. It shouldn't be completely stuck in a particular, super narrow range of emotions, or something like that.

If you dig deep into the utopian ideas people have — communism, anarchism, any high-level conceptual framework that organizes a notion of what a good future is — you will overwhelmingly find that they are actually negative views of the future, disguised and presented as positive views of the future. I hate to mention this, because it's obviously very controversial and people are still attached to these — I would describe them as low-dimensional, low-grade visions of the future. But just as an example, something like Marxism says, more or less, "The future shouldn't have class struggle," or "It shouldn't have the bourgeoisie," or "It shouldn't have injustice." It shouldn't have X, Y, Z. Sure, okay — what if we don't have that? Does that make it wonderful? No. You've just ruled out a tiny corner of the possible badness in the universe. Isn't it insane to base your whole worldview, and your visions of paradise, merely around what you don't like? It's insane. It's a complete and utter lack of imagination, a failure to dig deep into what you could have. And oh my goodness, if you actually open your mind up to what you could have, there are some wonderful discussions to be had — but you require an adequate conceptual framework to actually dig into that.

### Value bottoms out in states of consciousness

A very, very, very key part of that framework is understanding that what ultimately matters — the source of value — is states of consciousness. That is where value bottoms out: the inherent goodness or badness of the universe is determined by the goodness or badness of the experiences that inhabit that universe. Visions that talk only about modes of social organization, or even about being free of pollution, going back to nature, whatever it may be, are all talking about the external physical world and how it's organized. David Pearce might describe all of those utopian visions as rearranging the deck chairs:[^deckchairs] not getting to anything actually deep, if you see what I mean.

So, in order to explore positive visions of the future, I urge you to open your mind to the role of consciousness in the future. Not only raw pleasure or something simple and low-level like that — eating candy all the time, or something like that — but, first, understanding that experiences have instrumental value, because consciousness can be used for information processing. From that fact alone, consciousness is clearly very likely to play a very big role, if only as part of a computational system. But second, consciousness also has intrinsic value. As Nick Bostrom might put it, utopia resides in the spacetime of awareness.[^letter-from-utopia] That's where utopia is. It's not out there in the world; it's not machines and robots or something like that. No, it's in the spacetime of awareness. Now that we have clarified that, the question becomes: what do you want to fill the spacetime of awareness with? Well, let's go through some fascinating visions.

## Camp Hedonism

First of all, what we call **Camp Hedonism**. This is yacht parties and drugs, talking to very attractive people, going to very beautiful festivals, eating a lot of beautiful, very tasty food, experiencing fancy, fancy perfumes — the good stuff of life, or something like that. And let me tell you something: there's a lot of truth in this perspective. There's controversy about whether this is good science or not, but look at what people regret on their deathbed — so to speak; no, not so to speak, literally. If you interview people who are about to die and ask what they regret, they say things such as "I shouldn't have taken myself so seriously" and "I shouldn't have worked that much." But above all, they say things like "I should have enjoyed myself more. I was too caught up in the winds and currents of life, and I didn't stop to smell the flowers" — or, as it were, to go to the club and party.[^regrets-of-the-dying]

Honestly, for a lot of people — a very large percentage of people, maybe a majority — a lot of what makes their life less interesting is that they're not actively looking to expand their horizon of experiences. A very beautiful insight that comes from Camp Hedonism is that value exists, and you'd better find it in the world. Go and get it. Be bold; try it. Don't be completely shy and miss out on opportunities. If somebody invites you to a weird party, go for it. There's a lot of value in that way of thinking. It knows a lot about the textures of experience. It's open: a very open, open-to-experience kind of vibe. And that's great. That's beautiful. Actually, I think any paradise worth its name will have a lot of that. We'll have artists making amazing, amazing art, and chefs making amazing food — or whatever we evolve to consume — and, basically, these intense, beautiful qualities of awareness that can be cultivated and experienced. All of that: we should have all of that.

Now, there is a caveat: in our current human vessel, as it were, there is this thing called hedonic adaptation. If you go to one yacht party with very beautiful people and amazing music, and you network and it's awesome, that's great. But if you're doing that every week nonstop, it's like pushing the pleasure button until at some point nothing is happening. And it gets a lot worse if you're talking about hardcore drugs like methamphetamine or heroin, things of that sort. They cause a lot of pleasure in the moment, but they come at a very, very high health cost, and also with hedonic habituation: if you actually plot how good they are over time, they tend to be net negative, especially if you use them a lot. Again, moderation is key. If you can have moderation and be rational about it, Camp Hedonism is great. And honestly, if you think about it, that is what people tend to imagine as paradise in a lot of cases.

### The hungry ghost realm

The corresponding Buddhist realm we might ascribe to this camp — describing more its negative quality — is the **hungry ghost** realm. The hungry ghost is the feeling of things not being enough. You had a meal, and you feel, "That was good, but I just need a dessert." Or you had a little bit of some drug, and it's like, "I feel good, but I should just have a little bit more." Romeo Stevens from QRI describes people in the hungry ghost realm as feeling that they need 50% more all the time, and it really doesn't go away. With things like hard drugs, a lot of partying, and the typical pleasure-seeking activities of humans, that builds up, and you end up becoming a hungry ghost. If you go to a rave and get into the in-crowd, chances are that a lot of the time at the party they're in a hungry ghost state of consciousness — which sucks. Really, filling it up with hungry ghost qualia is not a way to treat your mind, your consciousness. The hungry ghost is very sneaky: it sneaks up on you, and you don't realize how much of your experience is hungry ghost until it's too late, in a way. It can also entrap you very, very hard, because it makes you compulsively do the things that feed the hungry ghost consciousness too. So that's very important to avoid. So what is to be done?

## Camp Psychiatry

Well, let's go on to the second vision of paradise, which is **Camp Psychiatry**. I don't know, this may sound kind of scary. But what are the core insights of Camp Psychiatry? First of all, the recognition that we are pretty bad at taking care of ourselves. Humans are kind of like pets, in a way. We don't realize the extent to which, for example, eating very, very bad food has to do with the fact that the junk food is just right there. Or drug abuse — becoming an alcoholic because there's beer in your house and you have access to it, that sort of thing. Camp Psychiatry knows that. It's one step above, in that it recognizes that there are proximal and distal desires, first-order and second-order desires, and that we should cultivate the second-order ones more. It's not only about what you want right now; it's about what you want to want — what it would be good for you to want, if you could. How can you make it so that you actually want to eat salad, for example — by making really good salads, and things like that?

The other very big insight is the **area under the curve**. If you plot your hedonic tone over time after you take methamphetamine or something like that, you will see a very big spike, then a drop, and then the curve goes negative: you're going to be in negative territory. Especially if you've been using it before, it will be a net negative — the integral of this curve over time gives you a negative result. So those activities are actually just making you more miserable, all things considered. Camp Psychiatry knows this; that's what it looks for. It asks: okay, what is the net contribution of this?

![[fig-area-under-the-curve.svg|Hedonic tone over time after a dose: the net effect is the area under the curve]]

Very importantly, though, what it really doesn't know is that there are much more valuable states of consciousness than its normalizing aesthetic aims for. The way it tends to work is: you're presented with people who have very serious disorders, or maybe mild disorders, and the point is to normalize them — to get them to be productive members of society and, sure, maybe happy and so on. But intense happiness is not necessarily celebrated. It might actually be viewed with suspicion, as a manic or hypomanic episode or something like that: "You seem too happy; let's give you an antipsychotic or something to level you out." That's a big loss, because baseline human happiness can actually go way higher than the normal state of becoming a productive member of society. So that's important to consider.

I will also point out that an advantage of Camp Psychiatry is economies of scale. It's one thing to ask for help from a friend who maybe has a full-time job and a family and does other things, so that listening to you is a one-off, special thing — and who doesn't necessarily have specialized knowledge about it. Somebody who does this for a living, consulting with people day in, day out, hour after hour, will have a very good intuitive model of where your problem lies, simply because of the scale of it. Likewise, if you seriously want to provide a new therapy to humans in a way that will actually scale, it makes sense to do it through a specialized profession like psychiatry. So that's good.

### The animal realm

The Buddhist realm we might associate with the negative part of this camp would be the **animal realm**. The animal realm is the state of consciousness that develops when you become very comfortable with your everyday life. It's territorial. It's not ambitious or anything; it just wants to preserve its — you could say — mediocre way of life. That's a big risk. So many people end up stuck in the animal realm, working at a job they don't like for years and years and years, because its gravity well is too deep. It's very difficult to jitter yourself out of it, and I don't think Camp Psychiatry is very good at that. If you are in a worse realm, it can give you an improvement, but it's not the ideal. The aesthetic condensation of the negative side of Camp Psychiatry is **muzak and potatoes**, from Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion — anyway, a fun reference.[^muzak-and-potatoes] Muzak is elevator music: very, very, very boring music, just barely pleasant, just barely worth listening to. And you're eating unspiced potatoes or something like that. It's like, "Yeah, this is fine. It's okay. It's an okay experience." You're not suffering — but you're actually missing out on so much. So, what is to be done?

## Camp Wholesome

Well, let's move on to the next one, which is **Camp Wholesome**. Not in a super stereotypical, tropey way — but yes: rainbows and hearts and good vibes. A very important point is that a lot of people underestimate the power of love. It's one thing to take love casually and maybe expect the world to give it to you, to generate it for you. It's a very, very different thing to have a systematic approach in which you cultivate it as much as possible: a heart-centered way of looking at the world, feeling it, and interacting with it. That is beautiful. That is beautiful. For example, I have received lots of reports that if you do loving-kindness meditation for something like one hour a day for an entire month or longer, and then take a psychedelic at that point, your psychedelic experience is going to be so much more beautiful and loving, because this beautiful consonant metronome you've been cultivating will really come to the surface — and that can be so moving and healing. In a sense, it is true that love heals all wounds. It is true. The problem is that love doesn't last very long in most circumstances. But if you can kindle it, if you can keep it going, that is a massive improvement to one's life — enormous, a much bigger improvement to life than taking Prozac or another antidepressant. Perhaps I would equate it with doing a lot of exercise; actually, even more than that. So take seriously the cultivation of loving-kindness and its extension, the Brahma-vihāras — the divine abodes. Beautiful, fantastic; let's do it.

This aesthetic also has a notion of listening to your body, which Camp Psychiatry doesn't. Say you take antidepressants and get lots of nausea, and you feel that the drug is toxic to your body in some strange way. You explain that to the psychiatrist, and they say, "That's normal; those are just the side effects of this particular drug. It's going to go away — or it might go away — so don't worry about it," or, "Just accept that that's part of the deal." Oftentimes your own judgment of how good this actually is for you is correct. Very often, I would say, if you feel sick from psychiatric medication, it's probably something you should really be listening to. So paying attention to your body and how it feels, and treating all of your organs and all of your components with a lot of love, is very, very important. And it really pays off. It pays off.

### The failure modes of Camp Wholesome

However, this aesthetic does have some side effects when it's taken to the extreme. For a lot of people, this is the most advanced vision of paradise and goodness they can have, and its failure modes are actually pretty extreme, pretty stark, very neurotic, very unpleasant, and should be avoided at all costs. I'll just mention a few. First, this aesthetic tends to be innumerate: it just doesn't care about numbers and quantities. That's a huge problem, because the size of the effect you have on the world, depending on what you do, is distributed along a very long-tailed distribution. This is the whole idea of effective altruism: that you should actually take numbers and quantities into account. Super simple, right? This should be elementary school. But somehow you have super well-funded, very prestigious charities, with professors on the board of directors and whatnot, that aren't even actually trying to compute the cost-effectiveness of what they're doing. They might use the number of people served as a metric, rather than something that actually matters, like quality-adjusted life years, or the amount of time spent in super-unpleasant states of consciousness that you might be able to prevent per dollar, or anything of that sort. Camp Wholesome doesn't process that very well. It's very strange, but having a heart-centered aesthetic makes you very suspicious of numbers, and makes them feel cold and calculating — which is completely messed up and total BS. So don't fall for that. It's such a huge failure mode: so common, so common, and resilient.

It's also dissonant with what works. If it turns out that particular modes of organization — even particular circuit layouts, or something like that — don't look like they have a fundamental fairness, sense of unity, love, and so on, if they don't pattern-match to that, Camp Wholesome will experience them as dissonant. That's a problem, because a lot of the things that work actually work in a way that is completely independent of whether they follow Camp Wholesome. The next camp takes that further.

But I would say maybe the worst part of Camp Wholesome is that it allows you to smuggle a lot of shadow into your thinking and strategizing: you may leave some sociopathic tendencies unchecked because you justify them in the name of love, for example. Power dynamics and things of that sort that are actually really damaging may be justified within a framework of "Well, the other people lack empathy, the other people lack compassion, they are potentially sociopaths or psychopaths," or something like that. But you've got to understand that this is a very low-resolution, very low-dimensional aspect of that aesthetic, and it's really, really messed up and messy. Unfortunately, I think it underlies so much value destruction, in which a top-down imposition of whatever the current cultural ideas of what love and compassion should look like influences how things work at the low level. I think that tends to be hugely disruptive. So, not good. Not good.

### The god realm

If I were to map it onto a Buddhist realm, it would be the **god realm**, or the deva realms. In Buddhism there is no creator god; there is a pseudo-creator god, Brahma — a high-level deva, or god, who believes he is the creator of the universe but actually isn't. But Buddhism does have this notion of beautiful realms of experience where, if you're reborn there, you will live for millions of years, and it's so full of love, unconditional beauty, and unconditional love. To get there, you need to do a lot of good deeds in your life. You have to have a very clean soul. You have to cultivate compassion and the divine abodes: sympathetic joy, love, and compassion. If you're very diligent and put a lot of energy into all of those, they would say, you might be reborn as a deva, in a god realm. And even in this life, you will live in a godly, divine, beautiful, loving vibe, and that's extremely valuable.

But the teachings on the Buddhist realms actually also talk about a lot of the side effects that happen in the god realm. I posted a quote from *Opening the Heart of Compassion* on Qualia Computing, called "Traps of the God Realm".[^traps-of-the-god-realm] The god realm actually tends to be pretty delusional. It's completely disconnected from any understanding of suffering: its nature, its quantity, and its causal underpinnings. It just ignores it. It imagines that it has already made it — it's already pure, already godly, already in heaven. Whereas, no: everything is impermanent. It doesn't matter how much loving-kindness and so on you cultivate; you've cultivated a finite amount of merit. You've cultivated a finite amount of it. It's not a final release. It's not a final solution. It really isn't. And the god realm doesn't know that. The entities in the god realms, so to speak, believe that this is eternal, that it's forever. There are tales, for example, of what happens towards the end of your lifespan as a deva in the god realms: you actually start to decompose. You used to exude beautiful smells, but now you exude repulsive smells; you have exhausted all of your good karma, you will die, and you will probably be reborn in a very low realm, because you don't have any good karma left. That is a problem.

So, in practice, Camp Wholesome has the issue that it's often weaponized and used with a big shadow — usually with a big shadow — and it's also delusional. It has poor models of how the world works, and poor models of how even to maximize itself. It's very complacent. It's not good in all of those ways, but it does have some beautiful insights that we should take and carry with us as we continue on this journey of advanced visions of paradise.

## Camp Paleo

So what is the next one? The next one is **Camp Paleo**, which sounds very silly: connecting visions of paradise with a paleo aesthetic. But I'm not only talking about the idea — though maybe I'm referencing it too — that we should probably use a bit of understanding of evolutionary history to determine optimal nutrition and things of that sort. Camp Paleo goes much deeper when it comes to its aesthetic. I'll mention a couple of things. First, it's very cognizant of superstimuli. It recognizes that we evolved to be in a place that is much more sensorily deprived. There's this meme, or joke, that a single Dorito contains more nacho flavor than a peasant in the 1500s would experience in the entirety of their life. That probably does something to us: it resets our expectations and pleasure thresholds. Hedonic habituation and the hedonic treadmill come to mind again. Camp Paleo is very aware of that, so its members are very mindful of superstimuli. And that's great. That's fantastic. They're also very mindful of optimal nutrition, optimal sleep discipline, and things of that sort — but also of understanding human incentives and human nature, and of not being delusional or in denial about them.

I would say there's a beautiful marriage between Camp Paleo and Camp Wholesome, in which you are trying to maximize love while also understanding that people follow incentives, and that the animal self has strong constraints that it is often not willing to compromise on. An aesthetic that evolves to incorporate these elements can be good. Independently of each other, they lack some important perspectives and understandings.

Camp Paleo also has a healthy skepticism of completely new interventions. It's going to be very skeptical of large interventions, in yourself or in the world, that are completely new relative to our evolutionary past. And that's good. It's actually good that there are people who are very skeptical of any large-effect-size intervention and who would be much more cautious. Rather than trying to create paradise by trying to change culture, Camp Paleo would be much more of the opinion that we should try to create a beautiful seed that makes incremental changes in a somewhat cautious way, so that we don't mess with things we don't know about. There's the concept of Chesterton's fence. And it's pretty explicit about the fact that if you remove normal human incentives, things usually don't work. That's important to take into account.

### Blind spots and the dark side of human nature

Now, I will say that it does have a couple of side effects, namely that it may have some blind spots. To some extent, Camp Paleo may not really understand that there might potentially be aspects of human nature — I don't know, I don't know; this is a hypothetical — that make us a danger to ourselves no matter what. Take mutually assured destruction as a game-theoretically stable equilibrium for things like nuclear weapons: it may be that human nature is actually incompatible with being able to use that kind of technology responsibly. In that sense, Camp Paleo might be a little bit too romantic about how far you can go with human nature. I think a bit of a transhumanist vision can complement it, so that you recognize that yes, there's a lot of good in human nature. Human nature is this very incredible thing: out of this insane level of Darwinian selection pressure, red in tooth and claw, we created something that is not completely terrible. To quite an extent, humanity is a pocket of goodness relative to the horrors of Darwinian life in a broader sense. That's very, very important to recognize, be very mindful of, honor, and respect, without doing too many modifications. But let's also not be too romantic about it. Let's understand that human nature can also take us to hell, which is the dark side of Camp Paleo — I would say maybe the hell realm, to some extent, because an aspect of human nature is cruelty. With cruelty taken all the way — for example, in maximizing power or something like that — we can expect pretty bad stuff to happen. Which is why it's so important to bring Camp Wholesome and the other perspectives to the table. So let's move on.

## Camp Energy

**Camp Energy** is camp number five. Here's the elevator speech, the ad for Camp Energy: a lot of the activities we do build up **gunk**, without our realizing it. Gunk is a very broad category, but roughly speaking, there are a lot of aspects of our nervous system that you might describe as gunky. For example, if you are hungover, you might experience a weird viscosity in your nervous system, or some kind of unpleasant tension or anxiety, or whatever we may call it. That's gunk. Or you eat a lot of junk food, and over time you feel disconnected from your stomach; things happen that make it so that things don't flow, energy doesn't flow, and there are a lot of blockages. That's gunk. Gunk can be generated by food, or by taking recreational drugs. It can actually be generated by reading or watching very low-quality media and aesthetics — things that build up physiological stress — and even by social stress or feelings of meaninglessness. Anything like that will accumulate gunk over time. So what can we do about it? There is one weird trick to get rid of your neuronal gunk: apply energy in a judicious way.

### The hell realm and the sacredness of energy

*(I made a mistake, actually: hell is the realm associated with Camp Energy, whereas Camp Paleo is the **titan realm**, in the sense that its main side effect is escalatory dynamics — cruelty, and cruelty combined with power. That could be the negative side of the titan realm. Hopefully I didn't say anything too confusing; apologies about that.)*

The negative side of Camp Energy is the **hell realm**, because when you intensify the energy of your experience, you experience things more deeply. Energy is, effectively, a multiplier for valence. In that sense, feeling a stomach ache can be bad; feeling a stomach ache while on 5-MeO-DMT can be hellish. ### Power-washing the nervous system

Back to Camp Energy: there's a common thread among all of the following things — sauna, psychedelics, cold showers, yoga, meditation, breathing, exercising, intense art experiences, ecstatic experiences. What is the common thread in all of these things? It's energy: they are all energy-enhancing activities — art because of novelty, since novelty gives rise to energy. And I mean that quite literally. There's the interpretation from the free energy principle, in which unexpected things increase free energy, but that's a computational-level account. Here I'm actually talking about something physical: the sum total of the energy of the harmonic modes in your brain. I do think it increases with all of these activities, and it's important that it actually does something. What does it do? It's power-washing your nervous system, and that gets rid of gunk. This is very related to the perspective that when you activate the harmonics, you're equalizing the components of your nervous system and getting them to resonate with one another — and that process shakes off the gunk. In this frame, the phenomenon of purifications that you can feel in meditation would be de-gunking your nervous system. Phenomenologically, it actually feels like you're breaking apart some kind of mud substance that has coagulated. Being depressed might be very related to having a lot of that — like having a huge mass of Play-Doh encasing your body and making it very viscous. Camp Energy knows how to deal with that, and that's awesome. Again, you have to be very judicious: you have to know how to apply the energy to de-gunkify your nervous system in a net-positive way. There's a whole art to that — and hopefully also a science, as we develop these visions further.

### Alchemy and its shadow

There is one problem, though: it's not aware of the underlying causal reasons why this works. From the point of view of Camp Energy, this is alchemy. A lot of people implicitly have a Camp Energy aesthetic: yoga teachers, psychedelic facilitators, and advanced meditators tend to have one. That's pretty surprising, because it gets out of the typical cultural views we have about paradise. It also gives them a deeper understanding of ethical priorities. If you often have access to high-energy states of consciousness in a way that doesn't damage you and actually heals you, you recognize that they are, in a way, hyper-valuable. They become the center point of your life, the thing that gives spice to your life.

An analogy Mike Johnson gave in "The Neuroscience of Meditation"[^neuroscience-of-meditation] is that if you have a tuning fork with mud on it, hitting it with a lot of energy and making it resonate at its own resonant frequency will shake off the mud. At first the mud is damping it, but the more you hit it, the more mud it shakes off. Eventually, rather than biking uphill, the feeling is that of biking downhill. There's stream entry in meditation, after which meditation is apparently much more fun. It actually feels easier to do, because you have this momentum: you've mostly shaken off the mud, rather than feeling, "Ah, every time I hit it with energy, it just resists. It doesn't want to move."

Okay, so that's Camp Energy. But it doesn't have a deep understanding of the causal structures; from the point of view of Camp Energy, this is alchemy. And as I said, its shadow side, its dark side, would be hell. There are groups of people who use only Camp Energy as their aesthetic: maybe some cults — maybe even some sex cults — have this aesthetic implicitly. That can be very hellish, because if you think the solution to any problem is just some kind of energetic work, well, in many cases it doesn't work, and it's extremely unpleasant. Maybe traumatizing — and maybe traumatizing to an extent that it wouldn't be if you weren't playing with energy. So that's important to keep in mind. It's not the end-all, be-all, and in a sense it doesn't understand itself. It's alchemical, magical.

So what is it that we should do, given this?

## Camp Self-Organization

Well, the next vision — and now we're going very deep. I think this level of analysis is really not present anywhere else, as far as I can tell. Camp number six is **Camp Self-Organization**. Here you have a desire — an Eros — attached to causal structures that you actually want to understand. Why does energy allow you to get rid of energy blockages? And why does that connect to hedonic tone and feeling good? Camp Self-Organization is where you actually model, for example, the brain as a weighted sum of connectome harmonics,[^connectome-harmonics] maybe with non-linear interactions as well. You understand things holistically — not in a cheap, spiritual "Oh, everything is connected" way, but in a much, much deeper sense: how do a lot of tiny building blocks working in the same way give rise to emergent large-scale behavior? What is that process? What tiny circuits of self-organization, when tiled, would give rise to collectively beneficial behavior? That is the sort of thing Camp Self-Organization is thinking about.

It's not trying to figure out an aesthetic to impose on everybody — not at all. This transcends that kind of approach. Instead, it's figuring out what low-level self-organizing principles, when scaled up, give rise to the good and the beautiful, ultimately cashing out in states of positive valence. But also: what self-organizing principles are better for, for example, communication, flow, the dissipation of energy, the buildup of energy, and also the diversity of patterns or evolutionary dynamics? What self-organizing principles give rise to good evolutionary selection pressures? All of that is what Camp Self-Organization really, really cares about.

I would mention that the free energy principle would be in this space, and also **neural annealing**,[^neural-annealing] the work Mike and I have spent a lot of time on. Buddhist annealing is basically Buddhism through the lens of Camp Self-Organization, you could say.[^buddhist-annealing] I would definitely include the symmetry theory of valence[^symmetry-theory-of-valence] as well, in the sense that it's a whole account of how, with holistic field behavior, we can quickly and in a massively parallel way identify whether an organism is in a good state or not, as a function of whether it is in a consonant state. Those are principles of self-organization. So is the whole aesthetic that analyzes, for example, DMT trips as an excited world-sheet[^world-sheet] that then has energy sinks, either through recognition — Bayesian energy sinks — or through symmetries, so that the things you experience on DMT tend to be hybrids of semantic content and symmetrical configurations. All of that. All of that is the aesthetic of Camp Self-Organization.

The truth is, we haven't even begun exploring this. It's a very large topic, and it's definitely not yet connected with understanding positive and negative valence and the ways in which you can actually use states of consciousness for various purposes. Camp Self-Organization is not going to describe your weird DMT trip, or your weird experience, in terms of "Was this another dimension, or a different wavelength?" or something like that. It may instead talk about which of the self-organizing principles in the periodic table of self-organizing principles are being used as the building blocks of that experience.

### Self-organization in the body: greedy organs

It may very well be that you can describe a lot about a person in terms of very low-level self-organizing principles that give rise to emergent personality behaviors. Something Mike pointed out, for example, is that if somebody is very greedy, that may not only be a psychological condition; it may be a reflection of the self-organizing principle the entire body, the entire system, is using. So if you're really greedy, maybe it's not only that you act to get more and more and more compulsively without feeling satisfied. It may also be that your kidney works that way, or that your heart or your lungs have those problems too — maybe they're also greedy about the glucose or the oxygen in the body. Imagine you have a lot of greedy organs, all of them playing a zero-sum game: the organism deteriorates way faster than if you have something where all of the organs are trying to make the other organs as happy and healthy as possible — where there's synergy, and a positive-sum game happens that ultimately gives you something much, much more than the sum of the parts. So self-organizing principles may cut really, really deep. And there's also most likely a huge range of possible alloys of self-organizing principles that give rise to completely different outcomes.

### The human realm

The Buddhist realm I would associate with Camp Self-Organization is the **human realm**. The human realm is basically this desire to understand everything. This is the picture *Opening the Heart of Compassion* uses for it:

![[opening-the-heart-of-compassion-p148-human-realm.png|The human realm as pictured in Opening the Heart of Compassion, p. 148|340]]

Here, that basically means studying field behavior and self-organizing principles, understanding your DMT experience through that lens, and all of that. Well, there is a catch to it. It's good: it allows you to navigate better, and it makes you less flabbergasted by not knowing what's going on. But it also has the negative psychological effect of closing you off to the mystery of reality — the very deep mystery. The truth is that all of this is still just scratching the surface, and the moment you truly buy into an aesthetic for real and think it explains everything, you have closed so many doors of experience, so many perspectives and ways of seeing. It's a huge loss. So yes, you should use the human realm to make as good a map of reality as you can — but with the understanding that if you're only ever in that realm, you will be closing yourself off to whole realms of experience, and dampening your sense of mystery, which is an important ingredient for continuing to explore and understand reality. So that is the side effect.

It's not a terrible side effect. It's not as bad as, I guess, Camp Energy, where the side effect might be, I don't know, a bad 5-MeO-DMT trip or something horrifying like that. Being closed off to the mystery is not that bad. Maybe arrogance — the arrogance that comes with thinking you understand how societies work and things like that, and playing a technocratic role — also has side effects. But from the point of view of self-organization, I think you can build a pretty compelling case that it's applying the right lens, one that is ultimately self-correcting. If that is the level of analysis, rather than being guided blindly by your aesthetic, I think it's going to overcome a lot of its own problems.

### The coral reef

A coral reef would maybe be a beautiful, beautiful metaphor for self-organization and what it's capable of: lots of tiny modules pursuing their own self-interest, and as a consequence you end up with this beautiful, hyperbolic, self-organizing system. Likewise, when we think of paradise, maybe we shouldn't think, "What are we going to do once we win, and everybody does whatever we want?" or something like that. We should think about what kinds of circuits, what principles of self-organization, would actually scale up in a graceful way — not only, for example, tiling the universe with something beautiful, or something like that. It's more like: how do we make a dynamic system that is resilient and antifragile at every scale?

I'll just also mention that the free energy principle is definitely here. There's also the principle of maximum flow; I would put it here too. And anti-tolerance drugs:[^anti-tolerance-drugs] understanding that there are feedback systems in the nervous system, and that maybe we can get rid of chronic pain by combining an opioid agonist with an anti-tolerance drug — maybe some evolution of ibogaine, once we get rid of its heart side effects and things like that. That would be the self-organizing aesthetic too. Basically: how do we fiddle with the parameters — the gain, the precision of synapses — in order to prevent the buildup of tolerance, or even just natural hedonic habituation? How do you prevent that? In the end it may actually not be drugs; it may be something else, like using transducers at the precise moment within a meditation session, or something like that. Who knows? But basically, it's the lens that says we have to understand the cybernetic feedback systems within our entire organism and fiddle with them very carefully, but on their own terms, such that what emerges is something very, very valuable.

## Camp Paradise Engineering

Which takes me to the final one: **Camp Paradise Engineering**, which has a symbol of its own. *(Let me take a sip of water.)* This is where it gets extremely deep, and honestly, I cannot do justice to this cluster of ideas; you would have to listen to them while in samādhi for them to truly make sense. A very key component, similar to the six Buddhist realms, is this: you can only really understand what aesthetics are, and in what way they are sneaky attractors that block your ability to perceive further, by being outside of them — by disidentifying from them, and knowing that an aesthetic is not you. When you marry an aesthetic, you are in a sense embodying it, and whatever goes against it feels like an attack, which is crazy. That's a very crazy side effect, but that's how we tend to work.

### The aesthetic of the meta-aesthetic

Camp Paradise Engineering is aware of that. It's aware of all the realms and all of these different aesthetics. I would perhaps describe it as the **aesthetic of the meta-aesthetic**: it looks at all of these different ideas and ideological frameworks and makes alloys out of them, without at any point getting wrapped up in them and buying their vision entirely — but also without dampening them. It's being able to experience all of these different visions with a lot of equanimity and let their updates propagate through your system, but without allowing them to get a grip on you. I think that aesthetic will serve us a lot. It will serve us a lot.

An example of this is how openness and fluidity regarding perspectives on personal identity influence our visions of paradise. We should recognize that, given our current state — our current learnings, our intelligence, our access to experiences, and so on — there's a lot to learn. There is a whole lot to learn, and it's silly to make any kind of final picture for the time being. We really should further the science of consciousness a lot more before we're willing to be bold about precise visions and precise ideas. Instead, what we should be doing is laying out the groundwork, so that we have all of the ingredients available to us to construct beautiful visions that we don't get wrapped up in.

### Pure lands

I would maybe make an analogy with Buddha lands, or **pure lands**: places where you go to learn — ultimately, where you go to awaken. But to play at that level, you have to have gone through many stages. It's a very high level to play at, because you need to not be prey to your previous aesthetics, and that is so difficult. I don't know the percentage of people who are there, but I would imagine it's very, very low. It's a Kegan level five type of approach to reality, for those of you who are familiar with Kegan's levels of development.[^kegan] There are so few people theorizing at that level, and I think we really need it. There are a lot of people selling solutions, or just raising their hands and saying they don't know — but not these nuanced analyses with all of these perspectives taken together as different ways of seeing. I think we can make a lot of progress at that level.

### Consciousness vs. replicators

I'll just share a couple of things. I've made videos about this, but it's worth reminding you. **Consciousness vs. replicators** is an important frame here.[^consciousness-vs-replicators] There are no good guys and bad guys; that's a bad, low-dimensional framework. Again, from the point of view of Camp Value, you have to recognize that as soon as you define the good and the bad, it activates some pretty primal emotions in you, which distort your thinking and allow you to make coalitions based on that. But what is the point of making coalitions based on something that's not true, or something that is just very partial — a very low-dimensional approximation, underfitting reality? That's not good. That's not good. I think part of the aesthetic of the meta-aesthetic is recognizing the ways in which aesthetics give rise to various kinds of fanaticism and closed-mindedness: being very aware and cognizant of them, feeling it in your body, and really understanding it.

Instead, with something like consciousness vs. replicators, ultimately we're all on the same team. We're all on the same side. Whether we realize it or not, we are on Team Consciousness. There are gradients to how much we realize it, but to the extent that we do, we can actually play as one team. And we can identify the dangers as coming from replicators out of control: from viruses, all the way to ideologies, all the way to, perhaps, robots we might create, or algorithms that might be really good at making copies of themselves. From that point of view, there's no identification — no commitment to a particular view of your identity that ought to be preserved. In fact, the whole feeling, the whole desire for something that ought to be preserved, is then reinterpreted as a replicator strategy: perhaps an important ingredient in anything that is pragmatically able to replicate itself, but very, very dangerous when unchecked, when not understood for what it is — perhaps when it's wrapped up within the shadow of one of the visions, not only these camps but whatever memeplexes there are in society.

So disidentifying from any particular pattern — recognizing, to use Buddhist metaphors, that this is an impermanent play of emptiness, and that clinging to it only makes it worse — allows you to see reality more objectively and to assess the pros and cons of the various states of consciousness that could be the building blocks, the inhabitants, of the spacetimes of awareness of the utopia.

### A flexible sense of self

So: a **flexible sense of self**. There's this fascinating thing where, in some aesthetics — a monotheistic aesthetic, for example — there is a thing you pray to, an external creator God. But then there are also, maybe, transcendent Gnostic views of divinity, where the thing you pray to is a unity consciousness or something like that. And then, within Buddhism and other religions like that, there's: okay, maybe not even that. There's emptiness, or the state of neither being nor non-being, or nirvana, or something like that, which is very, very transcendent but ultimately not agentic. And then there are things like samādhi, or states of super, super elevated energy that don't have a self; they don't have a personality. I think we can make the case that they're important building blocks of paradise. Okay, maybe paradise is not tiling the universe with little monks in samādhi or something like that. But maybe samādhi is ultimately pointing towards what this value is that physics can produce: the one electron of the universe and all of its tangles[^one-electron] — what states does it prefer? And it's not a personal thing. This is the craziest thing.

Because once you're at this level of analysis, where you're able to disentangle yourself from standard identity, you recognize that even the typical way in which somebody might have a relationship with the divine has built-up assumptions about the self and about reality. But if reality is just so much more expansive — so much deeper and wider than any of that — then all of a sudden that feels like a strange play, an aesthetic that is in a sense very childish — almost, almost childish, I would say. Because if the sense of self is a possible variable, you have to ask yourself: if we are doing this for the universe, but in the future there is no self, no sense of self — for example, if everything is a post-samādhi society, with no sense of self in the future — who are we doing this for? There's no recipient, no imagined sense of self getting this as a benefit. So a lot of our programs — our implicit assumptions about how, for example, favors are transferred, or goodness, or karma — are fabrications that rely on very deep, inbuilt background assumptions about reality. If you start questioning them, you realize that the visions of paradise you could have could be free of that. You could recognize them as fabrications, go deeper, and explore a wider space.

### A seed, not a complete vision

It goes so deep — it gets so crazy — once you expand, vivify, and energize these high-level visions that integrate all of the different camps I've been talking about, while also remaining open to the mystery of reality. I think that is a good way forward. I think that's a good space, a great place to inhabit. It's very subtle. Right now it's a seed that we are protecting, nurturing, and trying to give life to. Hopefully it can ultimately sprout into something very beautiful and blossom.

But for the time being, I wouldn't want to say it's any kind of complete vision. We assume basics. Part of the negative vision of the future would be that utopia clearly doesn't have intense suffering; that's a constraint. I can't really imagine a utopia with intense suffering, or factory farming, or things like that. Those are negative constraints, but that's not a vision of the positive side. Whereas I think we should play with all of these concepts with a very open mind, in a kind of high-entropy alloy of consciousness, where you're open to, for example, how samādhi comes into the picture. There's so much juicy qualia — so much beauty of a very high-level, philosophical kind — that is so difficult to explain. So that's what excites me, what gets me up in the morning, and I definitely want to share that with all of you. Thank you very much for listening, and join me in imagining possible advanced visions of paradise. Infinite bliss, everybody! May you have a wonderful, wonderful day, evening, or whatever time it is. Take care.
