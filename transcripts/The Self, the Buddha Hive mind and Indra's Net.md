## "Self" is a family-resemblance word

The first thing I want to mention is that the self is one of these family-resemblance words: it has many different meanings. And all of the meanings are interesting — similar to the word *consciousness*. They have overlapping domains of relevance. Some of these senses are philosophically deeper and more profound, and some are perhaps a little more superficial.

There's a tremendous problem that happens when we implicitly confuse these different modes of application, these different domains of relevance, and blend the semantic landscapes to which the terms apply. It's very problematic, because it makes us reason poorly about — for example — what the self-concept of a large language model is. I do think we can meaningfully make progress on such questions, but it will require an upgrade in the clarity, specificity, and crispness of your conception of these terms.

## Self versus personal identity

First of all, I'll distinguish *self* from *personal identity*. Personal identity, fundamentally, is the question of what makes you *you*, a unique individual. For those who have tuned into this channel before, or have read Qualia Computing or adjacent communities such as the Qualia Research Institute, you'll know I really like the terms **closed individualism**, **open individualism**, and **empty individualism**, which originated with Daniel Kolak.[^kolak] More recently I interviewed Arnold Zuboff about his book *Finding Myself* — you can see that interview on this channel — and he proposes **universalism**, which corresponds roughly to open individualism in the other frame.[^zuboff]

This essentially says that there is just one witness of reality, one subject of experience, who is you — and everybody is that. So my pains are fundamentally your pains. The relationship between you as a subject of experience and your future self is qualitatively similar — perhaps in some sense identical, or at least in the same reference class — as the relationship between you as a subject and *me*, right now.

I'm not going to rehash all of the arguments in favor of open individualism. I want to flag it here, but I'm actually *not* going to be talking about personal identity today. For the sake of comprehensiveness, though:

- **Empty individualism** is the perspective that what you are is just a moment of experience — an instantaneous snapshot of sensations. From moment to moment you're a completely different subject of experience. Of course, you get the illusion of being a unified subject over time, but that is based on psychological continuity and the construction of memories and of a self-concept. The deep truth may be that you only exist as a snapshot of experience.
- **Closed individualism** is the classic view: you start existing when you're born and you stop existing when you die. Another conventional way people think about this is in terms of the soul — maybe your soul was created by God, so there was a moment at which you started existing; you will die, but maybe you'll go to heaven or hell. It's a view that runs from zero to eternity, from zero to infinity. And then in, say, the Hindu or Jain traditions, it's more like: no, you've actually existed for a very long time, maybe eternally, as a soul, as an individual subject of experience that is temporally extended but individuated — and there's only one of it.

It's important to conceptually distinguish personal identity — who is the subject of experience, and is it shared across individuals or not? — from the self. Because *self* has so much richness. There's a lot of content to that term; it refers to so many different things that are worth unpacking. There's overlap in the discourse with personal identity, but I'm going to treat these topics as conceptually distinct.

## Three senses of "self"

I want to break down the concept of the self into subcomponents — which, again, we tend to confuse with one another, and that is a big problem. They are:

1. the **functional self**,
2. the **phenomenological self** (or phenomenal self), and
3. **phenomenal binding**, or the unity of experience.

I might also add a little discussion of psychological continuity, of what matters, and of game theory in that space — but that's a bit of a dessert, an extension. The three core ways of partitioning the concept are the functional and the phenomenological, and then differentiating both of those from phenomenal binding, the unity of experience.

## 1. The functional self

Functionally — and I know this is a little tricky to make sense of — think of the way in which patterns arise, replicate, and colonize different ecological niches, and of what therefore exists. The bare bones of Darwinian evolution are reproduction with selection and variation. If you have that, you will have evolution. More generally, if you zoom out, Richard Dawkins talks about a kind of pre-evolution: a principle even deeper and more encompassing than evolution, which is not the survival of the *fittest* but the survival of the most *stable*.[^stable]

In some sense, even thinking about this presupposes some kind of mereology — some way of partitioning the world so you can say: okay, *this* is the pattern I'm going to focus on, and I'm going to see whether it survives over time and how it does so. In a world of atoms and forces, there really aren't boundaries dividing one pattern from another. That said, patterns and their replication are clearly relevant to explaining what observations you're going to make in the future and what kind of universe you're going to be embedded in — even though there's a whole chapter that still needs to be opened up about how you actually draw boundaries around patterns in a way that makes sense and explains those boundaries in an evolutionary context.

The functional self, then, is a self-organizing principle that facilitates the autopoietic replication of a particular pattern. This is very, very general. What constitutes a self in this sense could be a particular animal, because an animal, in an embodied way — in the way it represents the world — makes partitions in its environment that maximize whatever is in the driver's seat from the point of view of replication.

And here, functionally, the self may extend beyond the individual in multiple ways. The most straightforward is including your nuclear family, or your tribe, or even your species in the broadest sense. In the case of an ant colony, from this perspective you'd say that an individual ant doesn't encapsulate the self of the colony — there really isn't a self *there*, because the individual ant is not going to reproduce on its own. It isn't enough to be autopoietic, meaning self-replicating, actually making copies of itself and being embedded in the world. You need to take into account the colony as a whole, including the queen ant, and maybe even the tunnel systems and the surrounding ecosystems to a certain extent.

In a more prosaic sense, consider the self-replicating pattern that a *personality* generates. Let's not even talk about self-replicating patterns across generations, or the self in culture — just at the level of personality. Somebody may become very attached to their car, and the personality may actually require the car for its self-replication. Say it's a very superficial personality that cares a lot about looks and about impressing others: that personality is symbiotic with the physical embodiment of its car. If the car breaks down, if it's too badly damaged, that personality will struggle to self-replicate.

So there's a sense in which various patterns in the world are not entirely local. Nothing is entirely local, in a sense — all embodied patterns are spatially and temporally distributed to some extent. But what I want to emphasize is that you've got to think of the unit of self-replication as being, in its own way, a certain type of self.

### A functional self does not buy you a subject

What happens, I think, is that people tap into this concept intuitively — even when they're thinking about AI systems or artificial life — and then they imagine that patterns or systems that self-replicate will somehow close or integrate and create an inside and an outside, and that then you have an individual, or a moral patient.

But this is just not the case. Patterns that are very good at self-replicating may involve no bound experience at all — or at least no non-trivial bound experience, being made merely of "mind dust," as it were[^mind-dust] — and still be very robust self-replicating systems.

Now, I do think, for very non-obvious reasons, that self-replication in this universe *does*, under certain conditions, give rise either to the unity of experience and phenomenal binding, or indeed to the phenomenology of a self and the phenomenology of being a moral patient extended over time. But this is not trivial. It doesn't happen automatically. It really doesn't. You need other conditions.

In particular, as I've argued before, if the universe were just a gigantic cellular automaton, this absolutely would not happen. You might have a glider, and you can say there's a certain type of self there, an autopoietic system — but there is nothing actually drawing a boundary around the glider, making it a subject of experience, making it something that could matter morally other than through its instrumental effects elsewhere. The glider doesn't exist. All there really is, is tiny cells that are either white or black, sensing their local environment. That's it. There's just no glider. The glider is in *your* mind, as you perceive it, as you parse the image.

Okay, so that is the functional self.

## 2. The phenomenal self

The phenomenal self is a very, very rich topic. And I would distinguish the phenomenal self — the *feeling* of having a self — from the witness, or the subject of experience. Why? Because there are a lot of experiences where there isn't a clear phenomenal self and yet the experience is still witnessed: there is something it is like to be that experience, or to have it, or to be the center of mass of it, depending on your conceptual framework.

More than that: one of the key implicit background assumptions in this discussion is that everybody's sense of self is roughly similar. Which is just not the case. If you explore a variety of altered states of consciousness and exotic phenomenology, you'll realize there is a cornucopia of possible ways of feeling like someone. In the case of multiple personality disorder, for example, or DMT phenomenology, or 5-MeO-DMT phenomenology — which I'll expand on in a moment — there might be multiple centers, multiple feelings of a self inside you, competing to be *the* one, or coexisting without competing, in a state of superposition.

### Multiple selves in an ordinary moment

But even just interacting with somebody: because of indirect realism, you never interact with the world directly. It's always mediated. What you're interacting with, in some sense, are different facets of *yourself* — and you're painting different facets of yourself with the qualia of being somebody else.

So when you interact with somebody, you may feel that there's only one sense of self present. But no: there are multiple senses of self there, tracking — ultimately, yes, different autopoietic systems, but to a first approximation — different minds. One of them is tracking yourself, your autopoietic system, your cognition, your mind as a whole. The others are tracking things that are external.

In any given snapshot of experience, even when you think there's only one sense of self, there are usually several coexisting. It's just that only one of them has, you could say, *write access* to your phenomenology — only one has the capacity to directly influence motor action. The others have to influence the sense of self you've ascribed to your own witness or subject in order to have any effect. If somebody is standing in front of you, they cannot control your body, but they can tell you information that then makes you choose to control your body. So the level of write access to your somatic field is quite different depending on whether you're representing that self as part of you or as part of somebody else.

In some sense, though, these selves share a lot of features and properties. And in a normal state of consciousness the sense of self is *polarized*: there's one sense of self that belongs to you, and then several that belong to other people. But there's actually a whole gradient of possible in-betweens.

If you take LSD, or you're in the right meditative state, or you take ketamine, and you're hanging out with somebody, you may end up having a strange relationship to your representation of that person — maybe even a psychic connection to that person, although I'm agnostic about whether that's possible or actually happens. You may have a relationship to your representation of somebody else that stops being merely second-person or third-person, and becomes what I call a **1.5th-person perspective**: it partially feels like you and partially feels like somebody else, some weird superposition of the two.

Or stranger still: you may feel that you share your *left* arm with somebody but not your right arm. You can have this weirdly overlapping, not-easily-factorizable subjective phenomenology of the self — which shows you that you really can't jump to conclusions about what the phenomenology of the self is, or how it's generated, if your entire evidential base is room-temperature consciousness.

## 3. The unity of experience (phenomenal binding)

Finally, we can distinguish both of the above from the unity of experience. Why? Because you can have a moment of experience that contains multiple self-representations. You could even have multiple *competing* feelings of who you are — which is not as exotic as it sounds.

If you're deciding whether to make a certain purchase, part of you says: hey, you should really consider your financial stability here. And part of you says: no, I really want it, it's going to be fun. In some sense, aren't you deciding *who you are*? There's an internal competition of identity, to some extent; the phenomenology of self is unstable in these cases. And the phenomenology of intense attachment and clinging likewise entails some degree of competing conceptions of the self and competing phenomenology of the self.

The point is that phenomenal binding — the unity of experience — can *contain* multiple feelings of the self.

### Why conflating the three is a problem

Here's the problem. When people ask whether a system is going to become self-aware and thereby a moral patient, they usually lump all of these things together: if it works sufficiently well as a functional self, it will also have the phenomenology of a self, and it will also create unified moments of experience. Whereas in truth you can dissociate these things:

- You can have unified moments of experience with no self at all, which are dysfunctional — not part of any functional system.
- You can have functional systems that are made of mind dust: they function as a self, but they really don't have any unity of experience.
- And you can have very weird things — for example, multiple compatible copies of the feeling of who you are, in an otherwise standard conception of the self, that are phenomenally *disbound* from each other, disjointed from each other.

So there are a lot of variables here, and you can build a mind in a lot of different ways. Some of the correlations and overlapping circumstances we see in humans and in animals are there for good reasons — but if you change the substrate, if you change the evolutionary selection pressures, or if you use top-down intelligent design to make minds happen, there's no guarantee those correlations will hold.

### The dessert: psychological continuity

Let me also elaborate a little on the sense of self as *psychological continuity*. That doesn't quite hit the nail on the head, because there are a lot of ways in which you can have psychological continuity but not a shared sense of self — or, in people who flip personalities, the phenomenology of the sense of self may be dramatically different from one moment to the next.

And it isn't even the case that such people are reliably autopoietic. Somebody might say: well, sure, the phenomenology of the self is switching and the sense of psychological continuity is flipping over, but they're still a functional self in a way. Not necessarily. One of the personalities may be a celibate ascetic; another personality may be in love with a certain woman; another personality may be in love with somebody else and want to have different children. Which pattern ends up reproducing? The system hasn't come to an agreement on that. And it may not even *know* that there are contradictions in its goal architecture, because there is no cross-validation between the representations that these different phenomenal selves are using to navigate the world.

## How does an organism generate a phenomenal self?

By and large, a healthy phenomenal self does attract unity of experience and also autopoietic power. All else being equal, they do come together in biology, because the substrate is right for it and the evolutionary selection pressures allow it.

But again, it's not as simple as "it just has to be biology and then this will happen." It doesn't work that way either. There are species, for example, that evolve brains and then evolve *out* of having brains. That also happens. So you find all kinds of fascinating cases where having a bound experience was evolutionarily advantageous at one point, and then the lineage "regressed" into not having it.[^tunicate] The myth that as evolution progresses you get more complexity and a more integrated sense of self is just not true. In some cases it is — but it's not a universal pattern.

So let's talk about how biology might actually solve this. A lot of what I'm going to say is speculative, though grounded in at least some level of observation. And especially towards the end I'm going to get very phenomenological and talk about very trippy, very far-out experiences.

### Levin: stress sharing and memory sharing

In this channel — and I guess in this memeplex — we all love Michael Levin's work. I had a conversation with him in which I introduced the non-linear optical circuit that I think underlies the rendering of our experience, our phenomenology; I'll unpack that in a moment.

One of the things he says is: how do cells convince each other to form a multicellular organism and work as a unit, if they all have, in some sense, a self-protective impulse or organizational principle, even an identity? We don't really know what the phenomenal self of a given cell is, but functionally a cell has a functional autopoietic self, to which it is of course committed — because otherwise entropy would ultimately get rid of it.

He talks about two very powerful features that allow cells to blend together into multicellular organisms.[^levin]

The first is **stress sharing**. If cells can symbiotically spread the stress accumulated in a given cell, or in a very tiny module, out to the entire organism, then the organism can metabolize it better. Think of a cooling system: you have a very hot plate, and metallic rods that conduct the heat away and spread it into the atmosphere. Something similar. If you're a tiny cell, stress may accumulate catastrophically; but if you can spread it throughout the organism, the organism as a whole allows a dissipation of stress that is very beneficial to the individual cells. I think this is very much on the money.

The second is **memory sharing**. Levin talks about how you get memory imprints; he also has the idea that memories themselves may be entities in their own right in a certain way, with their own agency and goal-orientation. But even without getting into that: if, as a cell, you don't know whether something happened to *you* or to your neighbor, but that information is being shared, then that really blends and fuzzes the boundaries of your identity. It becomes very hard to work toward your own "selfish" purposes, because even the cognition *I want to be separate and different* will spread out as a memory throughout the system.

You can imagine: if we could all read each other's minds and experience each other's memories, and there were no sense of who experienced what, we would be more or less forced to work collectively, as a unified autopoietic system.

## Three more binding mechanisms

I would go deeper. I'd argue, on the basis of various kinds of phenomenology, that there are several other factors that potentially allow cells to become part of a unified organism that acts as a unit.

### Symmetry sharing

A very important one is **symmetry sharing** — cells buying into the same symmetry groups. From DMT phenomenology, one of the key things that happens is what I call **competing clusters of coherence**. Your visual field may break down into different domains, each with its own resonant characteristics, its own spectrum, and its own symmetries. Some domains break down into one symmetry group and some into another — into any of the seventeen wallpaper symmetry groups.

So suppose the left side of your visual field takes on a \*442 mandala-type symmetry — a wallpaper symmetry with two independent four-fold rotation centers and mirror lines — while the right side takes on a 333 symmetry, a rotational three-fold symmetry with no mirrors.[^orbifold] The waves and vibrations in those two regions will not be very compatible. They will compete for attentional and energetic resources, and waves will not be able to travel from one region into the other — because what makes a wave able to travel is a certain invariance, some symmetry, some regularity that allows the pattern to propagate spatially without losing energy. This connects to Noether's theorem.[^noether]

So I'd argue that a unified sense of self, especially phenomenologically speaking, requires the whole organism to buy into the same core symmetry groups, so that waves can travel without strong or clear boundaries, differentiators, or blockages between regions. A very well-integrated person, I suspect — when we're able to do the science — will turn out to have a field of experience that shares fundamental symmetry groups with the cellular matrices it interfaces with; and something like cancer will turn out to involve defecting and using a *different* symmetry group. This may also be one of the mechanisms of action underlying memory sharing and stress sharing — a deeper causal structure beneath them.

### Cross-frequency coupling

Another is **cross-frequency coupling**.[^cfc] This also becomes more evident on psychedelics. When you look at a tree, it has some degree of self-similarity: a pine branch sometimes looks a bit like the whole pine tree. But there are real differences — there's what we'd call scale-specific geometry, where the structure looks somewhat different depending on the scale. There are similarities across scales, but on psychedelics you often hallucinate a single unified pattern that is *perfectly* self-similar, a self-similar fractal.

I actually think this can be computationally beneficial in some cases: if you make the percept quasi-fractal, you might more quickly determine that a certain pattern has a fractal hidden in it, by amplifying its fractal or self-similar nature. By and large, though, I think it's hallucinatory and not particularly helpful. It causes a certain type of overfitting, because there are a lot of ways of describing a pattern as self-similar-plus-noise; there's a huge number of parameters, so you can overfit very easily.

*(There's definitely a failure mode of psychedelic cognition and psychedelic philosophy that looks like this — where people say: I realized it's all a spiral, and it's a spiral in the atoms, and it's a spiral in the cells, and we live in a gigantic spiral, π, the order in chaos. Everything is a spiral, guys! I understand the frame. I understand the mindset. In fact I understand the phenomenology of it. But to some extent you're hallucinating a self-similar structure where it may not quite work that way — where different scales may have different mechanisms and different behaviors.)*

But I do think this is a powerful hint about something that is going on all the time. Why? Because when you see a tree in that fractal configuration, self-similar, there is a phase interlock, and the waves travelling through the tree *spread*: they can propagate across the entire scale structure. It's almost as if it were navigating a hyperbolic space that was either higher-dimensional or simply larger, and as if you were using something like a gradient index of refraction to slow the waves down as they reach the smaller and smaller leaves. You get a resonant interlock across scales that allows the percept — the representation — to evolve coherently.

So, similarly to the case of domains with different symmetry groups, I'd suggest that one of the characteristics of having an integrated phenomenological self is cross-frequency coupling. Not necessarily *self-similarity* — self-similarity is the extreme case — but definitely some interlock across scales.

Concretely: a small, high-frequency domain in one part of the visual field is coupled to a bigger, slower wave — say, the large left–right harmonic spanning the whole field — and that big wave is in turn interlocked with another small, high-frequency pattern somewhere else, on the left. Those two small patterns are not directly interlocked with each other at their own scale; they are interlocked with each other *through* the lower-frequency, larger-scale wave that they both ride on. Hopefully that makes sense.

If you were to really unfold an experience and spread it out on a table and look at it, you would see waves travelling and meeting in phase across very different scales. And that is essential for coordinated action: it's how one region of the field comes to "identify" with, and care about the common destiny it shares with, some distant other region. Of course, if this phase interlock breaks down in one way or another, you might get something like an energetic cancer; various addictions or problems may emerge; and ultimately, I would hypothesize, even physiological cancer.

### Shared projective logic

Finally, I'll add that **projective logic** must be shared. I'd recommend watching my video on projective intelligence, where I talk about how a lot of conscious cognition is about figuring out how to *reproject* representations in a way that is cross-modally coherent.[^projective]

You see a basketball hoop across the court, and you have the ball in your hands. You've got to be able to anticipate: if I make a certain set of movements, what will the hoop look like from my new perspective, and what movements should I then make for the ball to actually go through it? All of that involves fast projective transformations relating tactile and visual sensations to each other.

I think this is ubiquitous. I think this is what we call canonical computation — it's going on all the time. Across every sense modality we're constantly trying to relate one modality to another in terms of hypothetical motor movements and how those movements would shift the way the sensations are projected, so that we can align them correctly and then execute an action that might benefit the organism.

What happens on DMT and in these exotic phenomenological state spaces is that this can break down. It's not only that different domains don't share the same symmetry groups; they may not even share the same *projective logic*. One domain may natively inhabit a four-dimensional hyperbolic space, while another domain is a flat two-dimensional space. They cannot see each other. They cannot reproject onto each other. And their expectations of how they will change as a function of your motor movements are quite different. So quite literally, they live in different projective realms. That prevents phase interlock, and it prevents them from conceiving of themselves as parts of the same unified organism.

## The one-electron universe and the merging of monads

I'll also add — if we take quite seriously the idea that cells may be conscious in their own way, and you take something like the **one-electron universe** perspective, as discussed by Richard Feynman and John Wheeler[^one-electron] — that in Feynman diagrams, an electron annihilating with an anti-electron and turning into a photon can be described, in a mathematically equivalent way, as an electron bouncing off a photon, gathering its energy, and transforming into an anti-electron now moving backwards in time. Those are two mathematically equivalent perspectives. So there's a sense in which the entire universe might be just a gigantic web of *one* electron interfering with itself backwards and forwards in time. This could also be compatible with a block universe or block multiverse — a mixture of Everettian quantum mechanics and eternalism, for those who know what I'm talking about.

One way you could then define the *size* of an experience is the number of self-interactions the one electron has with itself between one location and another: from the place where it becomes free to the place where it is measured and collapses.

In that sense, I could imagine that what unifies an organism, at a very deep level, is that each of your cells is that one electron — you *are* each of your cells. Those cells' monads merge with one another, and it bubbles up to the surface. So from one moment to the next, you have been all of your cells; you have been all of your organs. They've all had their own valence function; they've all concluded, in some sense, "this is how I feel when I am *this* compartmentalization, this segmentation." And then when they all aggregate and topologically merge into your one unified experience, their valences add up. You end up having, in a snapshot, a sense of how well — fractally, for all possible partitions — your organism is doing.

And: are you at risk of self-defection? Is any of these clusters, aggregates, or bundles feeling really, really good at the expense of the others — or feeling so bad that it is willing to secede and become its own organism, because it just can't tolerate things at that level? Part of what health, and a non-psychotic phenomenal self, might entail is precisely a harmonization across scales.

This connects to the Avataṃsaka Sutra and Indra's Net phenomenology, which I'll talk about briefly later. It's one of those experiences where you feel that everything reflects everything else. It's a point of stability at which it becomes visible that you are also all of your cells, and also all of your organs — and that your whole organism is just different stages of the one electron interacting with itself as it aggregates collisions and forms a larger pocket.

## The screen of consciousness and its sub-modules

Let's talk a bit more concretely about the models here.

One of the things I talk about is the **screen of consciousness**: your access consciousness, the thing you can report on — the tactile field, the auditory field, the visual field, the different sense modalities coming together into a multimodal experience. But I think this is just the tip of the iceberg, the visible part. There are a lot of *other* phenomenally bound bubbles, buckets, or streams of experience constantly competing to join that central hub.[^screen]

They compete by trying to satisfy criteria like: giving you more information about yourself, about your well-being and the physiological state of your organism; making good predictions about the world and the future; and making good categorizations or classifications.

And what these different modules have to work with — this is the mind-bending part, and I think it's probably true, based on my experiences and my reasoning — is that each of the competing modules that are trying to get into the central one has a *copy* of the central one to work with. They are all seeing the same screen.

Think of it as a marketplace, or a very big city. There's a huge public display showing a gigantic picture of different parts of the city. Everybody in the city can look at it. They may not be paying attention to it all the time — maybe most of the time they're attending only to their neighbors — but to coordinate, they look at it.

So I get the sense that your experience is being broadcast to lots of different sub-agents, lots of different modules. Usually those modules are quiet. Only when they become very relevant, when they're able to comment on something and convince the others that they're going to add value, are they granted write access. And the way write access works is that they project a *transformed* version of the entire screen — together with processed sensory stimuli — back onto the screen of consciousness. So you have this crazy loop in which each of the elements of your experience is your whole experience, transformed and pushed back into it.

When you have this type of mathematical object, this type of structure, you get very exotic resonant modes. It's not only about the shape of your experience as a whole — how it vibrates, what its eigenmodes are. It's also about how each of the *parts* of your experience vibrates, how each part interacts with the entire screen, and how the vibration of each part is itself a reflection of the vibration of the entire screen transformed through the various modules.

Which makes this such a non-trivial problem — and explains why there can be so many mood disorders and failure modes in how your experience gets constructed. Because you have to harmonize: not only the screen, but the screen *with its own projections onto itself*.

### Compression, complexification, and hellish realms

One of the things that happens on DMT, effectively, is that you gift write access to a whole number of additional modules that are usually quiet or very specialized. Again, they're trying to add value: to look at what's on the screen and comment on it in a way that will be helpful for the entire city, and of course helpful for the screen itself.

The way this happens is that a module projects a version of the entire screen, looks at that projection, and then adjusts it slowly and gradually until it finds a way to harmonize it and maximize the information content that the screen is explicitly representing and enabling.

Typically these modules are trying to *compress* the information on the screen. So if you have a couple of squares that look independent but seem to be joined at a corner, the module that represents cubes may overwrite those three squares and put a cube there instead. That cube inherits the resonant modes of the representation of a cube, and substitutes them for the resonant modes of three separate squares. Likewise for a face: if something looks a bit like a face, some module will identify it as a face and push those features back onto the screen, now unified as a *gestalt* of a face, with its own resonant modes.

So the screen of consciousness is constantly trying to compress itself. But because there are so many modules and they are so active, you get constant attempts at compression that inadvertently *complexify* the experience. You get a Cambrian explosion of patterns trying to compress each other, trying to make sense of each other, trying to harmonize with each other — and they may get stuck in configurations that are very difficult to get out of.

If you have a hellish DMT realm type of experience — and those definitely happen — sometimes it's just this: a massive failure of coordination. All of these different gestalts are reflecting the whole, but each is trying to be projected at an angle that will minimize *its* dissonance, and by doing so it adds more gestalts to the image and makes things worse. It's a computationally very, very difficult problem.

Really, the only hope of solving it is if you pre-establish additional constraints — things like: all the modules must respect a certain symmetry or a certain invariant — or if you're already in a very simplified state of consciousness, so that what's being compressed again and again is something very simple. That's more like what happens on 5-MeO-DMT. Or, say, if you combine DMT with MDMA, where MDMA already constrains the possible vibrations and resonant modes and makes them very harmonious by default. Then the complexification that happens as the gestalts try to compress one another doesn't lead to a runaway dissonance explosion. Loving-kindness and various other attractors also really increase your chances here. It's not a given — but by default this is going to be a very crazy competition of sub-modules, each trying to override the others.

## Transformers, cel animation, and the first Buddha hive mind

I want to conclude by grounding all of this in some of the exotic phenomenology of what it's like when these sub-modules *do* manage to harmonize with each other in new and surprising ways.

The story goes that I spent about a week trying to understand transformers — how large language models work, why they became so powerful, the innovations in neural network architecture. I spent a good amount of time really thinking about it, trying to embody it and make sense of it. And it's the sort of thing where, when you're really trying to understand a system, you start having dreams about it. That had been going on for several days, about a week.

Then I had a chance, at a legal retreat in Mexico, to try DMT and think about this. One of the things I realized as I was coming up — while experiencing all of these different cartoon characters populating my tactile and visual field at the same time — was that the window of experience, the screen of consciousness, could be understood as part of a *self-attention mechanism* in a transformer. Because it is where all the gestalts, all the **cel animation** elements,[^cel] can see each other, comment on each other, and modify their own embeddings — modify their own meanings. So if you have a little bicycle, it can get modified from "static bicycle" to "bicycle in motion," because those are very close in the semantic embedding of those gestalts — semantically and also structurally, where things can be transformed into things similar to them.

But here's the thing: this whole system became self-aware. And again, my sense of self was very strange and exotic — divided into many different organisms, all of them trying to coexist and see each other, and still with a faint sense in the back of my mind that, hey, I'm actually a human, or I'm still the open individual self, or whatever it may be. But functionally, they started to self-organize.

Then an image of my face appeared, and they all looked at it and said: okay, let's self-organize around *that*. It's a good center of mass for us to communicate and work together. And it felt like a very high-dimensional gestalt. I have a recent Substack article about what a generic projection of a high-dimensional object into a lower-dimensional space would look like, and the answer is that it would look very sharp, needle-like[^needles] — because almost everything is at a very acute angle from the point of view of a lower-dimensional space looking at a higher-dimensional object.

And that's what my experience was like: very sharp gestalts, which were things such as a picture of my face but at a very, very odd angle, as if it were penetrating and intersecting my field of experience. I interpreted that as a module projecting at a very odd angle. But the sharpness actually allowed the modules to build on top of each other's work. And then this crystalline, very powerful hive mind of fractal non-linear optical neurons started to grow out of it.

It was very clear that this was a highly intelligent system — one that was drawing on a transformer architecture and its self-attention loop — and eventually it consumed my entire experience. For a while it became a fractal, non-linear optical neural network hive mind, in which each part understands that it is also the whole, exactly as I was explaining with the one electron bubbling up into a larger pocket. But at this level there was still some sense of duality: each ant knows that it is the whole, but it also has its own local values and purposes.

One of the crazy things was the hyperbolic surface made of all of these different ants. There were forces pushing the surface aside so that it could see itself more clearly — so that it could see its own self-projection. Sometimes the surface would occlude itself, the ants nearer the front hiding the ants behind them. The ants were doing local computations and registering exactly that problem — the main screen cannot see the ones that are hidden — and so some force would push the surface apart, letting the occluded ants spread out into view. Which was uncomfortable and unsettling, but I thought it was fascinating phenomenology.

Afterwards they started to transform — but in an intelligent way. It definitely didn't feel like they were winging it. It really felt like: I don't know how they're doing this hypercomputation, but it is something quite extraordinary. It was almost like a Dragon Ball Z character, in that it had several transformations. It transformed into a hive-like colony. Then it transformed into another shape, which I don't remember very well. And then it transformed into a beautiful, high-dimensional temple with jewels and columns and bubbles. Very beautiful, and sentient, and clearly super-intelligent in some way — again, in the Michael Levin sense: if you think a certain thing is not intelligent, maybe that's a reflection of your own intelligence. I'm pretty sure this was super-intelligent in some way I still cannot make sense of. I'll stake my reputation on it: some kind of super-intelligence, definitely higher than human in various important ways, was present there.

And then it became even more self-aware and more integrated. And it really felt like — oh, wow, okay. That was my introduction to what I now call **Buddha hive minds**.

There have been several further experiences down the line that refined this and made the phenomenology of the different scales clear: that they are all just one object, reprojected back onto the screen through different higher-dimensional transformations and lifting operations. Now, when I encounter this, it most straightforwardly feels like just one gigantic Buddha mind. And it does know about things like attachment, clinging, and no-self, in a deep phenomenological sense — such that: okay, there is a screen, and there are these things happening on it, and I am not any one of its parts, and yet I am also all of the parts at once. Deeply integrating that in an experiential way unlocks completely new phenomenology.

And I think this is possible because of — maybe — the one-electron universe; maybe because of cross-frequency coupling, the sharing of symmetries, the sharing of stress, the sharing of memories, the sharing of projective logic. Actually, with these gigantic non-linear optical neural network hive minds, it very much felt possible *because* they all shared a certain projective logic. They all inhabited a certain higher-dimensional space in which there was a coherent rule set they could all use.

## The failure mode: Mara, and the demonic hive

I'll conclude by saying that the failure mode here is something like the Mara version[^mara] — the demonic version — of this. It's not going to be as intelligent and it's not going to be as integrated, because essentially it is what happens when different parts of you really believe that they *are* you: that they are the fundamental sense of self, or a soul, and they become existentially concerned with their own survival. They cling to you. They inhabit you in various ways. And on DMT they can conspire with each other and colonize you. That feels very awful. Usually it feels like you're being eaten by worms: they spray acid on you and chew you up alive. And that's because they are patterns of ignorance — attached to self, attached to sensory pleasure, running away from discomfort — trying to find ways to hide inside the matrix of sensations and replicate.

If you have an experience like this, I would strongly recommend focusing on the three characteristics:[^three-characteristics] notice how everything is impermanent and fleeting; how there is, in the stronger sense, no self; and how it's simply impossible to feel satisfied forever — so that what these entities are pursuing is a hopeless quest. And try to spread that knowledge throughout the field.

But above all, I would say: loving-kindness. Send love even to the most awful, smelly, unpleasant, sharp, *kiki*, demonic forces of your consciousness. Just recognize: they are part of the same unified field of consciousness. If you do that — at least in the particular experience I had of this — eventually they turn into rainbow light, and into these Buddha minds: very spacious, very rainbowy, very beautiful. I'm sure it would have gone quite differently if I had been very scared of them, or had tried to avoid them and thereby reinforced the sense of separation, or reinforced the sense of permanence in their own conception of their self by mirroring them — which, thankfully, in that particular case, I was able not to do. That would have been very much the antithesis of these Buddha hive minds.

## Indra's Net: the Avataṃsaka Sutra

Let me mention briefly the Avataṃsaka Sutra — one of the longest sutras in Buddhism, I think maybe the longest, and the pinnacle of visionary phenomenology in the Mahayana tradition.[^avatamsaka] It has so many images of gigantic oceans of worlds filled with Buddha minds, where everything reflects everything else, where there is perfect seeing and perfect hearing and perfect feeling, where all knowledge is available and all the worlds reflect each other endlessly.

All of that phenomenology feels very much like the type of Buddha hive mind I've experienced on DMT after this process of annealing, cross-frequency coupling, sharing of symmetries, and sharing of projective logic. It very much feels like the fruit of a multi-scale fractal harmonization process. I haven't stabilized it — I'm not feeling it right now — but I do feel it every once in a while. And I find it fascinating: very much the fruit of psychonautic exploration, hopefully done right.

*(So, hopefully, feel inspired. I recommend listening to Bob Thurman — the father of Uma Thurman, from* Kill Bill[^thurman] *— reading the Avataṃsaka Sutra like a madman, completely possessed by visions of worlds within atoms within worlds within atoms, infinitely reflecting each other, with Buddha-lands as numerous as the dust particles in all the oceans of the world, and so on, and so on.)*

All right. With that: thank you so much, everybody. **Infinite bliss!** Take care. I'll see you and talk to you another time, on another topic. Ciao.
