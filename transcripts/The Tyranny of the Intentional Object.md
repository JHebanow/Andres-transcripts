## The qualia of the day: clean air

But first, the qualia of the day: clean air. Here I have an air-quality meter. It has various settings — it's pretty cool. It measures PM2.5 and PM10, and also HCHO (formaldehyde) and total volatile organic compounds.

These are really important. When it comes to interventions you could make to improve the quality of your life — life expectancy, long-term outcomes, cognition, and so on — air quality is really, really key. All sorts of studies demonstrate that whenever there's a pollution day, the number of heart attacks skyrockets. And whenever the California fires are raging — which apparently is going to happen every year from now on, potentially — if you don't have an air filter at home, a HEPA air filter, you're going to be breathing the equivalent of something like 15 to 20 cigarettes a day just by being in your house. All of the advice usually given to you — stay indoors and don't exercise — really doesn't cut it. It's just a drop in the ocean. I measured it without an air filter when the California fires were raging, and the difference between inside and outside was only something like 20%. With an air filter — or even better, with two air filters — you get rid of up to 98% of PM2.5.

And studies indicate that this is not only an irritant for your heart and your cardiovascular system, immediately increasing the chances of heart attacks and cardiovascular events; in the long term it accumulates and stays in your lungs, and that leads to lung cancer, emphysema, and all sorts of other complications. It also affects mood — how you feel in the moment — and it affects cognition: your ability to reason through possibilities, standard intelligence-test batteries, and so on.

But PM2.5 is really just scratching the surface. Formaldehyde and total volatile organic compounds are something I've only recently been trying to improve, because the PM2.5 was already pretty good. CO2 is something I measure as well. With some experimentation I found the right way of doing it: exactly how much to open the window, exactly how much to run the air filter, which room to be in, which doors should be open. Right now it's pretty optimized, and I'm really happy with the air quality in this space, which is where I work a lot of the time.

## The tyranny of the intentional object

All right, let's move on to the topic of the day: the tyranny of the intentional object. "Intentional object" here refers to a philosophy term, which unfortunately causes a lot of confusion. In philosophy, **intentionality** is the aboutness of thought, or the aboutness of experience: what the experience is telling you, the meaning of an experience. The **phenomenal character** of an experience, by contrast, is what it feels like — its texture, its structure, how it is composed and organized. Of course, there is perhaps some kind of duality between them in the general case. Not always: there are a lot of "meaningless" experiences that could be richly structured, and there are also hyper-meaningful experiences with relatively little structure. But in general, there's a tight coupling between them.

The tyranny of the intentional object refers to the idea that we think the purpose of life has to do with intentional objects: that what we truly want and care about, and to some extent enjoy — what makes life worth living, if it is worth living — is the meaning we get out of life.

Here I'm, of course, a meta-contrarian — or a meta-meta-contrarian, or something of that sort. It's kind of obvious to say that life is just better when things are nice and you feel happy and things are pleasant and enjoyable. But then people signal their intellectual sophistication by saying: no, what I care about is not pleasure or avoiding pain; what I care about is meaning, or something like that. And okay, fine: in practice, because of the pleasure paradox[^pleasure-paradox] — pursuing pleasure directly doesn't lead to long-term happiness — it is true that if you pursue meaning, you're probably going to have a better life. But that's pragmatic. If we want to talk about the fundamentals — the foundational understanding of where value actually comes from — I totally do not buy that. I'm sorry. But let's walk through why; I'm not just going to state it and leave you hanging.

## Sandy the dog

Let's start with an example — a pretty classic one, actually, which I talk about in the article ["The Tyranny of the Intentional Object."](https://qri.org/blog/tyranny-of-the-intentional-object) Imagine there is this very cute golden retriever. It's very friendly — your best friend, of course — and it's called Sandy. Sandy just happens to absolutely love sand and the beach, and the beach contains so much sand, of all different grades. Whenever Sandy goes to the beach, it's mind-blowingly amazing for Sandy. That's what gives meaning to Sandy's life. It's just way better than eating or hugs. Those things are good — Sandy likes friendship and exercise and so on — but the beach is on another level.

So, is that fine? Or do you have a moral concern with Sandy being very into the beach? It's not an obsession or anything; it's just a lot of enjoyment, plain delight, and it doesn't cause side effects. Sandy doesn't shake when it doesn't have the beach, and so on. It's a plus — the cherry on top of the cake of a life that is pretty good: a very, very wholesome dog life. Is there a problem with this? I think most people will say: no, actually, I would love it if my dog had as big a passion for something as Sandy has for the beach.

So that's the matter of fact when it comes to the structure of the experiences of your dog, Sandy. But let's think about what was actually going on.

Let me tell you that behind the scenes, so to speak, Sandy has had, since it was born, a tiny chip implanted in its brain — in its pleasure centers in particular — in such a way that those pleasure centers can be stimulated without tolerance: without the development of addiction, compulsion, withdrawal, and so on. It's a particular type of electromagnetic stimulation that just doesn't generate the side effects of drugs or other methods of stimulating the pleasure centers. And it's programmed so that whenever Sandy is close to the beach, it gets activated.

Of course, Sandy has absolutely no idea this is going on. It's completely and utterly clueless — how could it know, really? There's no way for Sandy to know. From its point of view, it just feels like the beach is intrinsically wonderful and profoundly meaningful.

So now, given this, is that okay? I've asked this question to a lot of people, and I would say that maybe about two-thirds recoil in moral disgust at the idea that you have messed with the reward architecture of your dog. But come on — you're actually improving the life of your dog. Now, there is truth in the point that you're to some extent messing with your dog's agency, because now it has to self-organize around the fact that the beach is the best part of its life. And sure, that is in some sense manipulative.

### Isn't evolution already coercive?

But — and here is the very, very, very, very big *but* that you absolutely have to consider — isn't evolution already being coercive like that? And isn't evolution actually being coercive in a far more cruel, and to some extent far more purposeless and awful, way? Evolution has wired us such that our pleasure centers and the mechanisms of valence become activated whenever we do things that improve our survival, our social status, or our probability of reproduction. That's super coercive. We're doing the bidding of our genes; we are gene servants,[^gene-servants] simply because it's what feels good.

Of course, there are a lot of corner cases. If you become addicted to cocaine or whatever — okay, sure, that has nothing to do with the typical reasons why your nervous system would become activated in a pleasurable way. Those are attempts to hack this reward architecture, often in pretty unwise ways that backfire. But the point is that I would almost argue, perhaps, that what this owner did to Sandy the dog is way more ethical than what evolution is doing to us. At least there is a modicum of control — some kind of adult in the room saying: all right, this kind of pleasure is wholesome, and it doesn't lead to horrible suffering and consequences. Evolution doesn't care. Whenever our egos are threatened, evolution makes us feel that we're literally dying. Whenever there's a very low probability that you will, I don't know, have high social status, it feels like you're literally dying inside. Isn't that awfully cruel? It's actually really messed up, once you recognize it. Anyway, that's the truth of it.

## The heroin-drip objection

A lot of people would then go on to say: you're being so reductionistic about what matters in life. The typical straw man you might hear is: you're just advocating wireheading, or all of us being hooked up to a heroin drip, or something like that — but life is way more important and rich than that: having kids and family, your career, purpose, and so on. Why would you just hook yourself up to a heroin drip?

Of course, they're right in a very fundamental sense, but you have to examine this very carefully. The heroin drip simply doesn't cut it. First of all, you develop tolerance really quickly — which is something that in principle we could eliminate with anti-tolerance drugs, better pharmaceuticals, and so on. Okay, sure, let's imagine there's no tolerance. Is it still bad? Most people would say yes, because you are not servicing other people, and meaning comes from servicing others. Well, okay. But if you analyze the reason why it's ethical to help others, it's ultimately because you're helping them avoid very unpleasant states of consciousness and facilitating their hyper-meaningful, high-valence states of consciousness. If we actually have that solved, what exactly does it mean to help somebody who is already in a peak state? Is that meaningful? I'm not very convinced.

Furthermore, heroin produces a relatively shallow state of consciousness — we'll come back to this as we unravel what meaning means.

### We are all opioid addicts

But the thing I want to mention about heroin and Sandy the dog in this context is that heroin addicts and meth addicts — not all of them; it really depends on the particular nervous system you're talking about, but very likely, it's a very common thing — will often lie, cheat, steal; they will do anything. They'll offer themselves for sex. They'll do anything to get their fix. And I would claim — earlier I was talking about how feeling that your social status is threatened feels like you're about to die. Yes: people will lie, steal, and cheat to prevent their sense of social status from plummeting entirely. In some sense it's equivalent. We are all heroin addicts;[^paid-in-junk] it's just that the particular type of "heroin" we're addicted to is endogenous opioids. Is it that different? Not necessarily. It's an IV drip that is modulated by, again, survival, status, reproduction, and so on. It's messed up. We are not free. Evolution did not wire us to be free.

### Dissonance, consonance, and resonance

Now, what happens when you're withdrawing from heroin or something like that? First, your biorhythms will be all messed up. Your heart rate will be really offbeat relative to your breathing, the rhythm of your stomach, and other biorhythms.[^heart-coherence] A lot of things basically become out of tune, and that produces profound dissonance. You can look up our symmetry theory of valence[^stv] and why dissonance is ultimately the signature of low valence. Basically, biorhythm dysregulation is a core reason why experiences feel really bad. Whether it is withdrawal from heroin or from one of these evolved things, approximately the same thing happens. It's really bad; it feels awful. Of course you want to relieve that — it doesn't make sense not to.

On the flip side: biorhythm dysregulation is the negative, so what is the positive? I would say it's some sort of consonance. In the realm of social interactions, we might try to operationalize this as **interpersonal resonance**. There's quite a bit of interesting discussion in the article "A Future for Neuroscience" by Mike Johnson about how people may be wired slightly differently — whether they're like metronomes or have entrainment mechanisms with other people.[^enq-mq] Not just any two people put together will resonate; it's actually very finicky, depending on personality and so on. But in the grand scheme of things, there is some kind of mathematical property that makes you enjoy being with others. And that, too, is similar to something a drug might give — maybe not heroin, but maybe something like MDMA. And we chase that a lot.

## What Sandy doesn't know

So that was Sandy the dog. The way I would summarize it in terms of the tyranny of the intentional object is this: Sandy believes that what it really loves is the beach in and of itself — that the beach contains the atoms of goodness, that it's holy, it's divine. But in reality, being at the beach triggers states of consciousness that are positive in valence, or very positive in valence, and that is not explicitly represented in Sandy's consciousness. And it is exactly the same with humans. Whenever meaningful states of consciousness happen — whenever meaningful operations occur — they often trigger high-valence states of consciousness, but that triggering process does not get explicitly represented.

That's one of the fascinating things: things can happen in your consciousness that do not get encoded as information in the future states of consciousness that represent the operations that happened before. So introspection is something that has to be learned and practiced in order to ultimately understand that this is what's going on. It doesn't come naturally; we weren't wired for that. Of course, becoming a Buddhist monk and meditating for 10,000 hours is the sort of thing that really cements that understanding. Then it's crystal clear that it's not that the cashier is slow and stupid — it's that you're irritated. That's the kind of insight you will get from a lot of meditation and genuine introspection.

## What is meaning?

This makes us wonder, then: what is meaning? Why do people think so much about meaning, and why do they confuse it with the source of goodness in and of itself?

There's a lot to be said about meaning. Classically, there's Frege and 20th-century philosophy of language, with the distinction between sense and reference.[^frege] The morning star and the evening star are the same planet: Venus. So it's confusing — the evening star and the morning star are different terms, but they're the same planet. What's going on there? There's some kind of weird equivalence. The solution, at least in that paradigm, is to distinguish sense from reference. The sense of "morning star" is different from the sense of "evening star," because each comes with a lot of contextual import about where you're pointing at the thing. But the referent is the same: physically and fundamentally, from these two different perspectives, contexts, or states of consciousness, the two terms reference the same ground truth, the same fundamental object.

### Felt sense

Of course, I don't think that cuts it. Meaning is far more interesting than that. Mike Johnson, again, would mention a third component: **felt sense**.[^felt-sense] Meaning is a felt sense. It's something you experience — not necessarily in an explicit, symbolic way. Sometimes you do experience it symbolically, but not always. It's a felt sense. So the same words have different associated felt senses in different cultures or subcultures. In fact, he would claim that a lot of disputes and arguments between people come about when the felt sense associated with particular words or concepts differs between subcultures. And war between subcultures unfortunately involves trying to redefine the meaning of terms, because those terms are really key to each subculture's particular semantic web. That's nasty; it's very unfortunate. We don't want war. So hopefully there are ways of getting around it — and of recognizing that, ultimately, we all want the same thing, which is states of high valence. We probably shouldn't be obsessing over meaning, because that's going to lead to that kind of problem.

### Grounding meaning in qualia

I will also mention Quine, another 20th-century philosopher of language, who had the idea that meaning is fully contextual: the structure of the semantic web is what gives rise to the meaning of a particular term. So if the semantic web changes — maybe gradually, but surely — any given term could eventually mean something different. He would even say that "2 + 2 = 4" could eventually become false in a different web of semantic content, because the structure of that web just doesn't make it a true statement.[^quine] Which is really crazy to consider.

But that neglects felt sense, because felt sense will turn out to be, in some sense, objective. In the same way as the rest mass of the electron is an objective feature of the world, your felt sense — your state of consciousness, the qualia triggered by a particular term — is also an objective feature of the world. So I am far more interested in cataloging felt senses than in being, I guess, semantically nihilistic and saying that there are only semantic networks and nothing truly means anything in the ground truth. No, no, no. Let's ground meaning in qualia, and then things actually make sense. There's actually a ground truth about what something "means" subjectively.

There's an article by Daniel Dennett, "Quining Qualia,"[^quining-qualia] which tries to show that qualia don't satisfy a lot of the criteria for being something real: that qualia just don't quite fit the scientific world picture and the semantic network that is part of consensus reality, and that we should just accept that and disregard them. I would flip that around entirely — turn it 180 degrees, put it on its head: rather than quining qualia, ground meaning in qualia. Then all of a sudden you realize that yes, meaning was a property of the universe all along: meaning is states of consciousness, felt senses. Those felt senses are what meaning is made of.

### Physical units for meaning

That in and of itself doesn't go that far, though. We have to dissect why a felt sense would mean something, and when a felt sense would not mean something. There's an interesting post by Max Hodak from Neuralink — pretty cool; I'll add a link in the description — in which he tries to identify what meaning is.[^hodak] The really cool thing is that he's trying to find physical units for it. His theory ends up being about the extent to which a particular idea or interaction changes the expected future valence of an agent, and his units are something like energy times time over information, which amounts to something like action per bit. So there are some actions that will drastically modify valence, and in that sense those might be really meaningful, and others might be very meaningless in that sense.

I think that's the right spirit. Yes, let us find the physical units for meaning. It sounds absurd, but because of qualia formalism I honestly 100% believe there's going to be some sort of physical units for meaning. And of course, a theory of everything should also explain meaning in mathematical terms.

### Meaning in a single moment of experience

What I have to say about that is that I totally agree meaning is very connected with valence. The thing is that states of consciousness do not exist over time. In some sense you have snapshots of experience — discrete moments of experience strung together by the brain — but they're their own separate beings. In empty individualism, each of those time slices is its own consciousness, its own being. In closed individualism, there's some mysterious soul that connects them together. And in open individualism, we're all just consciousness: you're actually all of those moments of experience, but you're also every other moment of experience too.[^individualism] The point is that you cannot really meaningfully integrate meaning across time, because you're talking about many different moments of experience. So, just as we did with time — phenomenal time; you should look up my video on what time is, and on exotic time[^phenomenal-time] — the answer is going to be to look at the individual moment of experience, examine the structure of that moment of experience, and identify the mathematical features that encode meaning in it.

So what could those features be? There are several hints. First of all, **intention** matters a lot for meaning. I would also claim that meaningful components of your experience have a lot of potential to effect change — and more specifically, a lot of potential to effect change of the valence kind. In other words, very meaningful things in your consciousness are bundled-up potentials to change how good or bad you feel.

If we talk about something out there that nobody experiences, that is gray, has no flavor, and will never interact with anything or anybody, most people would probably consider it meaningless — unless they get a kick out of thinking about it, in which case it would be meaningful because of the kick they get out of thinking about it. But intrinsically, they would think of it as meaningless. I think that would be a corner case. Compare that with being told: hey, this is the cup of eternal love; when you receive it, you will be accepting the universal love of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara and Jesus and Krishna, all bundled together, and you're going to become a transcendent infinihedron of omniscience, or something like that. Well, hell yeah — that's going to be a very meaningful thing.

In between, you have things such as the concept of a hug. That's an operator on your experience that actually does warm it up: it increases its hedonic tone, it has particular valence effects, and to that extent it's very meaningful. Or college graduation: when I was studying the most pleasant experiences people have in their lives, a certain percentage of people said college graduation was among their top three most pleasant experiences.[^log-scales] No doubt that, given our culture, a lot of people consider it a very pivotal moment, one that transforms your sense of self, your relationship with others, your career prospects, etc. So it's a very central node in your semantic web, with a lot of valence effects — valence implications.

### Attention field lines

Moreover, let's look at the structure of the experience. There's this thing called the **attention field lines**. I think I mentioned them in my video about 5-MeO-DMT,[^5meo-video] where I talked about how, through the annealing process, the attention field lines become parallel, which corresponds to a super-high-valence state of consciousness but also dissolves internal boundaries. In some sense, a 5-MeO-DMT state of consciousness — a peak state of consciousness — means everything and nothing at the same time: because all the attention field lines are parallel, no information can be encoded. So what does it mean? It just means *this* — "this is it," suchness, something of that sort. The attention field lines of a typical moment of experience, by contrast, are going to be pretty detailed: they'll have curl and divergence and a lot of other mathematical properties. My sense is that the syntax and grammar of meaning in our consciousness actually involve a lot of manipulating these little curls, vortices, and convergence points in the attention field lines. And when something becomes deeply meaningful in your experience, that is often correlated with — or in some sense intrinsically a part of — a lot of your attention field lines concentrating on that phenomenal object.

It's crazy to think that introducing meaningful concepts into your consciousness changes the topology and shape of your attention field lines. Of course, if you're getting married or something, hopefully you're not getting distracted by, I don't know, the pink dress on the side or the bird over there. Hopefully all the attention field lines are converging on this operation on your sense of self and your sense of community that is going on. In that sense it's richly meaningful, because all of these attention field lines are converging on this phenomenal object — which is not necessarily a perceptual object; it's more of a conceptual object, but it still has a geometry. So yes, I think meaning is intimately connected with that.

## Meaning abuse

Another thing to mention is that precisely that ideology — "let's pursue meaning rather than pleasure" — I think can absolutely be overdone, and people overdo it all the time. Just as there is legitimately something like drug abuse — staying up for 13 days on meth, or gambling — okay, sure, that's drug abuse; let's use that term. It's awful; you shouldn't do that. It's going to hurt you; it's not good for your nervous system whatsoever. Likewise, I would claim there is **meaning abuse**. You organize your life around a particular source of meaning, and the empirical information challenges it. *(Love ginger juice.)* Oftentimes you will then find yourself scraping the bottom of the meaning barrel, trying to make sense of it all, clinging to a sense of meaning even though sometimes, patently, all the evidence seems to go against it actually corresponding to anything real.

I definitely don't want to give concrete examples, so as not to offend people — I don't want to make enemies. But I would say that things such as tribal affiliations tend to be very charged with it. Also the stories that people tell — religions, absolutely, as well. There's meaning abuse in religions. When you're experiencing a kidney stone or something like that, and you're suffering, and somebody says you should just pray to Jesus — no, you shouldn't just do that. Sure, do it, but you should do something else too: get some painkillers, some hard opioids, because this is just so awful.

Meaning abuse happens all the time. Mother Teresa, for example — she clearly had access to a lot of fascinating states of consciousness — would deny painkillers to dying patients, and she would say awful things, such as that experiencing intense suffering and pain is sharing the cross of Jesus, that it's like kissing God, or something like that.[^mother-teresa] No — that's psychopathic meaning abuse. It's the same thing as the heroin addict lying and stealing and cheating in order to get their fix. It's awful; you shouldn't do that. In other words, you should recognize the limits of meaning's capacity to improve your valence, your happiness, and your well-being.

### Nihilism, eternalism, and meaningness

David Chapman makes a really good case about this in his online book *Meaningness*, which is also in the description. He describes two extremes: nihilism and eternalism. Eternalism not in the sense of the philosophy of time, but in its association with meaning: in eternalism there is a universal principle that gives meaning to life — God, or justice, or whatever — but there's one, it's eternal, and everything has to circle around it. That has a whole host of problems; meaning abuse, as I said, is one example. At the other extreme you have nihilism, where you just think there's no meaning. That gives you some good things, but it also ends up being really, really bad in other ways. So instead, he urges you to think of meaning as a gradient, and actually as a phenomenal quality.[^meaningness] I would connect it with the felt sense of meaning: you realize that some things are more or less meaningful, and that pursuing meaning in some contexts and particular situations is actually very enriching. I would 100% endorse that message. Absolutely.

But you shouldn't try to extend meaning to places where it's not going to do much. Again: kidney stones. Don't blame them on your karma or whatever. Just get ultrasound therapy and break those stones, or go on a roller coaster and get them through in an efficient way,[^roller-coaster] or whatever. Have a good diet that prevents the formation of kidney stones. That's the sort of straightforward, obvious advice. Just don't rely on meaning for that sort of thing. And also, don't abuse meaning too much — the equivalent of staying up for seven days smoking your meaning pipe, expecting that to generate really positive outcomes in the long term.

## Would constant happiness be meaningless?

I mentioned I was going to get back to the heroin reply. A very important version of it is the idea that if we find a way to just be happy all the time, wouldn't life be meaningless? I think there's, obviously, an ignorance here about the intrinsic connection between high valence and the hyper-meaningful. Very few people say, "I'm experiencing intense hyper-dimensional bliss, but my life feels meaningless." That's not really a complaint you hear about. What you often hear is, "I'm deeply depressed and I feel life is meaningless." As David Pearce would say: fix happiness, and the meaning of life will take care of itself.[^pearce-happiness]

### A reward architecture that hides itself

An important part of this is status. It feels bad to think we are pursuing pleasure. We would like to portray ourselves as very altruistic and self-sacrificing beings. Of course, there's some of that, and it needs explaining — that's important. Self-sacrificing love and selfless love are a property of the universe, and ideally we will eventually figure out how to harness them for good. But on a day-to-day basis, a lot of people's motivations are not like that, and we have to accept that — accept people for who they are, and embrace the fact that we are not pursuing universal love all the time. Hopefully we can make it work without admonishing people, guilt trips, and so on.

But the point is that our reward architecture is such a bully. Taking the philosophical steps to come to terms with the fact that you're always pursuing higher states of valence — that realization feels bad, and therefore people don't embrace it. But don't you see the irony here? The realization feels bad, and for that reason they don't pursue it. In other words, they're still obviously just pursuing higher valence. Your reward architecture doesn't want to know itself — or, one way of putting it, doesn't want *you* to know it. It has these shields around it. And to some extent, I would say, that's why consciousness research is so underdeveloped relative to something like quantum mechanics or chemistry: you need to overcome a bunch of self-insight barriers before you can look at the thing directly without much of a problem and just be self-honest about it. I think that's super important and grossly underrated. People get really agitated whenever you suggest that all they're actually pursuing is high valence. But again, that agitation, and the response to it, is itself more evidence in that direction, as it turns out.

## High-dimensional pleasures

The other thing is that there is absolutely a misconception about pleasure and pain. People would say heroin is a very shallow kind of well-being, as opposed to doing something wonderful for others and being respected, or proving a theorem that has been unsolved for hundreds of years, and so on. And I would say: yes, you're right. So let's look at what's going on. There's something about the **dimensionality** of the experience. We have some discussions and writings about what it means to have a high-dimensional versus a low-dimensional experience. Fundamentally, it has to do with correlated degrees of freedom, and with what we call virtual spaces and virtual dimensions. Just for a quick example, DMT absolutely does increase the dimensionality of your experience, no doubt. You can actually experience things that have four or five spatial dimensions, implicitly, in terms of the network of correlations. It's incredibly crazy.

And the truth is that a lot of pleasures that people describe as shallow are indeed low-dimensional. There is absolutely a distinction between something like MDMA and an opioid — a palliative. MDMA is going to be far higher-dimensional. The absolute valence may not be different, but the impact it will have on the organism, and how it's going to modify its self-organization, absolutely depends on the dimensionality. In that sense, higher dimensions are better for pleasure, for the most part. Given the choice, you should generally go for the higher-dimensional one, even if its absolute value is not that good relative to a lower-dimensional one, because it's going to be better for your nervous system, and it's also going to connect with far more things in your nervous system.

### Mystical experiences

I'm going to connect this with mystical experience. In some sense, people talk about mystical experiences as transcending our meaning structures. But at the same time, people talk about things such as: Jesus came in front of me and said that it's all about love — and that was the peak experience of my life. Here there's a duality. I would claim that, if you introspect and pay attention — especially if you've trained and have practice doing this — when Jesus, or Buddha, or Krishna, or whoever, is telling you that everything is made of love or is for the purpose of love, introspect on the quality of the phenomenal space and time you're in. I would claim it's actually a smooth, regular space, for the most part, with a lot of symmetry signatures: wallpaper symmetry groups, space groups, or hyperbolic symmetries. All of that is a signature of symmetry, and therefore a signature of high valence. So I would say that if you're having one of those mystical experiences, it's actually a super-high-dimensional, very high-energy, very high-valence state of consciousness. And yes, those types of experiences are what ultra-meaningful stuff is made of. They're what we use as the nodal points for what matters in life.

That's why meditation, psychedelics, and peak religious experiences absolutely modify how you conceive of what matters. Right after smoking 5-MeO-DMT, it's not only "Oh my gosh, that was incredible" — or awful, if you have bad luck or a bad set and setting. Let's say it was incredible: you say, "Oh my gosh, that was incredible," and it changes everything. Why? Because it was such a high-dimensional, high-valence state of consciousness that it becomes a space around which the entirety of your nervous system can self-organize. The higher the dimensionality, the more room there is for more of your metronomes and sub-networks to come together and coexist simultaneously. 5-MeO-DMT is basically of such high dimensionality that it allows everything to happen at once: the experience of the clear white light, or perfect silence, or ultimate peace, and so on. That's a hyper-ultra-symmetrical, high-valence, high-dimensional experience. And absolutely, it is better than plain heroin. So yes, there's absolutely something very real about high and low pleasures. I'm not going to deny that.

### Wireheading done right

But then, what is the problem with figuring out how to optimize our states of consciousness so that we have that as something we can rely on? So that, rather than going to sleep, maybe you experience eight hours of transcendental ecstasy with no tolerance and no side effects. Is there a problem with that? I don't think so — as long as it functions, and doesn't make you dysfunctional or unethical. And most likely it's not going to make you more unethical, because it tends to generate hyper-compassion and identification with consciousness as a whole; it makes you motivated to help others, and so on. It's probably just going to be a massive net positive. So there you go: yes, wireheading done right actually entails using hyper-meaningful states of consciousness — except that the reason they're good is that they're high-valence. It's a roundabout way, but ultimately I do think it matters a lot.

## Sustainability and collective lift-off

I think I'll wrap up with this. I agree that just high valence all the time is not going to cut it. We actually need to plan this carefully and understand how it can allow you to remain functional, responsible, and so on, so that you're not taken over by entities that actually suffer because it's evolutionarily advantageous that they suffer. No, no, no. We want to generate replicators that are aligned with the interests of consciousness. That is the way in which consciousness will win. It's not going to win by you in particular exiting the matrix by taking a bunch of 5-MeO-DMT, or DPT, or whatever might be the best drug the high-dimensional kids will be taking next decade. No, no, no. The point is a collective lift-off. And to do that, you need sustainability and functionality. You need to be able to give to the economy — to give things that are not only useful for others, but that also move others toward things that are aligned with consciousness. This is a very tricky problem, very high-dimensional, but I'm pretty certain that things that just keep you on your couch all day long for the rest of your life are not going to cut it.

## The three inspiration devas

To get there, here's a framework — just one piece of a bigger puzzle, but it stands out on its own. I call them the **inspiration devas**: things I personally derive a lot of inspiration and meaning from, and that I think are very wholesome. There are three.

The first is **loving-kindness**. Seriously — doing loving-kindness meditation every day. At the beginning you will probably feel silly. You might have memories of a middle-school bully telling you that you're very feminine, or whatever, for being so loving or carefree. That's in the past; stop bullying yourself. Honestly, practicing these states of consciousness — self-love and love toward all sentient beings — oh my gosh, it's so valuable: so good for your mental health, and so good for others too, for how you're going to interact with others and care about their well-being. And at a societal level, I expect almost nothing but benefits from increasing the amount of loving-kindness meditation in the world. So that's very good. I highly, highly recommend and endorse it.

The second is being **cognizant of human nature**, because love is not going to be enough. The reason has to do with game theory: if you don't take into account what the animal human drives actually are, eventually the tank will be empty, so to speak. Maybe your prefrontal cortex — your "higher self" — will say: yes, this is the aligned path. But then your animal self will say: uh-uh, I'm not going to do that for free anymore; I've got to be paid. So we have to integrate that, not look away, and not pretend that we're angels or anything like that. Basically, we need a seamless integration of incentive alignment in the animal world — in the animal self — and access to unconditional love in this more refined, higher-self kind of world. I think that is where the good stuff is going to come from. Maybe not in the far future, but definitely in our times, I think that's the place to push.

And finally, something I've called **rainbow god**, or also **rainbow consciousness**: the desire to know the state space of consciousness — to understand it all. I don't endorse that on its own, because I do think there are some understandings of consciousness that are probably just harmful. Knowing what something super painful is like — you may not benefit very much from that, honestly. It's just self-torture. But if you're very curious, you may do it nonetheless — like eating a Carolina Reaper pepper or something. My suggestion is: don't do it. There's an opportunity cost: you could be exploring interesting smells in that time, rather than suffering on your couch experiencing the million Scoville whatever. Personally, I don't have much of a compulsion to know the awful parts of the state space of consciousness. No, no, no. But I do have a heck of a curiosity about the entire state space of consciousness. I'm curious about whatever trillion possible scent combinations there are, and about multimodal experiences. I'm very curious about what DMT states of consciousness are capable of. What is it like to combine salvia and MDMA? I've never done it, and I'll probably never do it, but I'm very curious what it's like. And the truth is that the state space of consciousness is so ridiculously massive that this is not only a thousand-year enterprise; it's probably a million-plus-year enterprise.

### Where the nectar is

And I think the nectar is in the Venn diagram — in the combination of these three: organizations, people, and cultures that recognize the paramount importance of loving-kindness and of caring about all sentient beings, together with a recognition of the animal self and how to align incentives — and also, literally, of what makes you healthy as a human. Think of yourself: for the most part, if you were a monkey eating junk food, taking drugs, and staring at a screen 14 hours a day, somebody would call animal protection services. We don't tend to realize that we are monkeys too, and we do that to ourselves. So also take into account our evolutionary constraints, and so on. And maintain an open line of communication with rainbow consciousness — or at least a tamed, ethical version of rainbow consciousness. That, I think, is where the three inspiration devas — divine beings, or energies — are really wholesome and beautiful, and very good for all of us. I would love it if more people cultivated them. I think it's just very good.

So with that, thank you so much. This was fun, and I look forward to seeing you next time. Have a wonderful evening, or day, or whatever time or climate or state of consciousness you find yourself in. May it be wonderful.
