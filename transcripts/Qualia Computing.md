## Why qualia computing: the substrate matters

So let's get on to the topic of the day, which is qualia computing. We already have the concept of computing; why do we need qualia computing? I would say it's because part of the claim here is that the substrate actually matters.

In classical computing theory we have the **Church–Turing thesis**, which essentially states that a function on the natural numbers (although you can generalize it) is computable if and only if it can be implemented with a Turing machine. You hear a lot of people invoke the Church–Turing thesis in philosophy of mind: we don't know exactly what the brain is doing, but because it's clearly some kind of computable function — we're not talking about uncomputable things; let's be realistic — it must on some level be something that we can model with a Turing machine.

As far as in-principle reasoning goes, I think this sort of holds. But we have to be really careful not to throw the baby out with the bathwater, because it presupposes a whole ontology for what the basis of computation is: what computation is for, what it is about. If you dissect something like the Church–Turing thesis, you will come to understand that its conception of computation is one where what you have is inputs and outputs, and a particular computation is a transformation of the input into the output.

Reality is a little bit more complicated and interesting than that, because you also have intermediate states in the transformation from input to output, and a lot of interesting things can happen there. In particular, the implementation can give rise to computational speedups. If you're using a particular quantum computing algorithm, for example, in principle you might be able to translate it into something that a Turing machine would be able to compute. However, you will not be able to do that with the same runtime complexity. In other words, the particular quantum substrate you're utilizing for one of these algorithms allows you to solve the problem with a runtime complexity different from what you would get with a Turing machine. Of course, you can always compensate for this by scaling down the speed of the simple computations and speeding up the computation of the more complex ones, so that you emulate the outward behavior of a physical system that isn't a classical Turing machine. But if you do that, you are masking what is actually interesting about the system, which is that it was allowing you to do those computational speedups.

## An enriched conception of intelligence

Taking a step back to figure out what the nervous system actually uses consciousness for, I will have to ask you to redefine your very conception of intelligence. What is intelligence, and what kind of problem is consciousness solving for which genuine intelligence is required? Here an interesting perspective comes from David Pearce in his essay "The Biointelligence Explosion,"[^biointelligence] which I highly recommend reading. One of its main overarching claims is that intelligence is a function of one's whole phenomenal world-simulation, and not only a property of the sequential, logico-linguistic serial processing that runs on top of the massively parallel world-simulation implemented in the nervous system.

It's very crazy to think about, but the way in which you render objects — the things around you, the shapes, the colors, the vibe, the mood you're in — all of those things contribute to the information processing your nervous system is doing. Being in a particular mood is computationally very significant. It's not an epiphenomenon; it's not trivial. There's a reason why moods were selected by natural selection: usually they're adaptive to the particular situations we are in. That's not always the case, but at least from an evolutionary standpoint, that's why they were recruited.

So there are quite a few ways in which we can enrich our conception of intelligence beyond serial logico-linguistic reasoning, which is the classic conception of intelligence. It's obviously really important and obviously correlated with a lot of really important things, but it is by no means the whole account. If that is your only account of intelligence, you will be deeply misled about what the nervous system can do.

Intelligence involves several things:

1. **Binding**, both local and global. Intelligence requires the ability to generate a world-simulation in which features are properly bound to each other, so that a particular color belongs to the shape it's associated with in the environment and you don't perceive disembodied colors, for example. **Local binding** is features put together into a unified phenomenal object; **global binding** is the features belonging to the same experience, whether or not they're immediately connected to a phenomenal object. Either way, binding is both philosophically very puzzling and computationally very significant, so non-psychotic binding is a key ingredient of intelligence.

2. **A self**, as a coordinating mechanism: something that allows you to model what is happening as happening to somebody over time, with agency and the capacity to organize its contractions and expansions in such a way that it navigates the virtual environment. You could be really good at non-psychotic binding but lack an adaptive sense of self — for example, with schizophrenia, or perhaps some kind of psychedelic psychosis.

3. **Perspective-taking capacity.** This is an intrinsically challenging problem: how do you simulate what others are experiencing, what they believe you are experiencing, and so on? In the case of somebody like Shakespeare, that's sixth-order intentionality: what does he think that she believes that he wants that she dislikes? That kind of reasoning is something upon which the properties of a world-simulation are predicated, and it is integral to intelligence.

4. **A metric to distinguish the trivial from the significant.** Somebody like Kim Peek has tremendous computational power when it comes to calendrical questions or arithmetic, but that's not the same as a Perelman, somebody who can prove the Poincaré conjecture. That requires some kind of understanding to distinguish the trivial from the significant, and it's not only an IQ-type skill: it requires some deep heuristics and, ultimately, some philosophical insight into what matters in reality. So that is another key component of intelligence.

5. **The ability to navigate and utilize different states of consciousness.** You could have somebody who's really good at math but whose life is destroyed by a particular bad mood; that person would not be very intelligent when it comes to navigating mood states of consciousness. Let alone DMT, with its extreme changes in the state of consciousness and the computational properties those entail. Being able to navigate that is something intelligence is, to some extent, related to. I suspect that the superintelligences of the future will start out looking more like a Super-Shulgin Academy[^super-shulgin] than like a Super-Watson or a Super-GPT-3.

6. Finally, **serial logico-linguistic reasoning**, which is essential. It's obviously key in order to create digital computers, to reason philosophically, and to do altruism at scale: this systematizing type of intelligence. But it's important to recognize that it is just one element among many other kinds of intelligence that are really non-trivial.

One of the things David Pearce points out is that AI researchers may confuse empathy and people's perspective-taking capacity with the personality factor of agreeableness. Whereas, actually, high levels of agreeableness are computationally very demanding: doing perspective-taking properly is very, very computationally demanding. It's not just a personality trait. That's something that is easy to get really wrong, and it's an unsolved problem as far as I'm concerned.

## Intelligence as choosing the right self-organizing principle

So we've enriched our conception of computation, and we've also enriched our conception of intelligence. What's next? Next is the overarching claim, or observation, that to a large extent intelligence is all about choosing the right **self-organizing principle** to solve the problem for you.

There's a wonderful recent paper[^repulsive-curves] showing that if you take a tangled knot — in a cord, say — put it in a graphics engine, and introduce a rule whereby the surfaces repel each other as much as possible, then even a very gnarly knot will disentangle on its own and become just the cord. Is that intelligent or not? The intelligence there lies in selecting the right self-organizing principle — in this case, repulsion between surfaces — that will, as an emergent effect of letting it run in a massively parallel fashion, solve the problem for you. I would claim that this is much more similar to what we do on a daily basis than, say, programming a Turing machine.

So the claim is that, yes, we do have some specialized hardware to some extent, but we also have the capacity to program self-organizing principles that then solve problems for us, and there are a lot of examples of this. The tricky part — the meta-intelligence behind it — is the capacity to train new self-organizing principles to solve new problems. I would say it's a completely fascinating property of our nervous system that we're capable of reprogramming it so that it instantiates new self-organizing principles.

### Annealing

One self-organizing principle that is all over the place, and that we've talked about before, is **annealing**. Take the problem of getting atoms to align with one another in a lattice without having to go through each atom individually and orient it so that everything becomes a checkerboard pattern, or is otherwise properly aligned. You don't want to do that manually. Is there something you can do to the system so that it finds that configuration on its own? The answer is yes. You can heat it up on a certain schedule and then let it cool in such a way that you get islands of constraint satisfaction: bundles of atoms that do satisfy the constraint of being aligned with each other. These form nucleation points that propagate and then negotiate with each other.

In some cases an annealing cycle that heats up, cools down, heats up, and cools down again, going through several stages, might work really well: the islands of coherence congeal and crystallize, then you reheat a little so that they jiggle, and then they crystallize as a whole, so that the entire scope of the crystal lattice becomes well organized. In this case, choosing the right annealing schedule is the part that requires intelligence, whereas the actual organization happens at a physical level: you're recruiting the massively parallel nature of the universe to solve the computational problem for you.

### Optical Ising machines

There are some really exciting new applications in computing that do this. In particular, there's optics research at Caltech[^ising] that uses optics to solve what's called the **Ising problem**. It's very similar to the annealing problem of atoms wanting to be aligned with their environment, except that it's a much more general situation: you have nodes in a network — not necessarily a lattice; it could be an arbitrary network — and each node is trying to be in the same alignment as the nodes it's connected to, or the opposite; there are several formulations. The point is that the more alignment you have, the lower the energy of the entire network. If you do this with optics, you can explore states of superposition — many possibilities at once — which drastically accelerates the runtime complexity of the algorithm. So you end up with something that, at a physical level, recruits the massively parallel nature of the universe to find the local or global energy minima for you, and does so faster than classical computing.

When you have something like that, the problem is no longer how to solve this constraint satisfaction problem. The problem becomes: how do I take the problem I have and cast it as an Ising problem? Because then you have the specialized hardware — a physical system whose self-organizing principles will solve the Ising problem for you. The non-trivial problem becomes how to map your problem onto an Ising problem.

I think this will show up all over the place when it comes to human intelligence. We have a range of possible self-organizing principles that we're really good at instantiating, and the thing that is learned — the really tricky part — is figuring out how to translate the problem you have into a format that one of these self-organizing principles can solve for you. I think we will ultimately be hashing these out one after another: how we solve this particular social cognition problem, this perceptual problem, this homeostasis problem. I suspect that in all of those scenarios we will find some self-organizing principle doing the computational legwork for you.

Isn't it fascinating what it actually feels like to solve a very difficult problem? There's definitely an embodied component, but it feels like a massively parallel process in which you implicitly explore a massive space very efficiently, without explicitly laying out all the possibilities. You do it in this very crazy, massively parallel way.

## Minimizing dissonance: a unified paradigm

Also importantly, there are two things your nervous system is constantly trying to do. This comes from Karl Friston's **free energy principle**,[^fep] but we are blending it with Selen Atasoy's **connectome-specific harmonic waves**, and then adding **neural annealing**[^neural-annealing] and the **symmetry theory of valence**[^stv] to create a unified paradigm. What I mean is this: the free energy principle indicates that an organism needs to simultaneously (a) minimize prediction errors and (b) minimize the complexity of its model of its environment. In principle you could have a huge lookup table that predicts every possible new sequence of inputs as a function of the sequence of past inputs, but that is computationally intractable; it's impossible to actually train all of that up — you would need an infinite, or at least a very large, amount of computation. So in practice you need a balance between how complex your internal model is and how good your predictions of the environment are using that model.

The claim we make at QRI is that what the nervous system is truly trying to minimize is dissonance: dissonance between its harmonic modes. What does that look like? It looks like harmonic modes that are similar to each other but not actually capable of interlocking. They generate strong beat patterns, which create very sharp derivatives — very sharp transitions — which are a risk factor for **strain**. Strain is the thing that actually feels unpleasant: you are this field, and whenever that field experiences strain, that feels unpleasant. If you want to minimize strain, you have to minimize the risk factors for strain, which is stress, for the most part. And if you want to minimize stress, you want the harmonic modes to interlock with one another, so that the energy one harmonic has can be passed on seamlessly to the other without friction or stress in between. As a consequence, from the point of view of valence, all you are ever trying to do is minimize the amount of dissonance you're experiencing.

There are two key ways in which dissonance arises. First, you have bad predictions. Why? Because a prediction error is a lack of coherence between one layer of the perceptual system and the surrounding one. You have a nested hierarchy of layers, each trying to predict the one around it, so a prediction error is dissonance between one layer and its surrounding ones — as when an abstract model is not able to predict very well the next input of the more concrete neural layer that's connected to the environment. Second, you're also trying to minimize model complexity, because very complex models can have a lot of moving parts that can grind on each other. Or you can look at it projectively: when you have a very complex model and you project it from several points of view, the different projections can generate internal dissonance, and that's something you're trying to minimize as well. So in practice, whatever minimizes the total amount of dissonance is going to satisfy a lot of desirable machine-learning, or statistical, properties: it will balance the accuracy of predictions against internal model complexity.

## Sensing waves and points of view

When we update our internal world-simulation — and I want you to notice this next time you're tripping or meditating — in order to keep hallucinating, say, a sphere in your visual field with your eyes closed, you need to hit it with what feels like some kind of **sensing wave**. You might describe it as a wave of energy, or as ethereal sensing; it feels very, very mysterious. But I think this is the holistic field behavior of your experience, and ultimately it will be something we can understand scientifically. To give you a sense of it, it feels like ray marching in graphics: you have all of these rays of light being propagated, and they need to hit the sphere in order to energize it. You need to do this from many points of view, from many directions, so that you narrow down the actual position of the sphere. If you hit it from only one point of view, you may narrow down its position or momentum along one direction while maintaining a lot of uncertainty along the other axes. So hitting it from as many points of view as possible, at once or in short sequence, narrows down its position, and that is essential.

When you do that, I want you to notice that every time you hit the sphere with one of these waves of energy, there's a sort of oscillatory condition, in which the energy bounces back from where it hits the sphere. That forms a point of view — a point of view you're embedding in your experiential space, which defines a piece of information about the location of that sphere. In aggregate, all of these partial views build up to high confidence that there is a sphere in a particular location. But the essential artifact here is that you're going to get a coupling: every single time you generate, quote-unquote, a point of view, you're also going to generate a harmonic resonant mode. And when something is being seen from many points of view at once, it will be stitching together a lot of other regions of your experience.

This is mind-blowing, but when you have a highly coordinated experience — for example an orgasm, a meditation peak, a 5-MeO-DMT state, or, let's say, vomiting (vomiting is actually a highly coordinated action) — in any of those situations you have a central happening: a central set of contractions that are very precisely timed, so that the particular behavior required of them actually happens. And the rest of your organism is constantly sensing, bouncing these waves of energy off of them, synchronizing and synchronizing and becoming coherent with them, so that it becomes their oscillatory complement.

That is the problem we are always trying to solve. As I said, when you get absorbed into a hexagon deep in meditation, you're becoming the oscillatory complement of the hexagon. So to predict a hexagon perfectly, what the nervous system is figuring out is how to oscillate in such a way that its complement becomes the hexagon. You're solving the **oscillatory complement problem** all the time. Say you see somebody in the circus doing some amazing gymnastics, and you're thinking: how do I interface with that person? How do I play with them? You're simulating how that person behaves as a collection of metronomes, and you're figuring out how you can be the oscillatory complement of at least an act, or a sequence, of the movements that person is making. So solving the oscillatory complement problem is one of the things that benefits from instantiating a massively parallel self-organizing principle. I believe it's key to absolutely everything we do: we're constantly computing the oscillatory complements of inputs, of actions, and even of agents.

## Qualia critters and inner world-building

More on that in another video. To wrap up a little: as I was saying, you can introspect on how **oscillatory complement stacking** is the foundation of inner world-building. You can experience this on various substances, in meditation, or even just with your imagination, if you don't have aphantasia. You can even hallucinate what I call **qualia critters**: quasi-agents, little mini-robots — but conscious in a way, or rather, you're making them conscious — that recruit particular ways of contracting, particular oscillatory modes of your experience, in a coordinated fashion, so that they can perform a certain action repeatedly. Then you can create swarms of them; you can copy them. As soon as you have a swarm of qualia critters, you start to get the resonant modes of the entire collection, and that itself is computationally very significant.

When you simulate how a colony behaves in your mind, you're also solving this crazy oscillatory complement problem, except that it's for every one of those critters relative to the rest of the collective: you're finding an attractor in which each qualia critter is the oscillatory complement of the entire colony. That's a tremendously computationally demanding problem, but it gets solved really quickly. If you take DMT, within a minute or two you will solve very, very complicated constraint satisfaction problems in this massively parallel way, generating crazy qualia critters that are finding their oscillatory complement. I think all of this runs so efficiently in our experience precisely because we're hooked up to the fields of physics, and we're using the massive native parallelism of reality itself, and how it unfolds over time, to solve these other computational problems.

## DMT as an energizer

I'll end with two more things. First, we can reinterpret what happens on many different substances in terms of tweaking the self-organizing principles of your experience, so that the balance of forces ends up different from what it was before. In particular, I think of DMT quite a lot as a massive energizer: it energizes the entirety of your experience, which generates crazy **non-linearities** — standing-wave patterns that are self-reinforcing — with a bias toward the high frequencies. A lot of what happens on DMT is very fast flickering, very fast resonant modes of your nervous system, which as a consequence generate waves of energy that collide against each other. Then you get attractor states: crazy geometrical patterns that are the energy sinks of that state. If you study them, you will see a lot of physical behavior, in the sense that the shape of these objects corresponds to an energy minimization process. On DMT, for example, you experience a lot of hyperbolic geometry[^hyperbolic] and a lot of minimal surfaces, all very reminiscent of energy minimization in physics.

Similarly to how we now have hardware from Caltech that can solve the Ising problem with optics — so that the problem becomes how to translate the problems we care about into an Ising problem — I would say the nervous system has a crazy capacity to generate insane non-linearities in its experiential field and then find their local energy minima. If you're able to cast a difficult computational problem as an energy minimization problem of that sort, you may be able to use the visual field for crazy computation. That's one of the things I'm very excited about for the next decade or so: figuring out ways in which a conscious visual field, or any other experiential field, can be recruited for very exotic computation, in a way that is exceedingly efficient and that nobody suspected before, because people are not thinking in terms of these enriched conceptions of intelligence and of computation. I find that quite exciting.

## Ketamine and slower wave propagation

Here's the other thing: ketamine. I would broadly describe ketamine, and dissociatives in general, as changing the wave-propagation dynamics of energy in your nervous system. Essentially, they make wave propagation slower — a kind of phase transition that makes everything move slower. Because a lot of our perception of the world is a high-level, emergent pattern of all of these non-linearities interacting with one another, slowing down wave propagation across the board produces some really crazy emergent effects. But I think the fundamental change in the substrate is very simple: it's just making the propagation of waves slower. I think we can probably show this empirically. We can measure how long it takes for stimulation of a certain sort to travel from one part of the nervous system to another on ketamine, and I would predict that it would go slower, and increasingly slower as a function of the dose.

As an emergent effect, this makes it really difficult for each layer in the nested hierarchical structure of the nervous system to be synchronized with its surrounding layer. It disconnects your internal models from the input they are presumably trying to predict. And this has a very strange effect: you can focus on the inner resonant modes of your deepest self — the most abstract and disembodied component of your experience — and be in a very blissful state, even if there's crazy music going on around you, or somebody is shouting, or other crazy things are going on. If you're in a sufficiently dissociated state, you're not going to phase-lock; you're not going to interlock with those external metronomes. You can, in a sense, choose not to interlock with them.

There's a corollary. Imagine you have a dissonant input outside of you. How do you prevent it from being translated into a dissonant internal state? There are roughly two solutions. The first is to dissociate from it, which is the ketamine route: you prevent coupling between sensory input and the resonant modes of your innermost state. You become a cocoon: the wave-propagation dynamics of your innermost experience don't resonate with the other layers, so they can just exist on their own. The second option is to predict the input perfectly. People who do a lot of noting meditation — very quickly noticing every tiny sensation arising and passing — can become really good at chopping up stimuli and predicting them very carefully and precisely, and if you can do that, you can preemptively cancel out what the input is going to be. So these are two completely different strategies: (a) you become really good at predicting, or (b) you become really good at dissociating.

Ketamine would be the latter. But there is an advantage to temporarily dissociating: it lowers the embodied constraints we have usually learned, such as our muscle memory. You can disembed yourself from those weights for a while and reduce internal model complexity. So to a large extent I would describe something like ketamine as annealing technology targeted at the innermost model complexity: something you might want to take if you need to reduce model complexity. Psychedelics across the board are more complicated, because they don't necessarily dissociate you from the lower layers. So you may end up dragging all of your human emotions and human needs into those crazy mystical states, which can be pretty unpleasant.

### Combining dissociatives and psychedelics

The really crazy thing happens when you combine dissociatives and psychedelics. I have a video and an article about what we call **free-wheeling hallucinations**,[^free-wheeling] which are states of consciousness that emerge from, let's say, 100 micrograms of LSD and 100 milligrams of ketamine taken at the same time — or you first take the LSD and a couple of hours later the ketamine; the point is that the peak effects happen in conjunction. My interpretation is that you are effectively getting dissociation from the lower layers of the nervous system's predictive coding, with slower energy propagation, and on top of that you're energizing the system, which is what the psychedelic does. So you become this cocoon — this innermost experience, dissociated from its environment — but supercharged, super-energized. Under those conditions you can experience some of the most bizarre, crazy, intense, and compelling non-linearities in the nervous system.

I really believe that as soon as more researchers — neuroscientists, people like Stephen Grossberg and so on — experiment in a very rigorous fashion with straight-up combining LSD and ketamine, a lot of these pieces of the puzzle will come together. This video is an advance in that direction, but we're still pretty far from a complete understanding. So that's LSD and ketamine: what happens with dissociatives and psychedelics.

## The world-sheet and mystical experiences

The thing I will conclude with, finally, is an implication of the fact that we represent the world with what we call a **world-sheet**: a depth map, the representation that is constantly being updated. On DMT, you experience so many waves of energy generating non-linearities that the non-linearities are generated faster than they can dissipate, so the homeostatic level of the amount of qualia you're experiencing ends up higher. You end up with representations of the world enriched with color, texture, and movement. Then the question is how to fit all of that together into a unified experience that is elegant, that minimizes its model complexity. The answer is that you do a lot of stitching together, and that's what ultimately accounts for the creation of a hyperbolic world-sheet: you're stitching together all of these internal representations. As I mentioned, every time a wave of energy hits a surface, you have both that and its oscillatory complement — the observer, the thing keeping track of that point of view. *(I'm sure this doesn't make sense unless you've recently had intense meditation or DMT experiences, but I promise, I promise this makes sense.)* So the world-sheet is the equilibrium of all of those crazy oscillations trying to be stitched together into a unified representation that makes holistic sense.

You can do really crazy things when that happens, and this is not only DMT but psychedelics more broadly. Look at the **Mystical Experience Questionnaire**, which is often given to people right after a psychedelic experience. It includes items such as "experience of oneness in relation to an inner world within."[^meq] Apparently it's very common to experience oneness with an inner world within. From our point of view, that might have something to do with synchronization, or coherence, between layers of the mind that usually don't talk to each other. Say your cognition and your innermost emotional self usually oscillate in ways that are decoupled from one another. A psychedelic reduces the threshold for coupling of any kind of oscillation, so you may experience coupling between high-level cognition and your innermost emotions, and that might feel like something you might want to describe as the experience of oneness in relation to an inner world within.

Then there are other items, for example the experience of the fusion of your personal self into a larger whole. Likewise, your inner world-simulation contains a homunculus — the representation of your body — but it's just one part of the representation, and it has a certain vibrational signature (we can leave it at that for the time being). If that gets coupled with the vibrational signature of other aspects of your experience, you will experience local binding between your sense of self and other things: space, for example, or the walls around you, or something more abstract, like the feeling of presence all around you. You can experience crazy local binding connections between those components of your world-simulation on psychedelics.

Or take the experience of the insight that all is one; we've explained that before. Or the feeling of experiencing eternity or infinity. That's also super crazy, but totally possible, and I'll just refer you to the video "What Is Time?" or the article "The Pseudo-Time Arrow" from the Qualia Research Institute.[^pseudo-time] Basically, the arrow of time is also something that annealing effects can influence. You can experience a very simplified feeling of time, or, even more crazily, you can create time loops that are experienced all at once. It's a very crazy thing, and you need to read the article to really make sense of it. But when you have that kind of experience, you feel as if you're eternally stuck in that particular moment, because the ending of the moment contains the seeds of its very beginning, and there's no way out of it. You're in what is described as a time loop, or a limit cycle in dynamical systems, and you're stuck in there.

Anyway, the Mystical Experience Questionnaire makes a lot of sense if you think of your experience as the result of an optical, massively parallel system, and make sense of psychedelics as energized non-linearities within that optical system. To some extent I expect what I just said to make very little sense to most people, but I also strongly expect it to make a lot of sense in 10 or 20 years, when this is a much more concrete field of study. Hopefully QRI will have participated in that process.

## The Goldilocks zone of oneness

I'll also mention the **Goldilocks zone of oneness**. We've talked about how the feeling of everything being one comes very naturally when you're in a very low-entropy configuration of consciousness — when, say, everything becomes just one point to which you are synchronized at the center of your awareness. If you understand your experience as this massively parallel resonant state, there's a very straightforward way in which all views of personal identity can be represented in it:

- If you identify with all of the surfaces and the space represented in your inner world-simulation, maybe that would be roughly equivalent to **open individualism**: you identify with consciousness itself, which we all have, so in that sense we are all the same consciousness.
- If you identify with a homunculus within that world-simulation, that would be **closed individualism**, the standard belief most people have: they start existing when they're born and stop existing when they die.
- If you identify with the pseudo-time arrow — the flickering quality of experience — then you would be identifying with **empty individualism**: we are just one time slice.

In this picture, beliefs about personal identity correspond to different configurations of the world-sheet. If the world-sheet is sending most of the attention toward space itself, that lends itself to open individualism. Most people's intuitive sense of self is constantly generating oscillatory complements with the homunculus — the inner sense of self acting as the lead, or the nucleation point, for resonant modes — and that has a really strong closed-individualist feel to it. When you disable the self as the self-organizing principle that interlocks and stitches together all of the oscillatory complements, you can create crazy parallel realities that don't interact with one another — parallel in a way, because ultimately they're globally bound even though they might not be locally bound. When you do that, the sense of self doesn't really exist, and representations of open and empty individualism become much more accessible.

But I think a very plausible way of developing compassion, not being so afraid of dying, and being in touch with one's emotions would be to cultivate the feeling of the three views of personal identity being somewhat true simultaneously. You identify with consciousness as a whole, so you feel a sense of responsibility toward the well-being of other beings, and you also stop being afraid of death, because you identify with consciousness itself. But you also identify with yourself, with your own individual persona, so you take care of that timeline, which is also really important: you are the best steward of your timeline. And you also care about each moment of experience, which the narrative self doesn't always care about — although that's a topic for another video.

## Conclusion: world-sheet manipulation technology

To conclude and wrap up: I very much believe that when we gain world-sheet manipulation technology — something like a very, very well-controlled DMT-type experience — we will be able to recruit the massively parallel self-organizing principles our consciousness instantiates in order to speed up particular computational problems, or the speed at which we find their solutions. I think this is going to be an ongoing thing for many decades to come. We're going to find more and more ways in which neurons in a Petri dish, then the nervous system of a particular insect, and then the nervous systems of humans are solving computational problems in a far more general way than you would expect if we thought of them only in terms of classical computing. That is a really exciting journey, and I'm very confident it's going to rock the way in which we make sense of intelligence, of computation, and of what it is to be conscious.

With that, thank you very much, and I'll talk to you another time on another topic. Infinite bliss, everybody!
