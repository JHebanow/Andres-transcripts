## A personal history of the boundary problem

All right: the binding/boundary problem. A little bit of personal history, I suppose. I've been obsessed with the problem of consciousness since I was 16. I'd been thinking about it since I was something like seven years old, but I didn't realize how significant consciousness was, and how poorly understood it was, until I turned 16 and had a weird ego-death experience with — it turns out — marijuana. I've told this story before, but it made me realize: oh my god, we do not understand consciousness. I had no idea that the mystery was so wide and deep.

But it's something that takes time to mature. I've been obsessively thinking about it, but the way I think about it has still evolved tremendously over the years. I'm now about 32, so for about half of my life I've been obsessively thinking about this, and there have been phases — stages in my life, different levels of maturation — in how I approached the problem. And I've got to say that the binding/boundary problem didn't figure as the most significant thing, or even as something to study specifically. When I thought about consciousness at 16 or 17, it was more about the question of a first-person perspective: how do you get a first-person perspective in an otherwise third-person universe? I was curious about qualia, but I wasn't thinking that deeply about qualia, or about the palette problem — the state space of qualia — or anything like that. That came later, when I started to formalize the problem, talk to collaborators, and read the literature, and realized that we can actually break the problem of consciousness down into sub-problems.

### David Pearce: drawing a boundary around information

I definitely credit David Pearce here. I met him at Stanford around 2011 and developed a great friendship with him, and he pushed back on my computational theories of consciousness — I was a computationalist, a hardcore computationalist. I just thought about causal structures; I thought of information processing as the source of consciousness. But he pointed out: how do you draw a boundary around a bundle of information and say, "this is your experience", as distinct from everybody else's experiences, or from the space around you, or from this bundle of air around here? Through a lot of conversation, David Pearce eventually convinced me that this problem may actually rule out a whole class of theories of consciousness: functionalism, causal theories, computational theories of consciousness. They seem inadequate for making sense of the binding problem.

### Mike Johnson: qualia formalism and frame invariance

Later on came my QRI co-founder and former collaborator, Mike Johnson. We did a lot of work together, especially between 2016 and 2019 — a really solid, amazing collaboration. He gave me a couple of pieces of the puzzle that I took very seriously and incorporated into how I think about it. One is **qualia formalism**, which he coined in *Principia Qualia*:[^principia-qualia] the idea that for every system that produces a conscious experience there corresponds a mathematical object, such that the properties of the experience are isomorphic to the mathematical properties of that object. That was a very important way of thinking about it. The kind of solution you will get for a theory of consciousness will be a way of looking at systems and interpreting them in order to get a mathematical object out of them — plus some ability to read the mathematical properties of that object and transform them into the phenomenology of the experience.

Two other things I definitely credit him with — in addition to a whole lot of other things that have been important in my development — are these. First, he pointed out that there may be a more fruitful, more tractable way of talking about the binding problem. Instead of asking how 100 billion neurons distributed in the brain can simultaneously contribute to a unified experience, maybe you start out with a fundamental, universal unity of everything — the quantum field, or zero-point energy, whatever it may be. You start out with an ontology that is unified, and then the question becomes: how do you draw boundaries in it?

Second, a very important hint that I first heard from him — although it's been talked about in the literature, as we will briefly discuss — was **frame invariance**. Ideally, you want a solution to consciousness — that is, a way of interpreting a physical system in order to get a mathematical object that is isomorphic to your experience — that is frame-invariant: it does not depend on who the observer is, how fast they are moving, or from where they are measuring, because it describes the physical system in a frame-invariant way. It needs to be frame-invariant so that it isn't the case that if you look at it this way it is conscious, and if you look at it that way it is not. That seems an absolutely essential constraint.

### From atoms and forces to fields

Importantly, after thinking about it in those terms, one very simple way of describing this transition is to realize that if you live in an ontology — if you implicitly believe that the universe is made of atoms and forces — then the binding problem is actually impossible to solve, because there is no frame-invariant way of drawing a boundary. It depends on how fast you're moving and from what point of view you're analyzing it. The same goes for information, causal, or integration theories of consciousness: they're not going to be frame-invariant either, if you have an ontology of atoms and forces.

But the universe is not made of atoms and forces, and we have known this since the 19th century. That was one of the very big insights of Maxwell and Faraday, in how they started to think about electromagnetism: no, these are not just particles and forces — these are fields. The field evolves according to differential equations that are coupled together, so in a way matter tells the field how it should be shaped, and the shape of the field tells matter how it should move; there's this interrelationship between the two. In some sense you can even think of particles as field properties. One mind-blowing conceptual reframe, which is very helpful, is to think of electrons not as particles emitting forces — an ontology that doesn't allow you to solve these kinds of problems — but as places in the field where field lines converge. The electron is almost a manifestation of the field, rather than something inherently existing. There are some Buddhist intuitions there too — emptiness, or dependent origination: the electron doesn't exist without the surrounding field, because it is a property of the field. It's not a self-existing, independent, metaphysically enduring object or anything of the sort.

Now, if you have a field ontology — the universe is made of fields — then the question is: how do you get boundaries around those fields, in order to explain why I'm here and you're there?

### Steven Lehar: resonance as glue

The third very important personal piece of the puzzle, for me, was encountering Steven Lehar. His whole framework uses harmonic resonance as a conceptual and analytic tool for making sense of holistic information processing in consciousness — in particular, the interpretation of visual and other sensory gestalts, which have a kind of holism, a life of their own: they act as units, they behave as individuals. He proposed that the binding between these features happens via resonance. Why? He was criticizing the **neuron doctrine**, which takes neurons as the units of information processing in the brain, so that you can discretize the brain and think of it as a big graph. The problem there is that binding would be merely metaphorical. There would be some kind of tag saying that this feature here — this edge — and this color, let's say blue, belong to the same object; they're tagged as being part of the same object. But just because two things are tagged doesn't mean they're integrated. In fact, the whole idea of binding is that it is actually some kind of glue: if you move part A, which is bound to part B, then by moving A you automatically move B too — like an actual glue.

What Lehar was arguing in many of his canonical papers is that binding must be a kind of glue, and he suspects that resonance is that glue, because resonance gives you the capacity for affecting things at a distance. You're not breaking the speed of light or anything of the sort — you're still constrained by the speed of wave propagation in the medium, of course — but, taking that into account, there is nonetheless holistic behavior. When two things are tightly coupled in resonance, moving one will move the other, with a little bit of a delay, of course. But for many purposes that doesn't matter: functionally, it is still usable.

Lehar was thinking about the binding problem in terms of **local binding**: how features of your experience get put together into unified percepts. As far as I know, he never provided a solution to the **global binding** problem — how you draw these boundaries: how is it that all the pieces of your experience, which are connected to each other, are not connected with things outside of your experience? As far as I know, Lehar doesn't have an answer to that. But I thought resonance as a solution to local binding was a very, very important hint and clue.

### Desiderata for a solution

Those were all the pieces of the puzzle, and I was already thinking about them around 2017 or so. One of the ways in which I think you can make tractable progress on questions in philosophy of mind and consciousness — very deep philosophical problems that may have real solutions that eventually become science — is to keep in mind the constraints that such a theory must be able to satisfy. So already by 2017 I was thinking: all of these pieces of the puzzle generate constraints that a theory of consciousness must satisfy — in particular, for the boundary problem. What are these constraints? I formalized it, and, putting all of these things together, my desiderata — the things a theory must satisfy to be a solution to the boundary problem, according to me — would be:

1. **Frame invariance.** Again, it doesn't depend on your particular point of view, how fast you're moving, and so on.

2. **No strong emergence.** This is in line with the tenets of physicalism as defined by David Pearce: the universe behaves according to the laws of physics, even if you stay silent about what the universe fundamentally is. *(Spoiler: it is a gigantic field of consciousness.)* No strong emergence is entailed by physicalism. There is nowhere in the universe where, all of a sudden, quantum field theory doesn't work. All of the strange, bizarre things — superfluid helium, superconductivity, superradiance, black holes (well, I don't know about black holes), weird things like neutron stars, and so on — are accounted for by quantum field theory. They're just exotic high-energy, or very low-energy, configurations of it, where very different things happen. Nonetheless, there's no strong emergence: it isn't the case that when certain conditions are met, a new law of physics gets activated. We don't want that; we just want standard physics.

3. **Non-epiphenomenalism.** Unfortunately, this is a very, very confusing topic, but essentially you want to explain not only why we're conscious in this vaguely defined sense; you want to explain why the boundaries around experience are where they are, such that our experience actually makes sense. We can talk about our experience; we have the capacity to introspect and share it with others. How did that evolve? How did natural selection see and use the boundary of a moment of experience? How is it using it, and how is it causally significant? If you just have a way of interpreting these mathematical objects where, when such-and-such conditions are met, you get a boundary, but the boundary doesn't have any further effects as a whole, then that's not going to be a solution, because evolution would not be able to see it, and would not be able to recruit it for information processing or for causal effects. So we must avoid epiphenomenalism — not of reality or of consciousness as a whole, but in particular epiphenomenalism of things such as feature binding or boundary-making. That makes a lot of sense, and it's absolutely essential: you have to keep it in mind as a constraint.

4. **Weak emergence.** To conclude, the fourth one is that you have to explain this in terms of something like weak emergence, or weak downward causation. Otherwise you're going against the laws of physics, and you're probably not going to be right.

## The literature on the boundary problem

Now, some literature review. Chalmers, in his 2016 paper on the combination problem for panpsychism,[^chalmers] raises the question of binding quiddities: if you have something that solves the binding problem in panpsychism, it has to be something that actually has causal effects, and it has to make sense why it has causal effects — it can't be an additional law of physics or something like that. In particular, he attributes the formulation of the boundary problem to Rosenberg, in 1998, who wrote this pretty amazing piece called "The Boundary Problem for Phenomenal Individuals".[^rosenberg] I love that title because it's so ambiguous. It could be something you'd find in *Cosmo*, or in one of those cheap fashion or personal-development magazines: if you are a phenomenal individual, how do you keep boundaries with other people? How do you avoid being exploited? How do you avoid difficult breakups? You've got to keep good boundaries if you're a phenomenal individual! Of course, I'm joking. Later, in 2004, he published a very similar piece, now named "The Boundary Problem for Experiencing Subjects", which I guess removes a lot of the ambiguity: it's not a *Cosmo* article anymore. We found that Rosenberg was the first to very clearly articulate the difficulties of the boundary problem, and what a solution would need to satisfy.

A lot of people think of the brain as so highly interconnected that there are peaks of integration and interconnectivity. But how is that not a fuzzy relationship? If there's a network of interactions, maybe there's some clustering — this region is tightly clustered in its causal interactions in the brain, for example. But there are still edges connecting it to the rest of the network; you don't actually have a fundamental boundary arising there. So how do you get my experience versus your experience? These clustering approaches are fuzzy. Thresholds — on the modularity of the causal graph, say — will also be pretty arbitrary: why that threshold and not something else? And there has to be some kind of mechanistic motivation: the binding, or the boundary-making, has to be useful for something; it has to be meaningful; it has to have causal effects. Otherwise it's just epiphenomenal — something an external observer looking at the system would describe by saying, "it kind of looks like there's a boundary around here". No: it has to actually have significant causal effects.

The way Rosenberg formulated it was: how can consciousness exist at the middle level? It's like the classic Greek story of Scylla and Charybdis — two terrible monsters, and you have to navigate exactly in between. The unity of experience at the meso level — the human level — is very mysterious. Why is the unity of experience not at the microscopic or atomic level? And why is it not, for example, at the level of galaxies? We have trillions of cells: why is the unity of consciousness not there? Why isn't it at the organ level? And why isn't it at the group level, the family level, the society level, or the ecosystem level? Why exactly you, as an individual? That is how he formulated it.

### Schrödinger and the meso level

It's very worth pointing out that Schrödinger — the famous Schrödinger of the Schrödinger equation — was also a philosopher, very deeply motivated to answer the big questions about consciousness. In particular, he was an open individualist: Schrödinger believed that we are all one consciousness, as did Einstein. But interestingly, unlike Einstein — for whom there is no evidence that he ever contemplated the boundary problem (I wish he had; it would have been very significant, I think) — Schrödinger did. He was asking exactly the question: why is the boundary around us at this level, rather than at the level of the cell, the organ, the system, the culture, or the ecosystem? Why at the human meso level?

So with the boundary problem we have to navigate between the Scylla of tiny subsystems being bound and the Charybdis of why the galaxy is not the level at which everything gets integrated — or the whole universe, for that matter, the entire quantum wave function.

### A systematic search

Again, I've got Chris to thank for this: a very, very exhaustive literature review to see whether anybody had formulated anything similar. There is one researcher who has talked about topology potentially being significant: Robert Prentner.[^prentner] Interestingly, he was my TA back at Stanford for a course in philosophy of mind. Independently, he had the idea that topology is potentially significant, but he was thinking of something like the topology of a graph, as opposed to the topology of a field, which is where we are providing a contribution.

We searched the Scopus database with so many possible keywords — anything that might potentially have any relevance to the boundary problem — and it turns out there were really only three publications. One is by Fekete and colleagues, in 2016,[^fekete] which was restricted to computational theories of consciousness. Nonetheless, they were thinking about how there could be a graded measure for an observer-independent partition within a computational theory of consciousness, and they found some problems — in particular, how do you avoid having merely epiphenomenal appendages? If the measure says that the system integrates at this level, and you were to consider just one of its subsystems, and the measure now says that it integrates at that level, how is the smaller level being swallowed up by the larger one? You get issues around mereological nihilism, or a part–whole ambiguity, that make the account very observer-dependent and not frame-invariant. As far as I know, all computational theories of consciousness suffer from this; I don't know of one that has an answer to it. So you don't even need something like the **slicing problem** — the other paper we published with Chris[^slicing-problem] — to realize the problems with computational theories of consciousness. And I say this as somebody who was a true believer in computational theories of consciousness until David Pearce disabused me of that.

### General resonance theory

The other two significant publications are by Tam Hunt,[^hunt] who, with his team, is developing what is called **general resonance theory** (GRT). Their idea is that the boundary is constructed out of the lowest shared resonance in a system, and they argue that this does partition the universe into clusters, creating a very natural way of segmenting energy in the universe and therefore of getting actual boundaries. However, I would say there is a bit of a problem here: resonance is a graded process. Take two waves: it's not the case that you can clearly say where the resonance ends. Sure, their peaks and troughs are going in synchrony, but as you approach the outer edges, at what point do you say that the waves actually belong to that particular vibrational mode — especially in a non-linear system, where there's coupling between resonant modes? I'm very unclear about that. And then, spatially: where does the resonance end? So, as I see it — although we absolutely applaud the efforts to make it quantitative, mathematical, and precise — I am not convinced that general resonance theory actually creates objective boundaries, and especially not that those objective boundaries, as opposed to fuzzy ones, are in any way causally significant.

There might be ways around it, though, in particular around the topic of phase transitions. If in physics there were some way of saying exactly where a phase transition happens, and that also emergently gave rise to a lowest shared resonance, then that might potentially be a solution to the boundary problem.

As you will see in a bit, when I give you a better formalization and breakdown of the boundary problem, we think there are other promising theories for the boundary problem. However, other than our formulation — with Rosenberg as the precedent — there hasn't been in the literature, until now, a breakdown of the boundary problem that we think would be satisfying. Some solutions to the boundary problem can tackle some of these sub-problems or desiderata, but not all of them. We claim that ours tackles all of them, which is what makes it so promising.

### Bayne's formulation

There are also a lot of indirect discussions. For example, one of the things I read — in 2016, it must have been — is the book *The Unity of Consciousness* by Tim Bayne.[^bayne] I really appreciate that it tries to be comprehensive, and the prose is pretty excellent: it's very well written and covers a lot of ground. But it doesn't actually address the boundary problem. It wasn't until later that one of his reviewers, in another paper, said: okay, sure, you're providing some ideas for how to solve the binding problem, but how do you stop that binding from propagating to the rest of the universe? Bayne then tried to reformulate it. So Bayne's formulation of the boundary problem might be: why are particular experiences parts of the experiences they are parts of, whereas others are not?

Actually, I think it is very helpful to share many formulations of the problem, because some may spark a resonance in you that others don't. So I'm not going to say that this formulation is the one and only. For something as complex and intricate as philosophy of mind, having various approaches and various definitions is very helpful, because at the end of the day a lot of the progress depends on you actually being convinced that there is a problem.

So, to say it again, Bayne would formulate it as: why are particular experiences parts of the experiences that they are parts of, whereas others are not? There's a bit of an issue here. You're asking why an experience — and I take it he actually means features of experience — is part of another: for example, how is this speck of, say, the color of my hand bound — how is it part of the experience of your visual field? However, with holism, where you say that the experience happens all at once, then in some very deep ontological sense there are no parts of an experience. An experience is not made of smaller experiences. An experience is just a whole thing, whose intentional content — its internal information structure — is precisely the feeling that there are parts. Let that sink in; that's also mind-blowing, actually. In some sense your experience has no parts, but the information content your experience is rendering is that of an experience with parts. The parts are kind of illusory, because your whole experience happens all at once. Give that a little bit of thought at some point.

That is the issue with Bayne's formulation, and we prefer Rosenberg's more formal way of putting it: why are some experiences contained within a given phenomenal boundary? Now you don't have the part–whole ambiguity, and you're not going to have to talk about mereological nihilism; you're just asking why some experiences are contained within a given phenomenal boundary. We think that gives a little more traction.

### Electromagnetic field theories of consciousness

Section 2.4 of the paper is about electromagnetic field theories of consciousness, and whether they address the boundary problem. Here is the thing — you will see this all over the place: proponents of electromagnetic theories of consciousness will very proudly proclaim, "We don't have the binding problem, because the field is unified; it's a non-issue for us." Which is all well and good — but then you get the boundary problem. So what are you going to do? You solved one problem, but now you've got a different problem. We've got to tone down that pride a little: just because the field may be unified and smooth doesn't mean you've solved everything. You just switched to a different problem.

A few interesting things. For example, there are field-realist electromagnetic theories of consciousness, like Jones (2010, 2019).[^jones] It's called field realism: the energy of the field is the carrier of consciousness — of the amount of consciousness. I'm actually very sympathetic to it. Energy doesn't get destroyed or produced; it just changes form. So it's very appealing to think that the total consciousness in the universe is maintained, just in a state of flux. This is different from, for example, McFadden — one of the main proponents of electromagnetic theories of consciousness[^mcfadden] — who thinks that what corresponds to consciousness is the information in the electromagnetic field.

To keep moving: Jones (2013)[^jones-2013] would say that only sufficiently strong electromagnetic interactions can solve the binding problem in this context. That might give you an explanation for why two individuals don't share the same experience, but it doesn't address the deeper problem: within your brain, how do you determine which subsystems are actually contributing to a moment of experience? If everything within the brain has a strong field, where do you find the boundaries, and are they precise? It's one thing to say, "I have a solution for why you and I are not the same experience right now." But that is not enough, because you also have to be able to say, looking at an active brain, where the boundaries are. Is it because it ends at the skull?

That takes me to one of McFadden's proposals: that the conductivity of the cerebrospinal fluid might make it work as a Faraday cage. That's a very interesting possibility, but it doesn't really give you a solution to the boundary problem either. What is actually the boundary between the cerebrospinal fluid and the other parts of the anatomy, such that you could say that precisely here, at the level of fundamental reality, you get this boundary? It is actually pretty fuzzy. And it opens up the possibility of very fascinating thought experiments. For example, you slowly make a hole through your skull and dry out the cerebrospinal fluid, and you start reaching the diffraction limit, with electromagnetic waves starting to leak. It seems to me that this also has the fuzziness property: there's no precise moment at which you can say that now you have a fully enclosed electromagnetic field. If McFadden is right, that would in principle allow us — in the medium or far future, maybe in a few centuries — to mind-meld with others: if you create a dry channel without cerebrospinal fluid, and then create a Faraday cage around the two brains, they would share the same field.

That's an interesting possibility. Then there's Shani and Keppler (2018),[^shani-keppler] one of the trippiest papers we reviewed. It's more of a quantum thing, but it is electromagnetic insofar as they're talking about the electron field — in particular, coherence patches within the electron field. They also talk about zero-point energy: how in the quantum vacuum there are patches of coherence that are clearly delimited, and that actual moments of experience are like that.

Just to name a few others: Winters (2021)[^winters] talks about temporally integrated causality. That may help with the binding problem, but it doesn't solve the boundary problem. I actually talked about this in my previous video about solving the boundary problem:[^binding-video] integrated causality doesn't really solve the boundary problem, even if it gives you some pointers for the binding problem. Then Bond (2023),[^bond] very recent, is about coherent fields within expanses of photonic waves. But there, too, you have a fading-with-distance logic, where the boundaries are not actually strict.

In other words, people have been trying to propose solutions to the boundary problem, both directly and indirectly, and they are not really satisfying.

## Five boundary sub-problems

So we went ahead and broke the boundary problem down into sub-problems that we think are actually tractable, which formalizes the boundary problem.

### The hard boundary problem

The first sub-problem is the **hard boundary problem**. It's exactly as it sounds: we don't want fuzzy boundaries. We want absolute, real boundaries that are true and real independent of how you look at the system — 100%: you belong either inside or outside. Think about the binding problem in terms of, for example, transitivity: every two features of your experience are connected by the fact of being part of the same experience. So if A is bound to B, which is bound to C, that entails that A is bound to C. With fuzzy boundaries you can make chains — A is bound to B, which is bound to C, which is bound to D — but then A and D may be so weakly bound that you could say they're not bound anymore. So with fuzzy boundaries, I think you're really not thinking deeply about the properties of your consciousness, of the phenomena you're trying to explain. You're maybe explaining something else, not really looking at the thing, as it were.

### The lower-levels boundary problem

The second is the **lower-levels boundary problem**. This is the Scylla: why isn't the boundary at, for example, the cellular level or the neural-cluster level? Inside us, why aren't the partitions smaller than, let's say, the thalamus, or the brain, or whatever is actually the carrier of the internal world-simulation of our environment? You have to explain that. So that's a lower bound. In mathematics, when you want to find a value, you often say there's a lower bound and an upper bound, and the work is in between. Exactly like that: the lower-levels boundary problem asks why the boundaries are not at a smaller scale.

### The higher-levels boundary problem

Then the **higher-levels boundary problem** — problem number three — is why the boundaries are not at a higher level. For instance: when we're in synchrony, singing in a choir, or meditating together, why don't we all become one big field of experience? Now, some people say that does happen. I think the parsimonious explanation is that when you have the feeling of telepathy with somebody else — on LSD, for example, or in deep meditation — it is because the internal boundaries of your world-simulation are softening, or maybe even breaking down. So it doesn't mean that you're literally merging with the rest of the universe; it just means that you're softening, reducing, or eliminating internal boundaries via, let's say, a process of synchronization. If that is the case, you don't actually merge. You're not one with the cosmos; you're not one with the person you're having a deep connection with. You're just having an interesting synchronization between your internal representations of those other aspects of the world. Either way, any solution to the boundary problem will have to explain why the boundary stops at the level of the individual, or of the nervous system.

### The private boundary problem

Number four is the **private boundary problem**: why can't we merge with others? Rather than being about scale, it's about why, for example, the boundaries aren't transient: why is it the case that you have this enduring boundary for a given organism?

### The temporal boundary problem

Number five is the **temporal boundary problem**. This one is very tricky, and not many people realize it. It has a lot to do with the philosophy of personal identity — Parfit, Daniel Kolak, reductive theories of personal identity. Derek Parfit in particular had the idea that the continuity of the self through time is illusory: there are just fragments of memory that are constantly recreating the illusion of a continuous self. That would be diachronic identity, identity over time. And in some sense that's trivially true, because you're not sharing the same experience as your past self, and you're not sharing the same experience as your future self. So there is something dividing you, creating a boundary between you now and you in the past and you in the future. That already hints at the idea that you're not one enduring metaphysical ego over time: you're not one huge experience throughout your whole life. You're lots of different fragments — different moments of experience.

Speaking for myself, I affectionately call Derek Parfit "Mr. Almost Almost", because he always gets very close to the edge of a mind-blowing insight, then gets scared, takes a step back, and defaults to common sense. He does this over and over, in so many different contexts — unfortunately, I'm sorry to say. In the case of binding, he realizes that there is no actual binding across time. But if you read *Reasons and Persons* from cover to cover, there is no discussion of actual synchronic binding, or synchronic unity: the fact that every moment of experience does have a natural boundary around it. And I would argue that this is not illusory, for very deep reasons. Even if our experience is less unified than it seems, the mere fact that you can have a complex percept — even one with just two features at once, two colors, say — the fact that you can experience a contrast, is already some kind of fundamental unity of information in its presentation. Derek Parfit doesn't talk about that; he doesn't realize it. My sense is that he never really thought about it. But it completely changes the landscape of discourse on personal identity, and I think it favors, by default, something like **empty individualism**: the idea that we are just a time slice, a moment of experience.

Mike Johnson had also said this — probably when we met, in 2016 at some point. He was thinking about the temporal boundary too, and his idea was that experiences are probably some kind of 4D object in the block-time universe. He didn't have any particular mechanism of action, or an account of how that would be causally significant, but he was thinking in terms of 4D objects. So the temporal boundary problem asks why you are divided from the rest of the universe not only spatially but also temporally. Why are you not the same experience over time — not one huge experience for your whole life? Why are you experience after experience after experience? And how thick are these experiences along the time axis? Speaking for myself, my intuition is that each moment of experience is probably on the order of a millisecond or so. It could be a lot smaller, but I don't think it's longer than one millisecond — I think that would be very difficult to pull off. Maybe 10 milliseconds at most, but not longer than that, for very deep reasons I'm not going to go into right now.

## How EM field topology meets the desiderata

Section three of the paper gives precise statements of the five specific sub-problems of the boundary problem. Section four is about how the topological segmentation of the electromagnetic field could satisfy these problems — or rather, these explananda: frame invariance, no strong emergence, non-epiphenomenalism, weak emergence. And the answer is that it satisfies all of them.

### Closed field lines and knots of light

To give you a taste of it: Wolski (2011)[^wolski] shows that most magnetic flux lines occur in closed loops. So most of magnetism is topologically closed — typically; not always, but in the vast majority of cases. Electric field lines sometimes form closed loops and sometimes are open lines; it depends. And Irvine and Bouwmeester (2008) showed that you can actually make knots of light:[^light-knots] even in the vacuum you can have twisted toroidal structures that carry light in a topologically structured way. So the topology of the electromagnetic field, even in the vacuum, can have these interesting properties.

Now, this is a deep topic, but it has to do with the linearity of differential equations. It turns out that Maxwell's equations in the vacuum are linear, meaning that any combination of waves in the vacuum can be added together, following the superposition principle. Given that, the topology of the field in the vacuum may not have that much causal significance on its own. However — however — the moment it starts interacting with matter, it does become causally significant, because these toroidal topological pockets can, for example, confine particles. An electron may get stuck circulating inside such a pocket, as in a particle accelerator, where the structure of the magnetic field lines traps a particle so that it can't escape. It's essentially like that. So that is causally significant. I'll say one more thing about that in a second.

### Hard and frame-invariant boundaries

Field topologies are also hard boundaries. There's a precise spatio-temporal location where, for example, the field lines, instead of being open lines, all of a sudden — whoop — form a loop; if you follow the lines, you're eternally trapped in there. Then, elsewhere in space-time, they open up again. So there's a four-dimensional topological pocket, with hard boundaries. And this is frame-invariant. Of course, how you visualize the field will depend on how fast you're moving and how you measure it — but only in terms of your present measurements. The moment you integrate over time, you will reconstruct the same topology. Why? There's a very beautiful technical reason: according to special relativity, what makes something frame-invariant is that it is Lorentz-invariant — the Lorentz transform leaves that mathematical feature intact. And it turns out that topology is not modified by the Lorentz transform. The Lorentz transform is just squeezing and shearing — doing this hyperbolic transform on the field — but it's not changing the topology: it never cuts or glues anything, ever. So, boom: electromagnetic topology is frame-invariant. It doesn't matter how you're moving; it's relativistic.

### Holism and non-epiphenomenalism

Then holism: how do you get non-epiphenomenalism? Why was electromagnetic topology recruited by natural selection? There are many reasons, but one of them is that when you have topologically segmented regions of the electromagnetic field, the electromagnetic waves inside them highlight their resonant modes. It's very similar to why the sound of a guitar is an expression of the entire shape of the guitar: if you change the shape of the guitar, you change the sound, and in some sense, with enough data points, you can derive what the shape is just from the sound. Likewise, when you have an electromagnetic pocket with electromagnetic waves inside it — with total internal reflection, for example, in an optical network — you will have standing-wave patterns: the electromagnetic **eigenmodes** inside it, which are all of the ways in which you can fit a wave an integer number of times within that pocket. That is going to be an expression of the entirety of the shape. If you can measure those resonant modes, you can — up to a point; I think there are counterexamples, but largely — rederive what the shape must have been. And therefore, because it acts as a unit, it is something that evolution can use for causal effects and for compute.

Also — and this is a fascinating, very fortuitous, and very good coincidence — the topology of the electromagnetic field is implied by Maxwell's equations, together with the non-linearities that arise in various materials. That is also just standard physics. We are not introducing anything exotic: this is plain textbook electromagnetism, nothing fancy — even though most introductory electromagnetism courses are not going to tell you that the topology can be non-trivial. Usually you won't learn that until later, in a seminar, or by looking at the cutting-edge research happening nowadays.

## Three examples of causally efficacious topology

So that satisfies the desiderata. Let me give you three very quick examples of how all of this comes together into causally efficacious topological segments of the electromagnetic field.

### Möbius strips of light

First, as I was saying, in an optical network — with fiber optics, for example — you can trap light with total internal reflection. When you have a material with a very high index of refraction, and light hits its surface at a sufficiently acute angle, the light doesn't refract: it doesn't go through to the other medium. There's a precise point at which it just reflects 100%. That's called total internal reflection. You can have a fiber-optic tube with total internal reflection, and then — it's not going to be 100%, but very close to it — for a while you will have trapped electromagnetic resonant modes inside it.

Now here's a very amazing thing — with a twist, literally and figuratively. If you take a band of fiber optic — not a cable, but a surface, like a strip — and connect it like a Möbius strip, you will get new, exotic resonant modes.[^mobius] The actual topology of the optical medium has a holistic effect on which resonant modes are present within the region of total internal reflection. So again, this is topology having very causally significant effects.

### Skyrmion bundles

Second, **skyrmion bundles**:[^skyrmion] very interesting configurations of the electromagnetic field at the microscopic level that occur in some materials, where you get these topological pockets, and they can be used for compute — people are speculating about how to do that. Because they can endure over time, they are a way of encoding information in the field — not in its curvature per se, but in its topological defects, which can be carried over long distances. You may potentially be able to propagate them across a very noisy machine, and the topology will be invariant.

### Solar flares and coronal mass ejections

Third: solar flares and coronal mass ejections. Somebody might be very skeptical and say: sure, I believe that topology matters, but maybe only in a very tiny, esoteric way, only in a lab — as with the Möbius strip of fiber optic. But no, no, no: electromagnetic topology has enormous effects at the macroscopic level. If you look at high-resolution pictures or videos of the Sun — I highly recommend doing that — you will see plasma tubes that go in and out of the Sun. That is electromagnetic confinement: these plasma tubes are confined by the topology of the field. What happens is that the Sun is constantly spinning, and there are convection currents, and all of that is transforming thermodynamic energy into **helicity** of the field. The Sun keeps getting more twisted — more and more twisted everywhere, constantly, all the time — dense and stressed. So every once in a while it needs to release that stress; if it didn't, the Sun would be torn apart by the tension in its own electromagnetic field. Every once in a while, these plasma tubes get very twisted, and at some point they touch each other, and what happens is called magnetic reconnection. When that happens — boom — the plasma tubes can open up, and you get millions of tons of matter sprayed into outer space with enormous energy.[^cme]

So that's a case of topological changes in the field at an enormous scale — these things are larger than our whole planet — having enormous causal effects: disrupting our power grid, for example, or sending massive bursts of gamma rays into outer space. So don't be deceived into thinking that the topology of the electromagnetic field is a very niche, laboratory-only phenomenon. No, no: it's everywhere. It is routine. It is commonplace.

## How the solution addresses the five sub-problems

To finish: how does our solution address the various sub-problems? First, the hard boundary: that's the whole point. I will say, though, that we are agnostic about the deeper reality of physics. Some physicists might criticize electromagnetic theories of consciousness as not fundamental enough — fair — because what is truly fundamental is the quantum fields, and they're not spatial fields; they're very weird mathematical objects. If you look into quantum field theory, it isn't describing a 3D space, or amplitudes in a 3D space. It describes an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, in configuration space, where every point corresponds to a possible configuration of all of the particles in the universe at once. And then you have things like electron fields, which are operator-valued: every point in the space-time of an electron field is not just an amplitude, but something more like a matrix encoding an operator on a Hilbert space. Interpreting that mathematical object and getting our 3D spatial world out of it is very non-trivial — kind of an open question, actually. It can be done for extremely tiny systems, but at the level of the universe it's just a mathematical nightmare.

Arguably, that is the deep truth. So maybe the macroscopic 3D electromagnetic field is a kind of shadow of that deeper reality. That said, we argued in the paper that even if the electromagnetic field is not fundamental per se, it can still do interesting things — for example, work as a kind of waveguide, or as a way of confining information transfer or possible interactions at the quantum level. I personally think it's quite likely that we will empirically verify the electromagnetic theory of consciousness with the topological solution, and that it will be well established in science — but it will still be a little mysterious in what way it is fundamental reality. I'm fine with that. We're still making progress.

### Lower and upper bounds

Two and three are the lower bound and the upper bound. Here we would say that there are all sorts of topological pockets everywhere, around us and within us. Maybe a lot of the cells in your body do have a topological boundary around them, so you might legitimately say that they are their own experiences — especially if their dipoles are oscillating very quickly: the magnetic field lines would first point one way and then the other, and every time they flip there would be a different moment of experience — again, a boundary over time. There are something like 20 quadrillion ants on Earth. Just because it's hard to imagine that there are a lot of a particular thing doesn't mean that's not the case. So I think it's probably likely that we will end up finding out that the universe is actually teeming with a huge number of topologically closed pockets — moments of experience. What are you going to do? Well, hedonic calculus would suggest that our biggest responsibility and mission in this universe will then be to increase the welfare of the topological pockets in the world around us. A huge task — but, I don't know, you've got to commit to something.

### Privacy

Number four is the private boundary problem. Here, physical proximity and sharing the segmentation mechanism are going to be constraints. Electromagnetic resonances in the brain require a very high degree of actual physical connectivity, and synapses, in order to be self-sustaining; and their field strength decays very quickly outside of you. That said, potentially, if you were, for example, to hook up my thalamus and your thalamus with a neural bridge, and actually share the lowest shared frequency of resonance — and if that is actually the mechanism of topological segmentation — then it might very well be that you and I could become one subject of experience, no problem.

### Temporal boundaries

Number five is the temporal boundary. Here we reference the **pseudo-time arrow**,[^pseudo-time-arrow] a concept I developed to explain the feeling of the passage of time — phenomenal time. I've written a lot about it, and I have presentations online as well. The idea is that the feeling of the passage of time is contained within each moment of experience: it has to do with how each moment of experience is structured internally. That is what determines whether it feels like time is moving very fast or slow, or — even weirder — whether it feels like time has completely stopped, as sometimes happens in so-called moments of eternity, or that time is looping, for example. We call that **exotic phenomenal time**. It happens in meditation, on psychedelics, in spontaneous mystical experiences, in near-death experiences. So the normal flow of time is a special case of a much more general thing. For me, that means — not only that, but it does mean — that phenomenal time and physical time are different things. Physical time is more about entropy and the arrow of time in physics, whereas phenomenal time is about how each of these topological pockets is structured.

In fact, in this view, take the lowest resonant mode of the entire brain — which is perhaps what helps unify your whole brain. This electric dipole may, for example, generate magnetic flux first in one direction and then in the opposite direction, and every time it flips — bam — that's a boundary. So temporal boundaries would be something along those lines: moment of experience, moment of experience, moment of experience, moment of experience, and so on and so forth, with a nested structure. And as long as the magnetic flux lines are all interconnected, so that you can go from anywhere to anywhere within that flux, all of that will be a topologically closed pocket.

The trickiest part, I think, is going to be how fundamental the electromagnetic field is. But again, our theory makes predictions, and I think we can probe it empirically. So that's about it. The paper goes into more detail, and I highly recommend reading it in its entirety. You will learn a lot, you will get very much up to speed with the conversation in the literature, and you will understand why what we are saying is a new, meaningful, and non-trivial contribution to the field of consciousness, and to the study of consciousness.
