### Fractality and scale-specific network geometry

One of the first things I want to address is the concept of fractality — in particular the colloquial, common-sense notion of fractals, which usually refers to self-similarity: the object looks the same at different scales.

When it comes to sub-agents, to the human body and biological organisms, there is usually *some* level of self-similarity across scales. But obviously it's not an infinite fractal. It doesn't go all the way down, or all the way up.

The difference is more than that. The geometry — the topology — of the system may change as a function of the scale at which you're analyzing it.

One of the key visualizations and discussions of this can be found in an article I wrote in 2020, when the coronavirus situation was evolving, where I discussed how some graph-theoretical concepts about what I call **scale-specific network geometry** may impact policy when it comes to preventing the spread of something very contagious.[^network-geometry]

What I want to say now is that there are a lot of networks — definitely at the level of society, but I think also inside us — that are not self-similar, but where the concept that there are interactions across scales still matters a lot.

A super simple example: a network that at the very small scale is actually linear. If you're at one node in that network and you start moving to other nodes connected to it, there's going to be a while during which you're walking along a line. The lines are themselves made of smaller nodes.

This is not self-similar. If you're at a node here and you start walking and moving, the number of nodes you can reach grows literally linearly as a function of the steps you're allowed to take. You take five steps and you can reach only five nodes. But then there's a critical moment where you reach one of these connector vertices — and then you can go to many other ones.

If you extend this system, there might be a scale at which you don't see the lines anymore. You just see polygons. As a microbe, you may think: I'm stuck in a line. But as an ant — something much larger — you'd say: no, this is a polygonal space, and it's two-dimensional. Something that at a certain scale looks one-dimensional may, at another scale, be two-dimensional.

And if you continue to fill this out, adding more detail as you move away from the center, what may happen is that at an even bigger scale this actually looks like kale — like the crevices in a kale leaf, which is what's called hyperbolic curvature.

So here you find that as you start moving around, at the beginning the reachable nodes grow linearly. Then at some point, as you start accessing these connector nodes, it becomes quadratic — x to the second power. And then at some other scale, once you zoom out far enough, it starts to become exponential, because of the hyperbolic curvature: the area you can cover as a function of the radius grows like a constant to the power of the distance.

![[fig-scale-specific-network-geometry.svg]]

So that, ladies and gentlemen — and whoever else, any other sub-agent composition — is scale-specific network geometry. It's important because at different scales we may have different geometry. Entities at different scales may perceive themselves as inhabiting fundamentally different worlds, with fundamentally different distance functions or growth functions relative to their environment.

That's the basic setup: we're not talking about something that is straightforwardly self-similar, even though there might be similarities across scales. The point is not that you are a fractal of sub-agents. Something more interesting and far more nuanced is happening here.

Think of it this way: some things do repeat, up to a certain scale, and then they break down. Trees are a good example. Some coniferous trees look very fractal — but then you go to the lowest level and it's just a needle, a needle leaf. And then it breaks down; there are no further levels. It's a line. Zoom further and it's a plane. Zoom further still and it's a volume. The geometry keeps changing as a function of how you approach it.

### Cost of embodiment

The second very important thing: I wrote an article in Qualia Computing — this is a relatively recent insight, from last year — which I call **costs of embodiment**.[^costs-of-embodiment] This has become one of the canonical QRI arguments for why digital computers are unlikely to be conscious.

This is a complex, nuanced topic, and I could make an hour-long or two-hour-long video just about cost of embodiment. But it is important for me to cover some of the basics here.

It goes as follows. When you build a digital computer — how we have done it as a society, to a large extent — we are offering them the eyes and the hands and the maintenance costs. We put them together, and we can see how the different pieces are connected to each other. And we do that *in advance* of constructing them.

In some sense, digital computer systems embody *our* embodiment. They're borrowing from our visual field, from our tactile field, from our capacity to reason about counterfactuals and visualize geometric spaces. They cannot assemble themselves.

That doesn't mean they won't be able to in the future. I very much expect that with the advancement of AI — maybe even within a few years, if not a decade — digital systems will be able to maintain themselves. If you have enough drones and cameras and sensors, and access to primary resources and the ability to process those resources, you cross a threshold where, to quote John C. Lilly, you enter into a possible arrangement of matter and energy that might be described as **solid-state intelligence**[^lilly]: these computer architectures can maintain themselves, they can see in what ways they're broken, and they have enough slack that this whole society of robots and AIs is able to self-sustain.

We're not there yet. And importantly, the way these systems are designed really is not designed for that. The internet, complex systems, the electric grid — they break down all the time. It's humans who are constantly battling against this entropy, and they do it with their visual fields, their tactile fields, their auditory fields, their cognition, their counterfactuals. All of that is borrowed from us. The systems are not yet intelligent enough to do it — and even if they were intelligent, they're a fundamentally different type of system.

Contrast that with a biological system. Think about this: there are enormous hurdles to being able to create something like a human. You need to self-organize trillions of cells, for them to figure out where they are in the organism, to differentiate and know — am I an eye? Am I a tail? Am I supposed to secrete hormones? — super specialized roles, *without* the benefit of having pre-existing eyes. Biological systems bootstrap themselves.

### Cancer and the combinatorial partition problem

Think about the existence of cancer. Cancer is much more common in older organisms — but my god, you have an example where a subset of the organism perceives itself as a different entity. It's able to segment itself out, scavenge resources, multiply, and eventually conquer and kill the organism — not by intention, but because of replicator dynamics.

What is going on there? You have a situation where you're trying to coordinate so many little cells and trying to make them act as one, and you have this crazy situation where if *any* subset within that entire system manages to perceive itself, harmonize itself, and experience the rest of the organism as an *other* to go against and parasitize, then the organism gets destroyed and doesn't survive.

In other words, it's not like a computer virus, where there are a few key bottlenecks — okay, you may infect the cache, or do a cache overflow operation, you may be able to do a memory hack or something like that. No. Biological systems are way crazier. Take the whole organism. The cancer could be *any* subset of it: one small cluster of cells; or a larger region that contains that cluster; or a sprawling region spanning several organs at once — or several such subsets simultaneously, each defecting independently.

As the organism grows, it's not a matter of unifying all of the cells into a bundle. You want to ensure that **every possible partition** within the organism chooses to cooperate with the whole. And this is a combinatorial explosion — there is a massive number of ways in which the organism could segment itself.

Again, it's not a simple fractal. It's not "at this level, these are the modules, period." No — you have to take into account every possible partition, of which there are countless possibilities. This starts to hint at the magnitude of the problem, and at what it is that consciousness is actually solving for.

You have a system that self-organizes and prevents internal partition defections — and then, on top of that, is able to create a map of itself. Your conscious experience is in some sense routing signals throughout your body and creating a model not only of your environment but of yourself: proprioception, interoception.

### Hypergraphs, not sets

That already hints at the type of thing these sub-agent networks or architectures are made of. Even this is only a slightly better approximation than bags of bags. It's more like a **superposition of possible partitions**.

Think of a high school, or a middle school — groupings. At the level of three people, these three kids get along very well. But at the level of thirty people, that whole group gets along very well — the band, say, or the chess club. And maybe at the level of three, only one of them is in the chess club. But that doesn't mean those three aren't good friends. The alliances and the groupings might be different depending on the scale.

![[fig-chess-club-overlapping-groups.svg]]

So this is a very complex thing, and if you want to model it mathematically — and I recommend thinking about this — you start to require something that looks more like a **hypergraph** than a graph. You need edges that can bind together many of the nodes at once.

And it's not as simple as encoding or representing a hypergraph using two types of nodes — a bipartite graph, where the hyperedges become nodes symbolically — because you require, in some sense, holistic, near-instantaneous interactions within the groupings. With the bipartite approach, you'd have time delays.

I don't want to get too much into those details. But that is to say: yes, you need some kind of hypergraph data structure to start thinking about how sub-agent architectures are put together in a biological organism.

Organisms that work and survive and can reproduce are able to find the conditions, to finely balance them, such that no partition is defecting against its complement, or against the whole, or against other partitions. It's mind-blowing.

I will mention, though, that in the typical person — and I suppose in the typical dog, maybe even the typical plant — there may be a significant degree of factionalization, where along some partitions you have enemies, genuine competition for survival or propagation. But it hangs together, because you're taking into account the different scales, such that this is not actually fatal.

Back to the middle school metaphor: sure, this group of friends and this group of friends may battle each other out and hate each other for whatever reason. However, because two-thirds of both of those groups belong to the chess club, they can't actually kill each other — because if they did, the chess club would also break down. And they value the coherence and harmony within the chess club enough that these animosities remain marginal.

### A generalization of game theory

Here's the thing: competition within us is actually essential for information processing, especially of certain types. Which means you need a lot of glue — a lot of ways in which this combinatorial partitioning makes it all hang together regardless.

Michael Levin, who looks at bioelectricity and is an amazing researcher with a lot of empirical data and results, has definitely shown signs of starting to think along these lines.[^levin] Because he thinks about how, within biological organisms, you require **a generalization of game theory**.

Within standard game theory — which for some reason people are very annealed to; people think of it as the ultimate way to analyze systems — no. Classical game theory is extremely deceptive. That is not how reality works, as far as I can tell.

Because one of the key options is not only: do I cooperate, do I defect, do I make this move or that move? One of the options you have is: **do I merge with the other, or not? And in what way?** Does 80% of me break away and merge with 60% of the "enemy", so that now you have a different faction? That is an option. That is definitely an option within us, within biology.

So you require a really intense generalization that is conceptually very non-trivial in order to make sense of how game theory applies to biological organisms. And that's without even considering things like ecosystems — partitions within individuals, across individuals — and considerations about inclusive fitness within a species, which come with crazy game-theoretical considerations that I think are wildly underestimated. They're extremely interesting and very far from obvious.

So that is cost of embodiment. We are solving something very complex. We're not just putting a memory system here and an attention system there. We're doing something much more intricate.

### The mereology of a moment of experience

Remember, I've talked a lot about what kind of object a moment of experience is. It's something for which we don't yet have conventional words — because the standard mereology of public language and of communication between humans is set-theoretic. We define objects, then we define parts of those objects, and then we treat the object as just the sum of the parts.

Whereas in fact there are a lot of holistic properties. Even within a standard metal, from a physics perspective, you have delocalized electrons. So we are a certain type of thing that has many pieces of information and yet is one. It's this inconceivable **superposition of unity and multiplicity**.

### Sub-agents as operators: energy minimization

The next insight for how to think about sub-agents is that they're **operators**.

If you think of sub-agents as different facets, different perspectives or points of view within your moment of experience — in most experiences, we actually evolved and learned to try to overlap them as much as possible. Part of the consilience algorithm is that the perspectives try to reach the same information content and harmonize.

They can't always. If you're experiencing a lot of internal conflict and tension, it may very well be because the perspectives are not able to come to a consensus on what is true, on what is reality. When there's a very tricky social situation, or a very difficult puzzle, things like that may happen.

But they coexist — and they're usually invisible, because usually they converge. They become more obviously distinct when they *can't* converge: when the problem is impossible, or very difficult, or requires a very complex search process.

The key thing is that what all of the perspectives are trying to do, in some sense, is **minimize the energy of the system**. At the fundamental level, that is one of the key things — if not *the* key thing — we're always trying to do. We're trying to minimize our energy for physical reasons, and out of that, valence gradients emerge.

Think of an object that is chaotic and unstable. If you analyze it from a certain perspective, you may realize: oh, I could flip it over, and then it will stabilize. But then you look at it from a different perspective and realize: huh, I could probably just lift the center, and it will also stabilize.

So think of the different sub-agents inside you as these different perspectives, which have different opinions on how to stabilize the object. And oftentimes those stabilizations are mutually incompatible: either you do this operation and it becomes more stable, or you do that other one — but you can't do both.

In other situations, the operations they suggest lead to the same place. That might be the case, for example, when you do a lot of jhana meditation, or even loving-kindness meditation (which I'll explain further later, in terms of how it interacts with sub-agents). What you're doing is stabilizing the system in a coherent way. Pointing towards the same jhana from different perspectives will take you to the same place. That is a very coherent attractor — one way of minimizing the energy of the system where the sub-agents are able to cooperate.

It may be very difficult, because the place from which you start may require a lot of hill-climbing before you can descend into things like the jhanas — because the perspectives may not align at first. But over time, eventually the sub-agents agree, and they're doing the same operation all across the board.

### Physiological needs as substrate

Another important perspective: biologically, a key aspect of the substrate of this sub-agent architecture is **different physiological needs**.

If you stop breathing — if you hold your breath — within a matter of minutes you're going to feel a lot of negative valence. Drowning, not being able to breathe, is very unpleasant, especially if you haven't trained for it and don't have equanimity around it. And that's because you're breaking a need that every aspect of your body has. All of your cells are eventually going to die if you're not breathing. So the organism is going to be very coordinated around that goal.

On the other hand, consider taking a status hit on your chess club — but realizing that becoming flustered about it would cause a status hit to your partner, or might embarrass your family. When you have a situation like that: this move over here deprives a whole sub-agent network of necessary dopamine, which is what keeps it having some influence on the rest of the network — and eventually that cashes out into less glucose. So that coalition of neurons says: hey, don't do that. And this other coalition says: hey, don't do *that*.

In both cases, there is something at stake — the glucose eventually, or the other key currencies: neurotransmitters, vibrations, integration, oxygen. Any of those, at any level of organization, in any of the partitions, may cause self-organization towards a common goal for the different partitions.

That's one important way of thinking about it: we evolved such that the different parts that are functionally distinct have different loadings of different needs. You can factorize the organism that way. Your liver needs something, your stomach needs something else, your heart needs something else. They all evolved to be able to send signals of stress that compel the rest of the organism to mobilize resources to actually give them what they need. And the organism needs to do this.

Because they are in competition on some level — not on others, but on some level they are — they do require carrots and sticks. ### Principle one: competing clusters of coherence vs. global coherence

Think of a system of coupled oscillators — say a line of metronomes. All of them want to go tick-tock, tick-tock, at a certain frequency. Each may have a slightly different natural frequency: the first at 10 hertz, the next at 9, then 11, then 10, then 12. And each is connected only to its immediate neighbors in the line.

If you have only 10 of those — and you can run simulations on this; in fact we have some of those simulations at QRI, look up "models of cessation" at QRI.org[^cessation] — if you have a line of oscillators where everybody is connected only to their neighbors, usually what you see is that they start at different frequencies, but if the coupling constant is sufficiently high, eventually they all synchronize. They're all moving in the same phase, interlocked with each other.

Now, what happens if you keep extending this line? If you keep going until you have 10,000 nodes, you get to a point where they *never* synchronize. In fact, you may have traveling waves of synchrony: maybe three of them are in synchrony, and that patch moves around. Or you may have spontaneous synchrony in some regions, and then it disappears — kind of like virtual particles. A little bit of synchrony here, a little bit there, and it goes away.

Now, if you start adding connections — further connections that make the system more interconnected, such that this node now also couples to that one and that one — two key phase transitions start to happen. Eventually the network becomes a two-dimensional or three-dimensional mess; it's no longer a line, because you're adding extra connections.

The two phase transitions:

1. First, you get what I call **competing clusters of coherence**. There are regions where the network is internally coherent, but there's no *global* coherence — not all of the oscillators are phase-locked.
2. Then, if you keep adding more connections, you cross a threshold where you go from competing clusters of coherence to **global coherence**. The whole thing is actually synchronized.

What I want you to think about is that a lot of what we are might very well be networks finely tuned to sit **at the edge between these two phases**.

### Principle two: symmetric vs. asymmetric mappings

Another one is **symmetric versus asymmetric mappings** in systems of coupled oscillators. This is also something we have online.

Say you have **two grids of oscillators — call them grid A and grid B** — stacked one above the other, like two layers. Within each grid, every oscillator is connected to its immediate neighbors. But now add *jump connections* between the layers: each oscillator in grid A is also mapped vertically onto its counterpart in grid B, and vice versa.

One way of thinking about this is as *layers*: one layer here, one layer there, and everybody maps along the vertical axis as well. Of course, you can make a cube if you keep stacking. A very simple concept.

There are fundamentally two very different things you can do here.

One is to make the connections **positive in both directions** — meaning layer A wants to be the same as layer B, and layer B wants to be the same as layer A. If the parameters of the oscillators are close enough in both layers, they may converge.

### Symmetric and anti-symmetric coupling

So: you have layer A and layer B, and when the coupling from A to B is positive, and the coupling from B to A is also positive, they want to synchronize. Even if the parameters are a little different, they may vibe together and affect each other a lot.

I should also cover the case where **both are negative** and they're sufficiently similar. What you get is that they end up in **anti-phase**. Where before both layers peaked together, now one peaks exactly as the other troughs. So both couplings being negative is effectively the same situation as both being positive — the layers are still locked to each other, just half a cycle apart.

Think of two clocks that are still synchronized, but pointing 180 degrees in a different direction. It's essentially the same clock — the color is different, but they're synchronized.

If, on the other hand, it's **asymmetric** — one is positive and the other is negative — that is a recipe for chaos. You have a situation where one of the networks is trying to *be like* the other, and the other is trying to *be different*. When you play with this in simulations, you see that it generates complex, chaotic, strange attractors, where the waves never repeat.

[^hash]: *On the metaphor.* The programming sense is intended. A **hash function** maps an input of arbitrary size onto a short fixed-size fingerprint, allowing direct retrieval from the corresponding bucket rather than comparison against every stored item. This addresses a genuine computational problem: checking the current moment against the whole of past experience is intractable. The phrase "whenever there's a hit" is the vocabulary of hash-table lookup.

	One qualification is required. Cryptographic hashes are constructed so that near-identical inputs yield maximally different outputs, which is the opposite of what is wanted here. The relevant class is **locality-sensitive hashing**, in which similar inputs are mapped to the same bucket by design. His formulation is consistent with this: the screen "looks *close enough* to something it has looked like before."

	"Overcompletes" refers to the retrieval stage. Hippocampal subfield CA3 is standardly modelled as an **autoassociative network**, which settles into a complete stored pattern when given a partial or noisy fragment of it — **pattern completion**. The full loop is therefore: screen state → fingerprint → hit → retrieval of the complete pattern → projection back onto the screen, where it becomes a further constraint the screen must satisfy. This accounts for the bidirectional arrows. It also explains "holographic": an attractor network distributes a memory across its connection weights rather than storing it at an address, so any sufficient fragment reconstructs the whole. *Hash* describes the retrieval mechanism; *holographic*, the storage.

	The hippocampal circuit has a corresponding two-stage structure: the **dentate gyrus** performs *pattern separation*, mapping similar inputs onto more distinct representations, and **CA3** performs pattern completion..
