## The layers of the debate

Very importantly, I think this debate has many, many layers. There's a popular-culture layer, which really comes down to something like this: on the one hand, if the brain is really just an information-processing system, doesn't that mean that digital computers will eventually be conscious? And on the other hand, you have people saying, "Yeah, but machines cannot have feelings. A computer is cold and hard and made of silicon. It doesn't have feelings." That generalizes to: okay, maybe machines could be conscious. And then people come in and say, "Well, there's the intentional stance; maybe you're just projecting that onto the algorithms." But nowadays in our culture, the buck really stops with very smart people who, for the most part, are doing AI research: because of all the impressive results coming from that field, that is basically what people pay attention to. By and large, among people in AI, as well as in modern analytic philosophy, the main views tend to be functionalism, the computational theory of mind, and causal structural theories, which I'll get into in a second. ## Bad arguments against digital sentience

First, bad arguments. It is absolutely true that when you try to convince somebody that digital computers can be conscious, they will say things such as: "You can simulate glucose, you can simulate a plant in your computer with software, but no matter how detailed that simulation is, it will always be ones and zeros. You never get an actual glucose molecule emerging out of that simulation." And I've got to say, that's a terrible argument against digital sentience. It's almost embarrassing to hear somebody being so confident about an argument like that.

The reason it doesn't work is that the whole point of something like computational theories of consciousness is this: sure, these patterns may not create a glucose molecule at our level of abstraction, but relative to the other patterns going on in the simulation, if a pattern plays functionally and causally the same role as a glucose molecule, it is a glucose molecule within that simulation. Now, connecting that layer with our basement-reality layer might not be trivial. There might be ways of doing it — for example, with VR and Neuralink implants and things like that — where you end up interacting with something that is, in a sense, just as real as the things around you. But otherwise it is very silly to say that you don't get physical glucose molecules out of a simulation. That's a terrible argument. And there are just so many bad arguments like that.

This starts the part where I give you some important background frameworks, so that we can actually make progress here. Then I will go over the classic examples and conclude with the most up-to-date understanding that we have at QRI.

## Marr's levels of analysis

The first piece of important background is **Marr's levels of analysis**, from David Marr, a cognitive scientist. He has a book called *Vision*, which is one of the earliest works on machine vision in AI. Its introduction is a wonderful, wonderful piece: if you study psychology or cognitive science and you don't read about Marr's levels of analysis, you're really missing out on a very, very critical framework. It's day and night between people who grasp it and people who don't, when it comes to the clarity of the discussion.

The framework says that you can analyze an information-processing system by dissecting it into three levels of abstraction:

1. **What it does.** Marr calls this the *computational* level, although most people would talk about it as the *functional* level. In other words: what is the input-to-output mapping of the system? And what is its runtime complexity? Runtime complexity differentiates two systems that may have the same input–output mapping: from the outside they have different causal effects, and that matters.
2. **The algorithmic level.** This covers not only the input and output, but also the internal ways in which the information is handled. What are the internal representations? What are the precise algorithms? What kinds of data structures are you using?
3. **The implementation level** — how it is actually built. One example is an abacus: it could be made of wood, or of metal or something like that, but at the algorithmic level of analysis they're the same; you may be running the same algorithms.

In the classical formulation, each level underconstrains the one underneath. That is to say, there are many ways of generating the same input–output function, with the same runtime complexity, using many different algorithms: just knowing the input–output doesn't fully constrain what is going on under the hood, so to speak. Likewise, if you're given pseudocode for a particular algorithm, that underconstrains the physical implementation. That also really matters. Even if you read "to add or multiply on an abacus, do this particular procedure," that doesn't tell you whether the abacus is made of wood or metal. And I think that, for the most part, pretty well-informed smart people tend to segment these levels of abstraction and treat them in a piecemeal fashion.

### Interactions between levels

I will add, though — and this is very critical, and absolutely essential to solving the big problem here — that there is sometimes **interaction between the levels of abstraction**, because particular physical systems make some algorithms easier than others. An extreme example: if you have access to quantum mechanics, you can run quantum computing algorithms that are specifically targeted at quantum-mechanical phenomena. In that sense, the algorithm does touch the implementation level. If you read the pseudocode for a quantum computing algorithm and you don't know that quantum mechanics is a thing, the algorithm doesn't make any sense. You will say, "Okay, but what about this step? You're claiming that this step does a factorization very efficiently. How would you do that in practice?" And somebody has to tell you, "Well, actually, use the Schrödinger wave function." In other words, in some contexts the lower levels give rise to particular constraints on the higher levels. That is very important, and we will come back to it. But classically, the levels tend to be analyzed in a piecemeal fashion.

### Where theories place consciousness

Importantly, we can describe different theories of consciousness in terms of the level of Marr's levels of analysis at which they assign consciousness. There is what I call the **being/function divide**: different theories of consciousness will say that everything above a certain level is just what the consciousness looks like to the world — what the algorithm is doing, in a sense — and everything underneath is what the consciousness fundamentally is.

- **Functionalism.** People use the term in a number of different ways. For the purpose of this video, I'll describe it as talking about consciousness as a function — as doing a particular type of information processing. It is concerned with the input-to-output mapping, and maybe even the runtime complexity of the system as implemented.
- **Computational theories of consciousness.** By and large, these say that consciousness is happening at the algorithmic level of analysis: it's not only the input–output, not only what the system does, but also how it does it. The internal representations are essential; without those particular internal representations, the consciousness would be different. So these theories care about which algorithms the system is implementing.
- **Causal structural theories**, for example IIT (integrated information theory). These are concerned with the algorithmic level of analysis, but they add additional constraints; you could say they sit at the boundary between the implementation and the algorithmic levels. For them, yes, particular algorithms have to be instantiated, but the precise way those algorithms are instantiated also matters. The information flow only gets quote-unquote integrated when the precise causal structure is in place.
- **Physicalist theories of consciousness.** These say that consciousness is a thing rather than a process. At QRI, that's what we actually advocate for. I know this is the underdog nowadays; I'll just say, wait 20 years — mark my words. Physicalism says that the world is made of things — particles and fields — and that particular configurations of those correspond to instantaneous moments of experience, while the particular structure of those objects corresponds to the information in the phenomenology of a given experience.

Physicalism fell out of favor in philosophy for many reasons, and the crazy thing is that I agree with those reasons. I actually agree with the criticisms that functionalists and computational theorists provide against physicalism. One of them, for example, is **epiphenomenalism**: in what way does the fact that an embodied physical state has consciousness matter from the point of view of evolution? It just has an additional label that says, "Oh yeah, and this pattern, by the way, is also conscious" — not only does it have this particular behavior. What does that add? And that brings us to the hard problem of consciousness: if you can describe something in terms of form and function, where does consciousness come into the picture? It seems really arbitrary and random. That's a perfectly fair criticism, but I'll have a really good response towards the end of this video.

## The Chinese room and lookup tables

Now let's get on to the classic problems. First of all, the **Chinese room**, by John Searle. I don't need to go into too much detail, because you probably know this thought experiment, but basically he says: suppose you have a box, or a room, with somebody inside who has a rulebook that allows them to take Chinese symbols, do operations on them, and return some other Chinese symbols. The person inside may not know at all what they're doing. But from a systems point of view, from the outside, the box seems smart; it seems to actually understand what is going on.

I remember that in a class on natural language understanding — a graduate course in the AI part of the computer science department at Stanford — the Chinese room was brought up, and the entire room thought, "Oh, this silly, stupid philosophy. When is philosophy good for anything? Let us just do the work." By a very large margin — the vast majority of the people in that class, and the professors — they basically assumed that if you have a system that understands from a functional point of view (you talk to it, it talks back, it passes the Turing test), then, because that's such a hard problem, requiring such complicated information processing with internal representations and a lot of context brought into place, of course it has some level of minimal consciousness at the very least.

But here's the thing: the Chinese room is made fun of, but I think in a completely unfair way. Why? Substitute the person inside the Chinese room with a **lookup table**, where, for any combination of symbols that you put in, it just looks up in that table which symbols to return. There's literally no information about the content of the symbols in the operation that happens; it's just a lookup operation. So, from the point of view of the outside, the input–output mapping is clearly not enough — because even within a computational theory of consciousness, a lookup table simply doesn't have the internal representations needed. The standard argument Searle presents is, "You never get semantics from syntax." A standard response from a computational-theory-of-consciousness person would be, "Yes, but enough complexity in the syntax actually gives rise to emergent semantics." I think that view can be defended to a large extent, but it still requires internal representations, and a lookup table doesn't have them. So, to make the point more stark, I think the Chinese room really should be replaced with a lookup table. Then you'll see people actually reckoning with this problem.

A very classic response I get when I extend the Chinese room in this way is that the computation did happen and it was conscious — but before, when you created the lookup table. Sure, that's one argument, but it still means that you had maybe one conscious experience that is now able to scale up its behavior in an unconscious fashion. You still have the issue that every time you use the Chinese room — the lookup table — you're not generating a new moment of experience. That alone, I think, is a very important point. It rules out theories of consciousness that just stay at the functional level, because lookup tables can take care of that.

## Solving all the constraints at once

At the algorithmic level, though, it's a little more tricky. And here is where I need to explain the actual desiderata: what is it that we are trying to explain when we talk about consciousness?

Here's an analogy. Suppose you're trying to get to the Moon: you have a bunch of scientists thinking about how to take a person to the Moon and back. Maybe that entire room of scientists is aware of Newton's laws of motion, in which case they will probably focus a lot of their energy on how to achieve escape velocity. And truth be told, achieving escape velocity is very important; it's a sub-component of going to the Moon and back. But if they're not also taking into account how to seal the spaceship so that people can breathe inside — because they're not aware that out there in space there's no air — they're not solving the full problem. They're solving a component of it. In order to actually solve the problem, you need to take all these constraints into account simultaneously. There are a lot of things that could achieve escape velocity, but at the cost of creating completely inhospitable conditions. Likewise, there are many things that could seal the spaceship but are just too fragile to be pushed through. You require all of it together. You also need the fuel to get off the Moon, because it also has a gravity well, and gyroscopes to get back into the atmosphere at the proper angle. You need to take all of that into account, or you're not actually solving the problem.

The same goes for a scientific theory of consciousness. I think Mike Johnson has the best frame here, in *Principia Qualia*: a decomposition of the problem of consciousness into eight sub-problems,[^principia-qualia] with the further claim that if we are able to solve these eight sub-problems, we will in a sense have a full solution to the hard problem of consciousness — with the caveat that we would still have to explain why there is something rather than nothing, which is an important aspect. A more succinct version is David Pearce's four sub-problems.[^pearce-four-problems] That's the one I tend to emphasize, simply because it's easy to communicate; when I do actual research and write papers, I go into much more detail, with the eight sub-problems.

### David Pearce's four problems

So what are the four problems of consciousness that David Pearce offers?

1. **Why consciousness exists.** Any scientific theory of consciousness must be able to explain why consciousness exists to begin with. That's number one.
2. **The nature of qualia.** It has to explain the nature of, and the relationships among, all the qualia values and varieties that we're acquainted with. In what way are color qualia and auditory qualia related to each other? What is that state space? That is a huge issue, and largely neglected, at least as a complete question. Maybe there's psychophysics about color, but it's usually not connected with questions like "What is the geometry of that state space, and how does it relate to the geometry of audio?" At QRI, though, that's a very big topic for us.
3. **The causal structure of consciousness.** In other words, how is it possible that we're even able to talk about consciousness? If we're just algorithms processing information, why do we talk about this additional thing — qualia — rather than just about information processing per se? Of course, somebody like Daniel Dennett would say that qualia is just a shorthand for information processing. We disagree, obviously, but that's a very important thing that has to be addressed. Within that problem, you also have the question of why evolution recruited bound states of consciousness for computation, or for their causal effects.
4. **The binding problem.** How is it possible that 100 billion–odd neurons, spatially distributed and membrane-bound, can simultaneously contribute to a unified experience?

## The binding problem

The binding problem is the thing that actually made me reconsider. Discussions of the binding problem are essentially what convinced me out of a functionalist or computational-theory-of-mind understanding of consciousness. The main thing is that a lot of functionalists and computational theorists of mind essentially dismiss the binding problem; they think of it as a non-issue. For example, somebody like Daniel Dennett might say that we tend to think our experience is much richer and more bound than it actually is. He has examples in his book *Consciousness Explained*: you look at a wall papered with a tessellation of Marilyn Monroes, and there are visual illusions where the detail of a lot of those Marilyn Monroes is missing — but you look at one of them in a lot of detail, and it gives you the impression that all the other ones also have a lot of detail. He says that's just your brain not even auto-completing them, but giving you the belief that it auto-completed them.

On his account it's illusory — which is something I disagree with. I actually think that when your visual field is, in a sense, auto-completing those patterns, those patterns really are there in your visual field. That is qualia, and it is very rich. It's just that its mathematical structure is something called **mongrels**.[^mongrels] Mongrels don't look like normal objects; they're statistical textures. And they have a particular qualia, and it is very rich.

So generally speaking, in the world of functionalist and computational theories of consciousness, there is a general dismissal of the binding problem. They will say something like, "You only really attend to one thing at a time, but you have the impression that you attend to multiple things at once."

### Attention versus awareness

Here it's super important to point out the difference between attention and awareness. People like Dennett and others who are eliminativists or reductionists about consciousness tend to focus only on attention, and they think that is what consciousness is: the ability to focus on a particular aspect of your experience. But actually, your experience is simultaneously composed of an **awareness field**, which is massively parallel and holds a massive amount of information — but for the most part you don't remember it unless you attended to it. Attending to things gives rise to particular local feature binding: to actually get a face, you need to attend to it, because it requires binding the nose and the eyes and the mouth in the proper way, and that requires attention. For the most part, the only things you can report about your experience are the things you attended to. But that does not mean in the least that you didn't also have an awareness field — and that field is massively bound. You have an entire bubble world of experience at any given point in time, even though nominally you're only attending to one thing.

I've also noticed that this is particularly difficult to grasp for people who are heavily on the spectrum, or who have Asperger's or mild autism. They find it really difficult to make the distinction between awareness and attention; they tend to identify only with attention and consider awareness illusory. But no, I think you do require awareness. What is happening at the center point of your attention only makes sense within the context of the awareness field around you; without that context, the meaning would be different. Even the meaning of a symbol contains many pieces of information simultaneously, and it requires that simultaneous coexistence of information, or the meaning wouldn't be there.

So what I have to say is: yes, Dennett, we tend to think that our experience is more bound than it is. But any non-zero binding still requires a very, very good explanation, and the set of explanations thrown around in cognitive science and philosophy of mind is, for the most part, completely unsatisfactory.

### Why synchrony is not enough

For example, people often say that binding happens through synchrony: that synchronous activation of neurons binds the features together. But why would that be the case? Right now there are neurons in my brain and in your brain that are firing simultaneously, but they're not coupled together. Why would simultaneous firing alone bind experience?

Likewise, somebody like Dennett will say, very, very truthfully, that there is no center point in the brain where all the information converges. And even if there were, that center point would have spatial extension. So no matter how tiny the point where the information gets concentrated, you would still have to explain how binding happens even within that space. Concentration of information wouldn't solve our problem, because you still have spatial extension. So it's a bit more complicated than that.

But somebody like Dennett, or other eliminativists — Brian Tomasik and other people in our sphere, Eliezer Yudkowsky, Luke Muehlhauser, etc. — essentially throw in the towel. They give up and say that consciousness is just an internal representation of your attention or something like that, and that for that very reason there's really no center point: the unity of it is an illusion. Part of the content of the illusion is its unity. Except that, of course, for it to be an illusion, it has to be presented to you in a unitary way to begin with. So they have it backwards. But that's fine.

Most importantly, though, synchrony of activity is definitely not enough for binding, for simple relativistic reasons: there's no universal frame of reference that settles whether two neurons are actually firing at the same time. And I would claim that if you want to use synchrony for the unity of experience, you need actual perfect synchrony. Why? Because what would it mean for things to be just partially, overlappingly bound? That's a whole issue in itself; it's called **mereological nihilism**.[^mereological-nihilism] If a piece of a process or a physical system belongs to an experience in a loose way, then in principle it could belong to more than one experience.

Likewise, you get an endless generation of consciousness. Say you have a system A, and you append something else to it, so that now you have system A′: system A plus that part. A lot of computational theories of consciousness would say that the consciousness that A alone was generating continues to be there, except that now you also have A′. I don't know about you, but this essentially means that you get a combinatorial explosion of actual experiences as you build up a system. And something like the **Multiple Drafts** theory of consciousness, or Brian Tomasik's take on eliminativism, is basically claiming exactly that: that there is no ground truth to what experience you're having, because there's a patchwork of overlapping systems loosely instantiating algorithms to various extents, and there's no on-or-off nature to whether something is conscious or not.

### Discrete moments of experience

How we think of it at QRI, on the other hand, is that there are discrete moments of experience. The degree of consciousness of those moments has to do, in a sense, with how much energy and how much information they have. But no matter how tiny a speck of qualia is, it's still in some sense 100% conscious, because it's 100% made of qualia. I don't think there's any sense in which you could say something like, "This is half nothing and half qualia." That doesn't make any sense, right? If something is qualia, it's fully qualia.

So that's one issue. There's also this endless multiplication of observer-moments as you complicate a system, in a way that just doesn't add up. Whereas if you associate consciousness with something like physical energy, a lot of physical conservation laws come into play, and you cannot do weird consciousness-multiplying exploits — like adding a tiny component to a system and then getting the previous system, together with the new component, and the new emergent dual system.

## QRI's approach: frame invariance

Now I'll move on to how we think about this. How do you actually get something that is **frame-invariant**? Here's where connections with fundamental physics become very relevant. Only once you take into account the four-dimensional nature of spacetime, and the entire space at once, do you see that reality is made of, in a sense, bundles of energy. And the important thing is that, from a causal point of view, having things that behave as units can have powerful evolutionary implications.

### Functions are observer-dependent

Let me just run through a couple of things. First, **functions are observer-dependent**. We think that what a system is doing depends on how you interpret it. Even if you're a functionalist and you say that consciousness is a function — something that does a particular type of input–output mapping — that function as a whole doesn't exist. For you to interpret the system as enacting that function, you need to take into account, in your own mind, all of the input at once and think of it as a unit of information, and then take the entirety of the output at once and interpret it as a unit of output. But that is happening in your mind. Physically in the world — and even from a computational point of view — that information is not a natural kind. The information you fed into the system doesn't stand as a unit other than in your mind. In your mind it has this irreducible quality, but not physically and not computationally. So that makes functionalist accounts of consciousness just not frame-invariant. They're so open to interpretation, and no interpretation is more true than any other; they can only be assessed in terms of the utility they may have for you. Without that observer, the function doesn't have a frame-invariant interpretation.

Another very important thing is that you need a principled way to solve the binding problem that also allows binding to have causal implications. For example, a lot of free energy principle approaches to consciousness have the problem that dynamic systems don't have natural boundaries. What you interpret a dynamic system to be is up for debate: it really depends on the particular way in which you're interpreting that system for it to actually have a boundary.

### The binding and boundary problems

I should clarify that I talk about the **boundary problem** and the binding problem, and they're really two sides of the same coin. If you start out with a universe that has a bunch of particles and forces between them, then the question is: how do sets of particles come together to form unified experiences? Where does this quote-unquote strong emergence come from? We claim that, in that framework, you need an actual mechanism of action to get that to happen.

If instead you start out with everything being unified — and that is usually how we think about it at QRI — then the universe is fundamentally unified: you have the universal wave function of quantum mechanics, whose behavior is governed by the Schrödinger equation. We believe in that; we think that's true. And that wave function inherently works in a holistic fashion: you need to take into account the entirety of it in order to know how it evolves over time. That fundamental unity then makes us ask: how do boundaries emerge, such that you have a moment of experience and I have a moment of experience, and they are, in a sense, different moments of experience? Or even within yourself, over the course of a second, you have many different moments of experience. How do those come about? So all of a sudden the question becomes: how do you get those divisions?

Being frank, most of the researchers in this area who talk about consciousness as a field tend to emphasize that field theories of consciousness solve the binding problem, because you have this underlying unity. If you claim that a conscious experience is an excitation of the electromagnetic field — like Susan Pockett, or McFadden with the cemi (conscious electromagnetic information) field theory of consciousness[^cemi] — then, as those academics emphasize, consciousness being a field solves the binding problem. What they don't tell you is that then you have the boundary problem: okay, but why is only this patch of the field what you are?

## Topological segmentation

And we have a solution. We actually have a solution, and the answer is **topological segmentation**. I've provided this metaphor before, but it's pretty helpful. Imagine a balloon, and think of its surface as representing the field of consciousness — which, in this panpsychist interpretation, would be the universe. It's completely unified, and in that sense you would be the entire field. So where do boundaries arise? Where do individual moments of experience arise? Well, suppose you twist the balloon — twisting it would be something like what the Schrödinger equation is doing to the wave function, to the field. There's a precise moment where you get a pinch point, a topological segmentation: a collapse of the surface. That provides a boundary. All of a sudden you have a region of the field where the only way to go from a point in that region to a point in the other region is to go through the pinch point — a one-dimensional, collapsed, compactified region of the space.

In this model, what we are — these moments of experience — would be these pinch points, except that we may be higher-dimensional in that sense. *(Sorry, I don't mean to sound woo, saying we are higher-dimensional beings or something like that.)* What I mean is that the wave function has like 10 or 11 dimensions, depending on how you count,[^dimensions] but most of those dimensions are extremely rolled up — compactified. We are the wave function; we are the universal fields of consciousness, which would be pretty high-dimensional. And what the brain might be doing is creating a pinch in those fields, such that now you have these compactified dimensions with objective boundaries.

## Pearce's desiderata revisited

The wonderful thing here is that postulating these objective boundaries actually solves a ton of philosophical problems. Let's go through David Pearce's desiderata:

1. **Why does consciousness exist to begin with?** Well, everything is conscious: the fields of physics are fields of consciousness. In a sense, we are now trading that question for the question of why there is something rather than nothing — why consciousness is the thing that exists. For that, I would refer you to my video on zero ontology, on why there is something rather than nothing.[^zero-ontology] We've talked about that to a great extent, and I'm not going to solve it right here.
2. **What is the full range of qualia values and varieties we have access to?** These would be mapped onto different properties of the fields of physics. For example, color qualia might have to do with the curl of the electromagnetic field. In some sense, fundamentally, what would actually exist is color qualia, and color qualia then behaves in a way that is consistent with the curl of the electromagnetic field behaving that way. So it's switching the ontology around. In a sense, I think what the qualia formalism states is that all these different mathematical features of the way the fields of physics evolve map onto particular qualia values and varieties.
3. **Where does the binding come from?** The solution would be topological segmentation — well, for the boundary problem. I think that's really powerful, and in particular it connects to and solves the last one:
4. **What are the causal properties of consciousness?** Why are we conscious to begin with? Why did evolution go to the trouble of selecting bound conscious experiences? The reason is that having topological segments of the field gives you affordances at the computational level. And here is where it connects, again, to the interaction between the levels in Marr's levels of analysis. Similar to how quantum computing is a case where you use properties of the substrate to instantiate particular algorithms, so that interaction can happen: the substrate properties constrain, or give rise to, new classes of algorithms.

## Field computing

Likewise, there's this whole field called **hypercomputation**, and in particular **field computing**. There are a bunch of academics working on it who, for the most part, are not taken particularly seriously, for whatever reason — or rather, they say these things, and for some reason it just doesn't impact the field very much. But Freeman Dyson, for example, was very big on the idea that field computing might be the new paradigm.[^dyson] Just as the legacy of Turing is huge in the field of computing, once we understand how to recruit fields for computation, we will essentially be transitioning from a paradigm of ones and zeros — digital computing — to a paradigm where pictures, entire excitations of fields, have an algebra: you can combine them and make them interact, and they will do interesting information-processing steps in a highly, highly efficient way, because you're harnessing the massive parallelism in which every point in the field is influencing how the rest of the field behaves.

A classic example of field behavior is how water seeks its own level. You can use that for computation: the solution to a particular problem might be the level the water reaches. More generally there's annealing — the Ising problem, for example: finding the solution to a constraint satisfaction problem with annealing by literally taking advantage of physical substrate properties. Because of the integrated behavior of the field, you're able to find solutions that maximize the satisfaction of constraints in a way that is more computationally efficient than you could by discretizing the problem and analyzing it piecemeal, in a modular fashion.

### Imperfect modularization

From this point of view, the problem we have — and you could say a lot of mental illness — is not so much that we're not able to put things together in our conscious mind. Because the field starts out unified, a lot of the issues we have come from **imperfect modularization**. Of course, there are disorders where you're too modularized; you can imagine some people who are just too compartmentalized, and that's also a problem. But from synesthesia to schizophrenia to what it's probably like to be a baby, super-synesthetic experiences are kind of the default. If you take a high dose of LSD, all the fields blend into each other and you have an uninterrupted flow of energy between them. That will mess up computation for the most part — you're not going to be able to fill out your taxes very effectively, because that requires a lot of modularization, working memory, and compartmentalization — but you're able to process other kinds of information more effectively, like emotional processing or social intelligence, improving equanimity, and things like that. So there are computational trade-offs.

### A new paradigm of intelligence

My very, very, very honest assessment here is that as we start to understand the ways in which consciousness is used for its field behavior, that will give rise to a completely new paradigm of intelligence, one that generalizes beyond what David Pearce would call the mind-blind IQ tests. Despite my personal interest in that field — I like solving puzzles and so on — that's really just scratching the surface of what intelligence can be. Ultimately, intelligence also has to do with whether you're able to focus on what's significant. And what is significant is hugely contentious. Of course, I believe that what's significant has to do with hedonic tone and valence and eradicating suffering; I almost think a test of general intelligence is whether you can identify that as one of the main plot points of reality. But the point is that holistic intelligence has to do not only with whether you're able to solve standard IQ-puzzle-type things, but also with whether you're able to recruit field behavior in new, novel, interesting ways. I very much think, for example, that the crazy things you see on DMT are powerful demonstrations of how field behavior is the driver of the computational horsepower of the brain. That's something for you to explore.

## Resonance and IIT

I'll end by saying a couple of things. First, **resonance**: we don't think resonance is enough to solve the binding or boundary problem. It is very nice that resonance in fields is a holistic behavior that could be recruited by natural selection, and we think that's a piece of the puzzle. But resonance is more something that generates functional unity than an actual deep ontological unification — definitely not at the level of fundamental physics, quite yet. But if we can demonstrate that resonance gives rise to topological segmentation — something I'm currently looking into, which has to do with the cohomology of electromagnetic field behavior — then yes, maybe electromagnetic resonance is actually the solution to the boundary problem. I'll be very excited to share the output of that research.

### IIT's minimum information partition

Another thing concerns IIT in particular *(sorry, this is kind of a technical footnote)*. I do like that IIT takes the binding problem very seriously, but it has a very strange solution: the **minimum information partition**, which is basically a way of identifying the temporal and spatial grain at which integrated causality is happening to the largest extent.[^mip] There's a quote by Giulio Tononi saying that where emergent causality is happening to the largest extent has "the highest claim of existence," quote-unquote.[^exclusion] But here's the thing: all the other integrated causality structures within the system being analyzed are still there. The thing saying that the minimum information partition is the one with the claim of existence is the theory. Physically, the others are still there.

That's very different from other mechanisms of action, where you actually do eliminate everything but, for example, the shortest path — as with the extrema of physics. Take the path of an electron or a photon: it always goes through the shortest path in terms of the time it will take. That's super weird behavior, right? Light always goes through the shortest path, almost as if it could smell its way around and just pick the shortest one. Super weird, right? For a long time this was a mystery — the principle of least action. But with quantum mechanics, and in particular Feynman's sum over paths, we can finally understand it: light goes through the shortest path because it actually goes through every path at once. Most of the paths cancel each other out, because very small differences between those paths create very large differences in the phase at which the electron arrives at the point of measurement. I recommend Feynman's *Lectures on Physics* and his book on quantum electrodynamics, but the point is that only paths really close to the shortest path — or in some cases to the longest path — don't cancel each other out. That is very different from what is going on in IIT. In IIT, it is the theory that says everything else can be neglected; in quantum mechanics, there's an actual mechanism of action that eliminates all the lesser realities — the paths that didn't matter. So when it comes to solving the binding or boundary problem, I think we have to look for frame-invariant, objective mechanisms that actually have causal implications.

### No natural boundaries for causality

Finally, I don't think causal accounts of the binding problem work either, because there are no natural boundaries for causality. Imagine, for example, two dominoes that fall onto a big domino and push it — and maybe they're of precisely the weight such that if only one of them fell on it, it wouldn't actually tip over. In that situation you have, in a sense, irreducible causality, right? But the problem is that you cannot just say that the two dominoes falling caused the big domino to fall, because you can also go further back and say, "Well, actually, the further cause was me pushing them" — or further back still, all the way to the Big Bang. There's no actual boundary at which you can say, "Okay, these are the causes, and everything before them is something you can ignore." In reality, causality works as a weighted network, and a weighted network doesn't have strict boundaries. So you're not going to be able to solve the boundary problem with causality.

This is a very big sticking point with IIT. On top of that, their solution to the boundary problem doesn't really add up, because it doesn't have a mechanism of action for getting rid of the other irreducible causal structures. Whereas with integrated field behavior that has topological boundaries, you actually get emergent holistic information processing, which is why evolution would have recruited it. It has appropriate causal effects, and it is objective: the binding there is something any observer would agree on, just as any observer would agree that there is a pinch point between two regions of the field in a balloon.

## Neural networks and implicit integration

All of this comes to a head with the idea that, for example, artificial neural networks are able to understand at this point in time. They're very impressive. If you use something like DeepDream or feature-visualization techniques, you can get the impression that if you turn the whole system into a generative system, you could argue that it's conscious, because it's able to detect these high-level patterns and is in some sense integrating that information. But I have to say, very firmly, that that is **implicit integration**. Implicit information processing is not frame-invariant: it requires you to interpret the system as doing it. It's not happening on its own.

How we model a moment of experience at QRI, by contrast, is that you are actually a **resonance network**. As long as that resonance network creates a topological pinch in the fields of physics, it is the pocket of energy that comprises your moment of experience. And all the layers of the hierarchy are in resonance: when you see an object — a face, say — the parts of the resonant network representing the eyes are in resonance with the surrounding networks representing the face. The same goes for the entire body, the object, and then your associations with it. All of that is connected through a network of resonance, and in that sense it is an actual glue that has actual causal effects.

Take something like LSD, for example: you see an eye start to wiggle, and you feel like, "Oh shoot, the eye is being disassembled; it's getting dissociated from the rest of the construct." That would be where the resonance network is failing: the things that usually attach the eye to the rest of the face are failing, and so you have this disembodied eye. And that matters. When you have these dissociated regions of the network, they're still globally bound — they have to be; otherwise they would just blink out of your experience — but they may be more loosely bound, in the sense that there are fewer actual network edges connecting them. They're still globally bound, but locally they may be dissociated from the common features they're usually bound to. And that has causal effects: you may take the wrong turn if you're driving, or fall for a particular visual illusion if you're not binding properly. So it has causal effects. The binding is not epiphenomenal. You actually require this integrated binding in order to drive yourself in the world and respond coherently.

## Conclusion: mind dust

So I'll end by saying that, indeed, if you want to go to the Moon, you need to take all of those constraints into account. If you want to solve the hard problem of consciousness, if you want to understand what valence is, if you want to understand what's going on in psychedelics, you need to take into account all of the constraints I laid out, simultaneously. Otherwise your solution will just be partial. It will be something like, "We'll just strap a bunch of fireworks to this chair, and hopefully it achieves escape velocity." But out there in space there's no air, so you will suffocate. Likewise, if you build a neural network on a digital computer, sure, there's some level of implicit information integration — but because there's no actual mechanism of action for binding, whatever you create is just going to stay at the level of **mind dust**.[^mind-dust]

Now, I'm not saying that it's unintelligent. You can have a very intelligent, non-sentient system. But there's a class of problems it's just not going to be able to address — in particular, problems of introspection and field behavior — and it's going to have some computational deficiencies relative to us. And vice versa: field behavior is not optimal for every kind of computation. Anyway, in the future there's going to be a seamless integration between globally bound experiences and digital computation, and they're going to be synergistic. But the part that is going to matter morally is the integrated experiences.

All right, this has been a little longer than usual, but I hope it was worth it. Thank you so much, and I'll talk to you another time, on another topic. Infinite bliss! Take care.
