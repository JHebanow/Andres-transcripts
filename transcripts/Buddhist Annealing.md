## Wireheading done right

First, wireheading done right. In 2015 I conducted a study where I ran a factor analysis on descriptions of the effects of all sorts of psychoactive substances, and identified six really big dimensions that explain a lot of the variance — three of which are very relevant to valence.[^drug-effects] The three valence-related dimensions are:

- **Fast euphoria**: basically what we might associate with stimulants and the like.
- **Slow euphoria**: relaxation and deep feelings of well-being and bliss, associated with more calming agents, such as opioids.
- What at the time I called **spiritual euphoria**: obviously psychedelic, and to some extent dissociative. Nowadays I might actually call it **criticality euphoria**; it has something to do with self-organized criticality and fractality in the resonance network of our nervous system, which is a rabbit hole.

With these three dimensions you can roughly characterize the way different substances influence your valence. And they're not mutually exclusive at all. Of all these substances, MDMA is one that has a very high loading on all three dimensions. Whereas something like 2C-E, for example, is very high in spiritual euphoria, but it's so anxiogenic, with so much body load, that it doesn't really qualify as very high valence on the other two axes.

With that ontology, I then wrote "Wireheading Done Right",[^wireheading] where I examined the feedback structure of how we feel euphoria in everyday life, and how it gets influenced by things like drugs, and also by activities that raise valence in one of these dimensions. One of the things that came out is that if you do too much fast euphoria, you will have a rebound of slow dysphoria. If you take a lot of amphetamines or something like that, then when you come down, and in the days after, you're going to be very sedated — and it's an unpleasant kind of sedation, not a pleasant one. The flip side holds for slow euphoria: you get high-energy, anxiogenic types of experiences, the complete opposite. So if you're trying to hack mood, for the most part it doesn't pay off to just try to hit these high-valence configurations all the time without taking the negative feedback mechanisms into account. That's one of the reasons we are so interested in things such as anti-tolerance drugs[^anti-tolerance] — things like ibogaine, which seem to actually up-regulate opioid receptors rather than just activating them temporarily.

So I came up with a different architecture where, rather than having these negative feedback mechanisms, in principle we could modify our brain such that fast euphoria, sure, inhibits itself — so you don't get stuck in a fast-euphoria attractor, gambling all day, every day — but makes it easier for you to then experience slow euphoria. Then you experience slow euphoria; it decays, it inhibits itself, but it makes it easier to experience spiritual (or criticality) euphoria, and so on. So you actually create these cycles. Perhaps they could be tuned to the circadian rhythm, so that maybe you have one cycle per day or something like that. Something like this may be a really good first draft of an idea for how to, in a sense, improve the reward architecture of our descendants, eventually.

## Neural annealing

That is one piece. The other one is neural annealing, which was written by Mike Johnson[^neural-annealing] — a lot of writings on Open Theory and Qualia Computing foreshadowed that work. I think that work is really good, and it's very much worth reading through; I do really recommend it.

A very key notion is that in order to have a healthy nervous system, you actually require regular injections of energy, because otherwise the nervous system gets *cold-worked*. It's similar to a metal that is being used a lot: micro-imperfections add up, and eventually you get a brittle metal with a lot of microcrystalline structure. Whereas if you heat it up above its recrystallization temperature, the crystals will start to realign, and if you cool it down slowly, you can actually make it become a regular, symmetrical lattice, which has a lot of very desirable material-science properties. What's fascinating here is that you are, in a sense, fixing a problem — there's too much entropy in this object, the metal is disordered — paradoxically, by adding more of it: heating it up and then cooling it down. Of course, cooling it down is where you're using negentropy: you're letting the energy dissipate. But it's still paradoxical that you can get something more ordered by first using a lot of disorder. It's an interesting thing.

I really do think that this overall framework explains a lot. It really does explain a lot, in my view, about how societies work, how individuals work, what kinds of cravings we have, what kinds of beliefs we have. Because ultimately, we believe there's a mapping between the structure of belief networks and the structure of patterns of resonance — and whether those are in consonance or dissonance with one another. So when you do neural annealing, let's say with a psychedelic, the low-level implementation algorithm that is going on is actually a dissonance-minimization algorithm: you're trying to reconfigure the network in order to minimize the ways in which it interferes with itself, causing dissonance. But as a side effect, when you map it onto the belief network, you're reconfiguring the belief network in order to take into account more information that perhaps you were neglecting before. The end result is something like REBUS — relaxed beliefs under psychedelics[^rebus] — where the annealing process also happens at the level of the belief network, not only at the physiological level. I think fleshing out this mapping is very generative, and it's one of the things we want to be publishing peer-reviewed studies on. I think there's a tremendous amount of promise here, and it may explain the differences between psychedelics as well. Anyway, that's a rabbit-hole topic for another video in its full length.

So, basically: wireheading done right — the main premise is that being happy all the time may be an engineering problem rather than something that cannot be done in principle. And second, neural annealing: how beliefs, as well as the configuration of resonant networks, update themselves with the injection of energy.

## What flow is

With that mindset, I went into the retreat and did a ton of meditation, and there are some fascinating insights to communicate.

Number one: I've got to introduce what **flow** is.[^flow] Flow has a meaning in psychology as well as in colloquial language: being in a state where your planning and your actions come together, with no dissonance or stalling in your behavior — you can just go from one thing to another fluidly. That's related, but it's not really what Shinzen Young refers to as flow. The flow he cares about is essentially any feeling of impermanence — basically, any feeling of change. I know this is extremely general, but this definition really matters, because what happens a lot in meditation and in these states is that people tend to interpret what goes on in their experience with a prefabricated metaphysics — for example, energy flows in the body. It is absolutely the case that, deep in meditation, you can notice energy moving around your body. But you don't necessarily need to postulate something like etheric energies literally moving around. You can think of it as a propagation of energy between networks: it's not necessarily that the same energy is moving from one part of the body to another, but rather that there's the illusion of its movement. That said, that illusion can actually follow equations, be mapped, and have a lot of really important consequences for what kind of information processing you're doing and in what way you're modifying your nervous system.

So flow encompasses things such as even the feeling of your heartbeat; vibratory flow; strobing flow; feelings of pulsing vibrations; things that feel like plasma waves moving through your nervous system; and also feelings of tension and contraction — for example, outward-flowing stress or inward-flowing stress. There's a whole zoo of flow phenomenology.

A lot of meditation teachers will say: don't pay attention to it, it's just a side effect of meditation; what you're really going for is enlightenment, nirvana — that's what ultimately matters. Sometimes they call all of these flow phenomenologies the realm of the powers and whatnot: it might be interesting, but it's a distraction. I tend to follow more the notion that the flow actually matters. What you do with it will accelerate or decelerate the process of enlightenment, or maybe even hurt you in particular ways. So I think paying attention to it really matters, and on the retreat I had a lot of hours to actually pay attention to this flow.

I think it's a really great signature of the melting of the structures of your nervous system, and it has a very, very tight relationship with what we call the energy parameter in neural annealing. Robin Carhart-Harris talks about entropic disintegration: you elevate the energy parameter of your nervous system, and things that were, in a sense, coagulated become somewhat fluid. They first become liquid, then eventually fiery, and ultimately gaseous, or even pure space. So there's this progression, and it's really energy-dependent. By modulating the energy, and where you concentrate it, different things are going to happen to flow.

## The five hindrances as distortions of flow

You can also make sense of classic things in Buddhism, like the **five hindrances**[^hindrances] — the things that keep you away from enlightenment, and also from meditative progress. These are:

- sensory desire,
- ill will,
- sloth and torpor,
- restlessness, and
- doubt.

There are lots of videos on YouTube of advanced meditators explaining what each of these hindrances is. From my point of view, we can even characterize them in terms of flow. Take sensory desire — craving. In the worldview of Steven Lehar, where we're actually inhabiting a world simulation generated by our brain, desires are patterns of force in the motor-planning space, which works as a force field that deflects the movement of flow. You're trying to concentrate on the present moment, but you imagine somebody very attractive, or chocolate cake, and the flow that is directing your attention will gravitate towards it. So attention has a lensing effect on flow, if that makes sense. Likewise, repulsion — ill will — has a repelling quality in the force field of motor planning. That's why, literally, when there's something scary in front of you, it's harder to approach it: in the motor-planning space there's literally a force field that pushes against it. I don't mean it's a force field out there in the world; that would be a direct-realist interpretation. It's rather a force field within your motor-planning space. Another place where you really see this play out is on dissociatives in particular, where the motor-planning space goes haywire and you get these strong feelings of pulling or pushing, or of gravitational pressure — and for sure, they will modify the flow of attention. So each of the hindrances can be understood in terms of the ways in which it prevents smooth, regular, symmetrical flow.

## Lehar's duality: standing waves and frequencies

Another very important thing — and this is an insight from Steven Lehar[^lehar] — is that there is a duality between standing-wave patterns in, let's say, a Chladni plate, and the frequencies of vibration. If you know the shape and the physical properties of one of these objects and you can see the standing-wave pattern, you can immediately know — well, with some math, you can infer — what frequency of vibration is causing those standing-wave patterns. And vice versa: if you're given the frequency of vibration, or the spectrum you're going to stimulate the object with, you can map that onto particular standing-wave patterns. If you give it a complex spectrum, the standing-wave patterns may actually be unstable and exhibit complex or interesting rotations or transformations that are ultimately repeating. There's a tremendous amount of diversity in what can arise in the standing-wave patterns, and there's a dual relationship between them and the frequencies of vibration.

If you start exploring flow, you will see it's true. Well, okay, that sounds too confident: I think it's true, based on a ton of my experiences in meditation. And this duality is super, super, super important for figuring out how to, in a sense, do productive things with that flow.

## The seven factors of awakening

One of the things I would very heavily advocate is to look into what Buddhism calls the **seven factors of awakening**.[^seven-factors] The Buddha said that any mind that seeks enlightenment should first develop the seven factors of awakening and have them very deep inside, as the default mode of consciousness, so that it can then go through the stages of insight without getting distracted. The seven factors of awakening have an interpretation both in terms of wireheading done right and in terms of neural annealing and shaping the flow.

First, what they are. There is one called **mindfulness**, which is, roughly speaking, paying attention to everything that happens in your experience — not letting background processes be left unattended, because they can do things that will ultimately distract you, give rise to hindrances, or take you out of your productive meditation state. Then there are two clusters, an energizing cluster and a calming cluster:

- The energizing cluster: **investigation** — probing what is in your mind, sensing its texture; **energy** — being energized; and **joy** — positive valence, high energy, similar to fast euphoria.
- The tranquilizing factors: **tranquility**, **concentration**, and **equanimity**.

Again, I would love for more meditators who have read Qualia Computing and Open Theory, or who follow the Qualia Research Institute, to tell me whether this resonates with their experience — or, even better, if you're going on a retreat, absolutely let me know if any of this resonates with you.

The seven factors of awakening have a fascinating interpretation. First, in terms of wireheading done right: if you find a way to get any of these seven factors on demand — "I want more energy," and whoa, all of a sudden you're energized, and you can do that at will — that is one tool you can use to stay in the positive-valence region of the state space of consciousness, and that could be very beneficial. Likewise, if you're overexcited, maybe use one of the tranquilizing factors so that you continue to be in the pleasant and productive region.

### Mindfulness and the energizing factors

With respect to neural annealing, these are the interpretations.

**Mindfulness** means actually paying attention to all the flow and not letting any flow be left unattended. That's an important ingredient.

**Investigation** is focusing on the texture of the flow rather than on its intentional content — what it is about. Maybe the flow is representing patterns of attraction and repulsion around, say, a traumatic event, and you're processing that. Investigation in this case would be to pay attention to how the energy is moving around, and not pay that much attention to what that energy is representing — to the way it is aggregating and coagulating into semantic content, which is sort of a distraction for this style of meditation, at the very least.

**Energy** is taking all the flow that is going on in your body and energizing it — plucking it. By plucking it, you illuminate the harmonic resonant modes of the flow. And again, because of the fascinating duality that Steven Lehar points out, roughly speaking, that is also going to manifest as a vibe: the vibe of your space is going to be dual to the standing-wave patterns of your flow. In this sense, adding energy is a way to illuminate what's going on, to get a sense of the extent of the flow. The other thing is that, because flow has various states — liquidy, fiery, gaseous, and so on — when you pluck flow you're going to be able to separate and identify them. Think of plucking a complex object that contains water and also metal and rocks. If the part you pluck is the metal, you will hear the ring of the metal; if the part you pluck is the water, you will feel the waves in the water; and so on. So plucking different parts of your flow works like a percolation algorithm: it highlights all the flow that is in the same phase as the part you plucked. It's a very helpful diagnostic tool.

Then **joy**. Once you know the eigenmodes — the harmonic modes — of your flow, joy is the judicious selection of subsets of them such that, when they co-occur, they are consonant. I'm sure a lot of people might be skeptical of this claim. It's something I knew in theory would be the case, but then I got to experience it — and I don't mean just confirmation bias: I really tried to disprove it, to find counterexamples. But in every case, it was true that the most joyous experiences I could have didn't come from energizing as much of the flow at once as possible. They came from selecting particular resonant modes of the flow and bringing them together. Even better is if you can get them to tune each other: if they're consonant, and you then focus on the emergent gestalt consonance that arises when they happen at once, that can have a top-down organizing effect on each of the resonant modes. You're tuning your musical key, you could call it — or really, the harmonic key of the resonant modes of your flow. So joy is really important, and you should practice it; it really pays off. You can get more joy once you understand this mechanism, because you can really focus on it, and I think it's more effective than just trying to get joy by thinking a positive thought. Though that is also very important — and I would also claim, of course, that if you pay attention to the texture of a positive thought, you will notice that it's actually like a pleasant chord, and it will have a symmetrical manifestation in the flow as well. This duality shows up all the time.

### The tranquilizing factors

Now, the tranquilizing factors. **Tranquility**, in our interpretation, is kindling very fast cooling systems in your nervous system, so that if a part of your nervous system is overexcited — strobing, or in a vibratory coherent state — you can hook it up to some of the fast cooling mechanisms in your body. The metaphor I would give is a river delta. There's a huge amount of river — the water here symbolizes energy, this huge flow of energy — and the river delta irrigates it efficiently: it has this fractal, scale-free structure. There's a lot of interesting research on how the most optimal cooling structures are not metal rods in parallel; they will generally have this river-delta quality. So tranquility is creating those structures in your nervous system. It's something you can practice, and you can also anneal tranquility into specific types of flow, so that you develop the ability to calm down quickly. Very, very helpful in life in general, but especially in meditation.

Then **concentration**. Concentration is really interesting because it's not straightforward. If I were to devise an equation for concentration, I would say it's the amount of energy of flow that is in synchrony, coherence, or harmonic relationship — in other words, how much of the flow is locked together in such a way that touching any part of it instantaneously touches the rest. That's why concentration is not only what we tend to think of in the West, which is single-pointed concentration. Nor is it a tensed-up state — that's also a very big confusion. A lot of people try to develop concentration by tensing very strongly. It's actually the opposite: you develop concentration by learning to relax in the right way, such that the pools of awareness come together in a concentrated fashion. So there's diffuse concentration as well as geometrically concentrated concentration. Both can be very high concentration, in the sense of having a lot of synchrony, coherence, and harmonic relationships, but phenomenologically they will look very different. I might even say, for example, that some ketamine states[^ketamine] are not actually single-pointed — they're very diffuse — and yet they can be very concentrated too, for this complicated reason.

And finally, **equanimity**. Equanimity is super important. If there's just one takeaway from *The Science of Enlightenment*, it's this: cultivate equanimity, guys — it will really be helpful. Equanimity is not interfering with flow. And this has tremendous implications for, for example, belief propagation. In the sort of duality we're exploring, when you have resistance in your body, it will generally also map onto information you are avoiding or don't want to absorb. Likewise, whenever there are strange beliefs, or resistance to beliefs, you will see a corresponding set of muscles in your body — or other aspects of your neurophysiology — that are tensed up and stuck in a self-reinforcing attractor that is unpleasant and not particularly beneficial. So equanimity has a strange dual effect: both in how you manage tension in your body — all the tensing-up patterns — and in how that manifests as resisting information.

A very important thing, though, is that Shinzen Young emphasizes that equanimity is not a top-down thing. You can't one day wake up and say, "I'll just have complete equanimity today," because a lot of resistance is actually subliminal. A lot of it is very ingrained in the low-level feature detectors of your nervous system, and it could be really early on in the stages of processing sensory data, or even of processing internal information. So it's not something you can just will.

That said — and this is a segue to a fascinating piece of this conversation — you can slowly percolate this top-down relaxation. At the highest level you say, "Okay, I will be relaxed today; I will not resist information, or pain" — which would be equivalent in this model. Well, not ultimately equivalent: there's a dual relationship between them. You can do that over and over and over again. The idea is that you have the hierarchy of sensory layers that culminates in very high-level percepts such as the self, with very high-level priors — which, in REBUS, for example, psychedelics might weaken the precision of. With equanimity, you're starting out at those very, very high levels and loosening the constraints there, and slowly, over time, that propagates downwards, and eventually the whole network resists less. Again, in this case, resistance will manifest as feelings of dissonance, which are inherently hedonically unpleasant — but it's also actually a matter of energy management in the body, and of information management.

So doing a lot of equanimity practice before taking psychedelics, for example, is something I would advise a lot. Take cold showers every day without stalling, without flinching — or trying not to; at first you will, but over time you develop the skill not to. In a concentrated state, that type of exercise will be really helpful, so that if you're experiencing something really crazy on DMT, you just let all of that dissonance propagate and use the rest of your nervous system as the way to offload it.

In order to go through this three-step process enough times, we have to be willing to look at the parts of our experience that are still solid and separate. Essentially, these are the deep knots in your soul. If you want to break that up with meditation, you first need to create these islands of flow, and then concentrate simultaneously on the islands of flow and on the knots of suffering and tension you have inside you, so that the flow slowly starts to entrain and propagate. Some of its big pieces may fall apart or break down, and eventually — and this, phenomenologically, is really crazy — it actually feels like you're plucking the thorns of your soul. It really feels like you're digesting these knots of tension.

Importantly, by increasing concentration — increasing the amount of coherence across your network of flow — something crazy happens too: these tensed-up patterns of suffering and dissonance that arose in some region of your nervous system can be translated, the way you can translate a knot in a rope. A knot in a rope can be moved from one region to another without breaking the rope, right? I hope that makes sense. So you can move the knots that maybe happened elsewhere — perhaps even from an injury. If you figure out a way, through meditation and perhaps even psychedelics, to move those knots to a place that is higher-dimensional and has a higher stress-dissipation capacity — or, you could call it, dissonance-cancellation properties — there they can be processed more efficiently. It's kind of insane. From personal experience, this quite closely describes a lot of the phenomenology I had on my retreat.

## Healing the subconscious

I've got to end with a reflection about the subconscious. I used to be very against the idea of a subconscious, because it just sounds very unscientific — also, Freud, and it didn't seem like they were doing anything real. But with time, I think it's valuable: there are a lot of really valuable insights, at the very least at the phenomenological level, having to do with the structure of the unconscious, and it has been rediscovered over and over again. For example, some strands of Buddhism talk about **storehouse consciousness**: in meditation, you can access regions of your mind that are usually not illuminated — they're dark, just not doing any interactions — and you can shine light on them. All of a sudden, a lot of the patterns of resistance and habitual responses that are stored in your nervous system, which maybe happened ten years ago and you haven't revisited, come up. And with these seven-factors-of-awakening flow techniques, you can heal them with neural annealing. You can energize them; you can highlight their consonant vibratory modes; you can then tranquilize the dissonant parts; and with equanimity you can, again, shift where that processing happens to another place where the processing is more efficient or more effective.

A fascinating idea that Shinzen Young has[^subconscious] is this. A lot of people think of storehouse consciousness, or the subconscious, as something that diagnoses you. You take mushrooms and your subconscious scolds you — that's a very common thing, isn't it? "Oh, you shouldn't have sent that message" type of things. Your subconscious is scolding you at one level. You could also call it the superego in that particular case, or what John Lilly calls the supra-self metaprogrammer — anyway, it's been discovered over and over again. So rather than only reading it or interpreting it, you could also send it messages to heal it. In Shinzen Young's case, the messages he advocates sending are contentless states of high concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity. In the symmetry theory of valence[^stv] and neural annealing, that makes a lot of sense: you're sending these packets of negentropy, of very high symmetry, and they will absorb the asymmetries in the rest of the flow, in the lower layers of the hierarchy. By doing that, you're reducing the net dissonance of your entire network over time. For example, taking MDMA and trying to heal a past trauma — we think it's something like that. The MDMA state is extremely high-harmony, and you're using that negentropy to heal the parts of the subconscious, so to speak, that have embedded in them these patterns of resistance and habitual responses that are dysphoric and asymmetrical.

## Harmonographs and the geometry of valence

The last thing I'll mention: when I talk about consonant flow in your nervous system, I don't mean it metaphorically. I literally mean the sort of patterns that a harmonograph draws. A harmonograph traces a figure from two oscillations, one per dimension, and the harmonic modes are the figures you get when the two dimensions are coupled together with a frequency ratio that has a small denominator. I would claim all of those patterns — sure, sacred geometry, the flower of life, et cetera, and so on and so forth — are euphoric. Why? Because they are consonant. And very dysphoric patterns might be the dissonant ones, traced with ratios that are not simple. This is a generalization of consonance and dissonance to two dimensions. But it actually generalizes further: to three dimensions, with three-dimensional harmonographs, and to higher dimensions — like on DMT, where there are implicit higher dimensions that can be in consonant or dissonant modes.

Again, it's kind of crazy to realize that the hellish DMT realms may ultimately turn out to be something like high-dimensional dissonance. These are feelings that do exist; it's just that humans don't tend to experience them. Likewise, the heaven realms on DMT — the high-valence realms — if you pay close attention to them, are also full of those very consonant patterns. Once you understand this, you can actually use the seven factors of awakening to direct your DMT trip and increase the strength of the attractors in the consonant configurations, which you can then use as sources of negentropy to propagate through the hierarchy and ultimately heal your traumas and your patterns of resistance — and achieve a slightly higher default state that is less neurotic and less psychotic, if you do it right.
