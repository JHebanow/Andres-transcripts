## The brain as a non-linear optical computer

Now on to the topic of the day, which is cel animation as a key metaphor to model DMT hallucinations. This is in the context of BaaNLOC, the **brain as a non-linear optical computer**, a framework we have been developing at the Qualia Research Institute for a number of years.[^baanloc] It goes as follows. The brain is not just a digital computer; it is not just a bunch of neurons connected to each other, instantiating something like a backpropagation learning algorithm. A lot of people have argued, in fact, that backpropagation is not happening in the brain, for a whole number of reasons — including Geoffrey Hinton, a pioneer in AI, who instead proposes something he calls forward-forward learning algorithms, which might be more neurologically realistic.[^forward-forward] There's also adaptive resonance theory by Stephen Grossberg, which is, I think, a more plausible account of how the brain actually learns.[^art] But the point is, there's more to the brain than just a classical neural network. In fact, you could think of the brain as having, in some sense, a field-based, analog computing substrate that is being coordinated with — used as reservoir computing by — a classical neural network. I think that frame is more promising.

There's this thing called local field potentials: microscopic electric oscillations in populations of neurons. In a patch of maybe about 10,000 neurons or so, the population-average level of activity modulates the electric potential, which goes up and down. There's substantial research suggesting that it is actually the state of the field the neurons are modulating that contains the conscious information. And I think that is much closer to phenomenology as well. Your experience is an integrated field of sensations rather than something pixelated. There's no such thing as mind dust, because the information is aggregated into a unified, bound moment of experience, so that you experience it all at once. So thinking of your consciousness as a field — or a pocket within a field, with boundaries — makes a lot more sense than thinking of it as just an emergent information-processing artifact of a bunch of classical neurons.

The brain-as-a-non-linear-optical-computer paradigm is effectively saying that there are two layers to the brain. There is the neural network layer, which is maybe relatively slow. And then there is the field-based layer of the brain, which is nearly instantaneous from the point of view of the neurons: you're holding the state of the field, which effectively propagates at the speed of light. Of course, most of the computations cannot be done at the speed of light, or anywhere close, because you're not going to be able to constrain it in the right way with the proper feedback. However, there's something like an emergent metronome — in fact there are a lot of emergent metronomes in the nervous system: some at around 100 hertz, some at approximately 1,000 hertz, and some at around 10 hertz, a wide range of orders of magnitude. One of those metronomes, for example, may have a lot to do with coordinating the signals so that the neural network can update the field in a fairly discrete, step-by-step fashion. But then the field will be doing something; the field is computationally significant. What it is doing, to a first approximation, is minimizing its energy. That's a function of the universe. It's not programmed in; it just happens because of physics. So the field configuration minimizes its energy, and in the process of doing that, it processes information.

## Laser chess

This has a good analogy with a game called **laser chess** — the analogy is going to make sense in a second. First of all, what is laser chess? Laser chess is chess that comes with a laser, so that you can use the laser to distract the other person while you play. *(No, I'm joking — that's not what laser chess is about.)* What laser chess is actually about is this: you have these fun pieces — a mirror piece; a beam-splitter piece, which the light both goes through and reflects off on one side; a double-sided mirror, as they sometimes call it; beam absorbers; and something like a beam emitter. You move a piece — all of these pieces have optical elements — and then you shine the laser, and the laser reflects, splits, or gets absorbed on the board. If it hits the other player's king, you win. It's death by laser, as opposed to death by being impaled by the enemy or something like that. In the commercially available laser chess sets, like the game Khet, the special piece, I guess, is actually a pharaoh.[^khet] I don't want to over-index on the Westernized version of a leader; we could even call it an earl. You're hitting the earl — which apparently etymologically means "leader" — with a laser, and then you win.

I think this is actually a brilliant metaphor for how the brain works. The neural network is setting up the constraints — the laser chess pieces. And then, every once in a while, you have a field-based wave propagation system in which the waves bounce off everywhere and converge until you get a standing wave pattern, and that is your state of consciousness. The standing wave pattern is trying to minimize an energy function, and in the process it is going to be doing things like highlighting features (I'll explain in a second how that might actually work mechanistically). The point is that you have a discrete step — setting up the board — and then, boom, you fire a laser, or electromagnetic waves, which from the point of view of the board pieces are nearly instantaneous. Of course, if you slow it down, you will see that it actually propagates at a certain speed, but it's effectively instantaneous from the point of view of the rest of the game.

I think a moment of experience is like that. A moment of experience is a standing wave pattern that integrates a whole bunch of features and effectively solves what is called a **constraint satisfaction problem**: it finds a way of stabilizing the field such that it integrates the constraints being put in place by the neural network. Those constraints may be things such as "there's an edge here", "there's a square here", "there's a face here".

Sometimes you see something that doesn't make sense — one of those Magic Eye–style images, or one of those AI-generated images that make no sense on purpose and are just a bunch of textures, what neuroscientists like to call *stuff* rather than *things*.[^stuff] (For context: when you open your eyes, for the first second or so — at least for the first 500 milliseconds — you don't really see things. You see stuff: low-level textures slowly coalescing. And then you use your attention to put the features together and turn them into things.)

No, wait…" Context in one part of the image may suggest that a region is in the foreground, while context in another part suggests that it is in the background. So you may have very inconsistent constraints that give rise to strange superpositions that are trying to resolve themselves in your field of vision. That is where the energy function of the field is failing to minimize: it is not finding a configuration that satisfies all the constraints, and that has an associated phenomenology — a feeling of awkwardly superposing partial interpretations. Escher has a ton of this. In fact, maybe this is the signature move of Escher: inducing the state of "if this were a coherent image, it would imply that there's a staircase that is constantly moving upwards" — you would be able to extract energy out of the vacuum just from an Escher staircase. *(Oh man, I really love Earl Grey.)*

You've got to kill the earl, and to do so you have to reason about how the laser will create a standing wave pattern on the chessboard. This is difficult to do. In laser chess proper — one of the commercially available games — it's actually designed so that it's not that hard. Here's an example. The pieces are designed so that they effectively only come at certain angles, and the laser can only be fired at 90-degree angles; it has to respect the grid, the grain, of the board. So typically, if you fire the laser at a beam splitter, part of it continues and part of it reflects at a 90-degree angle — again, by design. And because of the way the other pieces are designed too, the beam may walk around and eventually get stuck in a loop, for example. Because of this design, you can actually reason with your human mind about how the laser is going to move and spread on the board, and you can do it in a fairly serial fashion. In fact, you can almost think of the laser as another traditional piece — something like a rook, except that it can bounce off, it goes through mirrors, and things like that.

What I want to suggest is that normal, everyday states of consciousness are like that: the pieces of the laser chess board of our internal world simulation are effectively selected so that they click together, and the angles are 90 degrees or 45 degrees. There's nothing too exotic — nothing where, for example, the light might risk escaping and creating strange interference patterns, or go through polarized lenses, and weird things like that.

### Psychedelics bend the mirrors

However, you take some LSD and all of a sudden — remember the Escher-staircase feeling of failing to satisfy all the constraints at once? — that becomes ubiquitous. Psychedelics are all about those feelings: how do I satisfy all of these constraints at once, when a lot of them seem contradictory? You end up with very strange superposition qualities of experience. I'm going to say this happens because the psychedelic is in some sense disrupting how airtight the laser chess simulation is. In other words, the mirrors may get a little bent, for example. It's like when you heat a flat reflective surface, like an aluminum sheet. There's a thing called thermal expansion, and when you do thermal expansion on a sheet of aluminum it actually acquires hyperbolic curvature: it effectively becomes curved; it acquires negative curvature. Then, if you shine a laser on it, two things happen. First, the angle at which the laser reflects is going to be odd, and it will not fit nicely or neatly together with all of the other pieces.

But more than that, there might be cusps. Here's what I mean by that. This is something you can play with — maybe I can even demonstrate it here. If I hold the curved bottom of an aluminum can up to a whiteboard, you can see where the reflected light concentrates: that bright concentration is an **optical cusp**. If you're shining the laser on a curved surface like the bottom of the can, you may get odd places where the light traces out a bright curve, or takes a sharp turn, or has two special points where it is really strongly focused, and so on. Likewise, if you shine a laser on a surface that is curved, it will first focus and then become more spread out. Now, if the light is spread out and at a strange angle, but the pieces are designed to absorb the laser only at 90-degree angles, say, or at 45-degree angles — nice angles like that — suddenly the laser escapes containment. It starts to bounce off and interfere with itself, and it's going to spread out through the entire board in hard-to-predict ways.

In fact, there's a hierarchy of how weird this gets. The key thing to realize is that calculating how light will spread in a certain optical medium may actually be very difficult. It's very non-trivial. In some contexts it's easy, but in the general case it's a really difficult task, because you eventually need quantum electrodynamics and path integrals: you have to take into account all of the possible paths the light may take, and add up all of those paths with their complex amplitudes, to get a probability of observing, say, a photon there. More concretely, this also happens with matter — if you're shooting electrons, for example. And I actually think something like that is more realistic for the brain: a mixture — a non-linear optical computer that uses optics and electricity, photonics, the electric potential, and the magnetic field; it uses all of it. But I think it's usually fine-tuned so that interesting things don't happen — or rather, so that the interesting things are under the hood, if that makes sense.

### A hierarchy of optical weirdness

So there's a hierarchy of how weird things can be. Beam splitters are interesting because they can multiply the number of laser beams, in a sense. Odd angles are even more interesting. Curved surfaces — if you have a curved knight, or something like that — are going to be very interesting. Even more interesting are diffraction patterns. For example, suppose one of the knights is just a very thin line — it has a very thin spear, or something like that, thinner than the laser. If you think of the laser as a beam of particles, you would just see a shadow. But because light is also a wave, you end up with something called edge diffraction: light, even when it's perfectly coherent, even with a laser, partly wraps around an edge when it hits it. Isn't that crazy? It actually wraps around. Not only that — the shadow eats into the light. So you get a diffusion of light, a cone that spreads apart, like chiaroscuro, like twilight. In other words, edges do very strange things to light, even when it's coherent light like a laser. So that's another thing that can happen.

But there are even stranger things. You have birefringent materials, for example — materials in which light may split in two, because one of the two components goes through a higher index of refraction than the other. You may even have a variable index of refraction. I actually invented this idea a few years ago — maybe 2018 or so — of using a variable index of refraction to emulate 3D hyperbolic space. I think you can actually do this. The core principle is that if you blend material A and material B — let's say two transparent plastics with different indices of refraction — and smoothly change the concentration of the two materials as a function of the height in a slab of these materials, then a laser beam is going to curve as it goes through that material. So the laser chess pieces could have strangely shaped components with a variable index of refraction, and they may curve the light and then spread it. There's a bunch of things that can happen here.

So if you have this generalized laser chess setup, you may not be able to predict how the light is going to move around — definitely not with a human mind following heuristics. And you can change the rules a little, so that, for example, you kill the king not with a single photon hitting it, but with a certain luminosity — a certain amount of energy hitting it. Or maybe not even that: it could be something like irregularity. If you hit the king with a very coherent wavefront, maybe that doesn't harm it, but if you hit it only from the side, then maybe you kill it. I think game dynamics like that would be really fun, and they showcase just how non-trivial this can be.

## The laser as attention: the transformer analogy

Now, what I actually think is happening here, in a sense, is that we have an internal world simulation that has all of these optical properties, and we're shining a laser on it multiple times per second — hundreds, maybe even a thousand times per second. And the way this is integrated into cognitive processing is, I think, to a large extent for directing attention. In a sense, this might be related to the transformer architecture in AI, which brought about the large language model revolution. It's a fascinating architecture in which you effectively allow any part of a text to "modify" the meaning of any other part of the text. In one of these large language models — it's more complicated than this, but to a first approximation — you have a word-embedding representation for each of the tokens, meaning each token is a point in a high-dimensional space that contains information like masculinity. Famously, with word-vector operations, king minus queen is equal to man minus woman, because there's a kind of parallelism: one direction in that space makes everything more masculine, for example. And in a high-dimensional space there are a lot of directions, so you can encode a lot of properties. So in a large language model, if you say something like "here is Sparky, and he's a man", it will learn to pay attention to the word "man" in order to modify the meaning of "Sparky", and it might transform that word vector into a more masculine version of it. And it does this many times over; it iterates. Effectively, in one of these transformer architectures, every word gets a chance to look at every other word and subtly modify its meaning based on the other words — based on what it should be paying attention to, and how.

Now, in silicon this is an extremely inefficient operation, and there's a lot of evidence to suggest that the human brain is using information in ways that are far more efficient than a transformer is capable of. But the core idea — the core algorithm of high-dimensional representations of things being updated as a function of each other, because there's an all-to-all relationship in which everything can pay attention to everything else — that, I think, is actually happening in the world simulation of you and me. And this is where the laser chess metaphor becomes very relevant. You're setting up all of these pieces, then you fire the laser, and the light bounces around everywhere. In some places it's going to concentrate, and I think it concentrates where it does because a lot of these components are tuned towards that region. It's like a funhouse mirror, or a house of mirrors, where a lot of light can end up concentrated and focused in a particular region. I think that's happening. A lot of meditation moves — for example, "concentrate your attention at the center of your vision", or "spread your attention evenly throughout your energy body" — are effectively fine-tuning the positions, the angles, and the optical properties of the laser chess pieces of your world simulation so that the light all concentrates, or spreads out uniformly. Or — what's most common — it concentrates on the regions that are the most promising for disambiguating the meaning of everything else. You effectively have an all-to-all relationship in which every element of the world simulation is capable of modifying the meaning of every other element of the world simulation.

### Indra's Net

In fact, this is also closely related to Indra's Net.[^indras-net] Indra's Net says that the universe is like a series of droplets of water, where the actual picture that arises requires a kind of infinite recursion, because every droplet holds an image of every other droplet — including the images of droplets reflected in the surface of each of the other droplets. So when you have a bunch of droplets and take their reflections seriously, you get an infinite, hyperbolic house of mirrors. Of course, it doesn't truly go on infinitely, because of size limitations, the diffraction limit, and so on, but it can go pretty deep. What I'm suggesting is that the quality of your world simulation — what it is like to be you right now — is a stable attractor, a stable state reached once the light has bounced everywhere and every feature of your world simulation has had a chance to inform the meaning of every other feature. And I think this massive field-based parallelism is actually one of the core reasons why the brain uses data so efficiently.

## The self as the king

There are a couple more things I want to mention. I suspect our self-representation has something to do with agglutinated somatic sensations with which we identify, where the laser chess of your world simulation is effectively set up so that you're constantly paying attention to those components — the sense of embodiment in your chest or your face, for example. I think the sense of self usually involves fragmentary moments of facial information that carry affective information to update the system — "hey, how are you doing?" I don't want to trivialize the sense of self, because I think it's a fantastic computational discovery that really helps the organism a lot. And I do think that people at fourth path — people who are enlightened by classical standards and don't have a standard sense of self — have a better life, from a hedonic standpoint and from an information-processing-efficiency standpoint. However, you need a lot of fine-tuning for that. You don't get it for free; it's more like finding a needle in a haystack, because you've been doing a lot of search. Think about it this way: when you're doing a lot of meditation, you're slowly fine-tuning the pieces of your world simulation so that you tend to orbit more states of high coherence, where the light is evenly spread out. Not only that, you're also learning to have the light more spread out and still be functional, which takes time. It takes a lot of fine-tuning for all of these elements to be able to abide in that state in a functional way.

But typically speaking, I think the sense of self is computationally really powerful, and it's not easy to explain or articulate exactly what it's doing. My sense is that it is a special token, or cluster of tokens, whose meaning is getting modified by the other tokens — again, in this recursive fashion. A lot of information processing for a human has to do with: here's a given situation — how does it impact my sense of self? Your sense of self is one of the tokens, with a high-dimensional vector that includes things like affective information — how good or bad you're feeling, your disposition towards something — and, based on the context, the features of the scene modify your sense of self. Now, somebody who's enlightened doesn't do that anymore. But that means the whole system needs to figure out a different way to orient itself, to be able to update on a global sense of how good or bad you're doing at a given moment in time.

But to a first approximation, I'm going to say that the sense of self is like the king in the laser chess metaphor. Why? Because we're constantly trying to protect it. Suppose the circumstances are such that you predict your sense of self is going to get hurt — and I don't mean this only in the physical sense of your body getting hurt in an accident or something like that; your sense of self can also be hurt socially. You may experience a downgrade in your social standing in a community, and that will hurt. When we predict that the sense of self is going to get hurt, the whole system releases dissonant patterns. It makes you feel bad; it causes negative valence. So I think there is a reasonable metaphor here: if the lasers get hyper-concentrated on your sense of self, that hurts. Somebody who's enlightened is almost as if they don't have a king anymore — they don't have an earl in their laser chess. There might be some computational deficits, but on the whole I think that makes them more resilient, and there are probably fewer ways to hack them, or jailbreak them, so to speak.

## Fourier optics and cel animation

Anyway, that's a lot. Now I want to get into the juiciest component here. I think a lot of really intelligent processing is happening in this laser chess conception of your world simulation. One example: you can do analog Fourier transforms with a trick using lenses and optics. You can actually filter out the high frequencies, or filter out the low frequencies.[^fourier-optics] It's really stunning that this is possible at all, but it really seems to be the case.

And the way the world simulation is rendered, I'm going to say, has a really good metaphor in cel animation. So, just to keep everything on the table: you have the laser chess metaphor, and now I'm going to introduce the **cel animation** metaphor. Cel animation is a technique invented in the 20th century to make cartoons. You can imagine it takes a lot of time to draw each frame of a cartoon. So if you can get away with drawing only the things that are changing — and maybe not that many things change from one moment to the next — then you can save a lot on animator costs and actually make Disney profitable, or something like that. How it works is that you have transparent celluloid sheets, and you paint on those sheets, so you can keep the background the same. In fact, the background behind me isn't changing; it could just be a static picture. Whereas if I'm very still, if I'm only moving my lips, and maybe I get surprised all of a sudden, you might only need to redraw those parts of my face. So you can get away with big semantic changes in the scene — the classic little droplet of sweat appearing on an anime character. So cheap! You get emotional expressivity for so cheap.

But here's the other thing. The way cel animation is constructed is specifically for humans: it is going to emphasize changes in things that are semantically relevant for a human. And that is actually fourth-wall breaking to some extent. If you add noise to a cel animation production line, it's not going to be like adding noise to a video of a physics simulation. In a physics simulation — say you're watching how waves move around in a bucket of water, and you add rain on top of that — maybe the waves become noisier. That's a very physics-based alteration. But if instead you add noise to a cel animation process, it will be something like: somebody is at a store moving around, and then all of a sudden their lips become upside down, but nothing else does. Or they make a wrong expression — something that doesn't fit the semantic content. Or an object, like a ruler, appears — boom — and then disappears. So it's not that things nature carves at its joints are changing in a natural way. The cel animation was constructed with human semantics in mind, so the weird things that happen in the cartoon are going to be semantically strange and unusually relevant for the human mind. You may all of a sudden see somebody insult somebody else at random, or see a heart somewhere at random. Those are not things that would happen if you were just adding noise to a physical scene. This is more like adding noise to a human mind that is fine-tuned to process information in a semantically meaningful way.

### DMT hallucinations as cel animation artifacts

So I am going to suggest that what happens on DMT, in a sense, is that you're overexciting the brain as a non-linear optical computer, and as a consequence, the artifacts you experience are effectively something like cel animation artifacts. We do have something like a physics engine inside us, I think, that uses physical fields to minimize energy and so on, and you can have some exquisitely beautiful renditions of it — fluid dynamics in a DMT hallucination, for example. But it's mixed in with something much more semantically meaningful, much more about the human interface of perception. So a hallucination on DMT may start off as waves of water doing something physically quite believable, maybe instantiating a physical instability in a really believable way, but then the sea gets turned into a bunch of emojis, for example. Then you have wave dynamics of emojis, and very weird things may happen. For example, you may have an algebra of emojis interacting with each other, where, let's say, whenever a happy face and an unhappy face touch, you get a surprised face. On a DMT hallucination you may get streams of emojis interacting with each other and updating each other in what appear to be semantically meaningful ways. That, I think, is cel animation: we have internal gestalt representations that are effectively layers being put on top of the screen of consciousness, as it were. In a bit I'm going to explain how I think that actually works — it's more sophisticated than that — but to a first approximation, there's a unified field of perception on which you're slapping layers in order to modify the meaning.

But then, on top of that, once you've put down the cel elements, you shine the light, and the light gives every element of the scene an opportunity to see every other element of the scene. You have an all-to-all, transformer-like, attention-based transformation of the scene, which is a form of **hypercomputation**. I'm very, very bullish on the idea that the brain — and especially something like DMT hallucinations — is tapping into a much more advanced kind of computation than classical neural networks, classical computers, or even massively parallel processors. We're talking about something else: setting up the chessboard, the cel animation, then turning on the lights and having the fields of physics minimize the energy in a way that is adaptive for information processing. It's much more sophisticated.

### Holographic and recursive cels

And then other very weird things happen. What to some extent showcases the extraordinary computational potential of an analog, cel-animation-based hypercomputer is holography. Each of these cels is not just flat. It's not even just a classic cartoon — although you may experience a classic cartoon as well on DMT or on Salvia. More commonly, they actually have a depth map associated with them. So if something appears all of a sudden — let's say a hand — it's not just a flat hand. On DMT it will have depth. It actually gives the impression that you're traveling between dimensions, or foliations in some kind of hyperspace, because you may suddenly see a hand appear and then disappear. That's very common on DMT: things appearing and disappearing. It gives you the impression that you're a Flatland character moving across layers: suddenly there's a hand in this layer, and then there isn't. I think that's better — most parsimoniously and prosaically — explained in terms of something like cel animation principles for hypercomputation than by actually moving through higher dimensions. I'm still open to that possibility, but it would require quite a bit of evidence.

These cels are holographic, meaning that even if you can stabilize the hand for a moment, it's not just going to be a flat surface. It is something that integrates multiple perspectives at once. That, by the way, I think is one of the key hypercomputational properties of our world simulation: it works by generating holograms — fragments of a scene that integrate several perspectives at once — and then stitching those holograms together to create a scene that is consistent and coherent across the board. The computational potential here is enormous, because each of these holograms effectively encodes a particular perspective. One of the things that happens on psychedelics, I think, is that you end up with an inconsistent set of perspectives — inconsistent points of view — something we call **point-of-view fragmentation**: there's a whole bunch of different points of view, but there's no unifying point of view from which all of them make sense. It briefly relaxes the constraint-satisfaction requirements of the system, and you can have a superposition of multiple perspectives.

But on top of that, something even more general is happening: the cels can actually reference each other. You may end up, for example, hallucinating a window that shows a perspective of the scene happening on your left, let's say, but from above. So you may have this very fascinating configuration in which you can see a scene from two points of view at once — as if you could split your spirit, or as if you had a camera somewhere else and were projecting its view through a teleprompter-like transparency to create a superposition of otherwise incoherent perspectives. In other words, these holograms, these cel animation elements, can reference each other and recursively create branching structures that effectively change the topology of the entire field. If all of a sudden you can arrive at the same place by entering the window and then moving downwards, that means there is an additional loop in the topology of your field of consciousness. And this can become extremely non-trivial, especially at higher doses. By the time you get to an Indra's Net style of world simulation, where everything reflects everything else, I think that's an extremely non-trivial topology. We're talking about things like a hyperbolic manifold, where there's an infinite number of ways of arriving at one point by doing a loop through the system. And the field is still going to be trying to minimize its energy, which gives rise to really sophisticated mathematical properties that we have barely explored.

## Recap

I think that's most of it. Just to recap briefly: I think we're going to get a lot of mileage out of thinking of the brain as a non-linear optical computer with two steps. It has a slow step — the neural network component — which is all about setting up the non-linear optical elements. And then you have a very fast step, like shining the laser, in which the light goes through the entire system and stabilizes into a standing wave pattern. That allows, and encourages, you to add more energy to the regions that deserve more attention, which is going to be context-specific. It allows an all-to-all interaction: you're not just seeing how this part interacts with that part, and how that part interacts with another; you're seeing how every part interacts with every other part, because you're tapping into the path-integral nature of physics. You're actually allowing the light and the electrons to go through every possible path in superposition, and the actual state of consciousness you end up in is the superposition of all possible paths. That is something that can be utilized and recruited for hypercomputation, and it is behind why I think the brain is vastly more efficient than something like today's transformer architecture. And more than that, in exotic states of consciousness, these cel animation types can reference each other. They're holographic and recursive, and they may have genuinely non-trivial optical properties.

## A demonstration with polarizing filters

Actually, here's one thing I prepared to show you. I have two polarizing filters. The first lets through only light polarized in one direction, and the second only light polarized in the perpendicular direction. Of course, if you block all the light polarized in one direction and then block the light polarized in the other, no light goes through. And logically, this shouldn't change if you add a third filter — and indeed it doesn't when the third filter goes in front of or behind the pair. But — and this is a uniquely quantum mechanical effect — if you put the third filter in between, light gets through. Let me show you. There you go: by inserting the third filter between the other two, I can actually enable transparency. How is this possible? I'm going to suggest that a lot of the really exotic stuff you experience on DMT — gestalts interacting in very, very exotic ways, superpositions, infinite mirrors, invisibility cloaks, conditional invisibility where something is invisible only next to something else but not away from it — is based on very, very non-trivial optical properties of the system: things like polarizing filters interacting with one another, collapsing polarizing filters.

## Speculation on the substrate: microtubules and liquid crystals

The last thing I will mention — yes, this is very speculative, but it is grounded in a lot of phenomenology and a lot of thinking, and it is ultimately testable — is that I suspect that, at the implementation level, what underlies this non-linear optical computer setup may be something like actual microtubules. Even a cell membrane behaves like a liquid crystal, and people are finding out more and more roles that liquid crystals play in biology every day. The cytoskeleton, with its microtubules, is a liquid crystal. And I suspect that one of the things DMT is doing, for example, is not only changing how neurons fire and how they communicate with one another; through intracellular signaling it may be changing the structure of the microtubule lattice underneath, such that on DMT some of those cells may end up with a crystalline structure and effectively work like a laser — so that when you shine light on them, they might focus electromagnetic waves, or actually work as a mirror, because you're aligning all of the liquid crystals. So you may go from somewhat opaque to completely opaque, or to working as a reflector, a beam splitter, or an optical fiber.

Anyway, I strongly suspect that non-linear optics will show up all over the place once we improve the science of phenomenology. With that, thank you so much for tuning in. I'll talk to you about another topic on another day. Infinite bliss!
