## The meta-problem of consciousness

First of all, what is the **meta-problem of consciousness**? This is not just the hard problem — "how can form and structure give rise to subjectivity?" — but rather: why is it that we find consciousness mysterious and so hard to explain? Why do we stumble over it? We have so many beautiful theories of, for example, how particles behave, how plasma behaves, chemistry, biology — even AI. We know how to process an image, extract features, and say "there's a face in here." But somehow the fact that we have qualia and bound experiences feels genuinely mysterious, in the way it interacts with your world model. It's not that consciousness itself feels mysterious as it is, for the most part; it's really that our model of the world fails to account for it in some way.

Some people might say that science and religion live in different spaces — different *magisteria*, as Stephen Jay Gould would say[^magisteria]. But there's a very simple reason why this is not the case, and why everything does come down to the same empirical basis: all theories are ultimately evaluated by the same criterion, namely, are they good at predicting experiences — what experiences you're going to have? When I say "when I die, I'm going to go to such-and-such heaven," that's a claim; I'm making a prediction about my future experiences. Even though the epistemological handles on such a claim are different, it is still of the same fundamental kind as saying, "if I turn on the stove and put my hand on the red-hot surface, I'm going to feel a lot of pain qualia in my hand." This is an empirical claim that relates to experience. In fact, the very word *empirical*, etymologically, ties back to relating to experience. So the unity of experience is empirical. The features of experience are empirical. And the ways in which experiences relate to each other are all empirical matters.

Consciousness is very puzzling because, with our current frameworks, we can't predict the fact that a system like us would be conscious in the sense of having a bound, qualia-filled world simulation. So the question becomes: what kind of systems could do this? And among the systems that could, which ones would actually be confused about their own nature, and which ones would have some chance of figuring out what they are, more fundamentally?

So what I'm going to talk about today is really these two legs of what I hope will eventually become a complete explanation for the meta-problem of consciousness. First: what kind of dynamical systems — or, in a more abstract sense, computational systems, in the very general sense — could conceivably give rise to moments of experience? And second: within those systems, which ones would actually have some conception of consciousness, and maybe the hope of achieving self-awareness, metacognition, and ultimately deciphering the very nature of consciousness itself?

## The Reality of Wholes: three tiers of computational systems

On the first part: this is where, in recent blog posts — first of all in a Substack article called **The Reality of Wholes**[^reality-of-wholes] — I break down three tiers of computational systems, very broadly, and then argue that, based on the fact of experience alone, we must be in a tier-three universe (a tier-three kind of existence or ontology). And we can know this for a fact, however tempting it may be to believe otherwise.

### Tier one: cellular automata

Tier one is, let's say, the standard Conway's Game of Life cellular automaton, where you have one universal clock. All of the states in the grid have a certain time assigned to them and a certain state at that time. The way you evolve the system is that you look at every one of the buckets — in this case, the content of a bucket could be a one or a zero — then you look at its neighbors, and based on a series of rules you update the state of the central bucket as a function of its neighbors. The classic Conway's Game of Life straddles a balance: if there are too many "on" buckets around you, you can't survive, and if there are too few, you also can't survive. So the patterns that survive are those in the sweet spot of how many of the buckets around them are active. That produces all kinds of incredible emergent patterns.

But here's the thing: strictly speaking, anything that arises in a tier-one system will, by its very construction, always be nothing more and nothing less than the sum of its parts. You can apply something like integrated information theory to analyze the patterns that emerge in that world. However, any notion of integrated information that emerges there is really a convenient or economical coarse-graining of the system — one that lets you compress what is happening in a way that's convenient for your own tracking of it. The moment you see a vast empty space with a tiny glider, and you have the glider in your repertoire of learned patterns, you can infer that it will continue to be a vast empty space with just one glider moving. So it lets you compress what's happening and predict your own experiences more effectively. But strictly speaking, at no point is there any integrated information in a strong sense — only in the coarse-graining sense, or in the sense that, for patterns of this size and from this perspective, this way of looking at them makes it seem like the whole is greater than the sum of the parts.

By construction, the largest unit of information that exists in this system is either the system as a whole — the entire grid at once — or any given bucket and its neighborhood, at most. There's no update anywhere in the system that uses any more information than that. So there's no way to draw a boundary around a region of Conway's Game of Life and say "this is a bound experience," because you don't have the necessary criteria: you don't have any boundary-making mechanism, and on top of that, the boundaries are not themselves causally significant in any way. Evolution would have no reason to recruit them for information processing. Which is not to say there isn't fascinating evolution happening in this type of system — and, as we'll see in a second, there might even be observers in it, for a certain notion of an observer.

### Tier two: process physics

The second type of system is what you'll find in the literature as **process physics**[^process-physics]. The idea: you get rid of the universal clock. All interactions are completely local. Physicists are actually very motivated to do this so they can move beyond Newtonian mechanics — this is the whole Wolfram project of asking whether we can find general relativity or Lorentz invariance in certain types of cellular automata. You're not going to do that in Conway's Game of Life, because of the universal time and the updates happening in parallel, synchronously. In reality, there's no universal plane of simultaneity; it's entirely relative, entirely based on local conditions.

Interesting things can happen when you have sophisticated rules that act locally, where you have not a fixed window but a window that grows with the number of connections each bucket has. Regions of this network that are more interconnected have more things happening in them. If each node has an internal clock, and based on that internal clock it makes updates, and the number of updates it makes advances its internal clock, then you can expect that where the network is very dense, a lot of time is happening, so to speak. From that kind of reasoning you may be able to reconstruct time-dilation effects and the strange relativistic effects that happen when you have very contrasting speeds.

The problem here is that the unit continues to be fixed[^fixed-buckets]. You have these fixed buckets, and that's where all the information is. There's no moment where you get an aggregation of information such that the whole becomes more than the sum of the parts. Even here, at every single step, what happens next is still exactly the sum of the parts plus the rule set. Because that rule set is fixed and not variable, you don't have the capacity for a larger holistic system that acts as a unit. It's still just individual local rules. One important caveat: you can modify these systems a little and turn them into tier-three systems without realizing it, in which case you do get genuine holism — but usually you'll be able to spot the point at which you're subtly no longer in a tier-two system.

### Tier three: a general (holistic) computer

A tier-three system, to be very explicit, has actual holistic rules. Think of it as a kind of computer — but a *general* type of computer. A general computer allows arbitrary, physically integrated, unitary states as inputs, as internal steps of processing where these states are combined, fused, copied, and mutated, and as outputs. So in some sense, maybe there's a general quantum computer that can be built that can actually do everything this universe can do. David Deutsch has talked about this[^deutsch] — even though, for some reason, his work is actually used to justify the more conventional computationalist perspective. But in reality, he's talking about how you could get general physical processes to be emulated in a general quantum computer. And that, I think, has legs. It tracks, because you have these integrated physical states that you're using as your units of processing — your very ontology. A classical computer will never deliver, as an output, a quantum-coherent system — or a soap bubble, for that matter, or a lead atom, or a particular molecule. By its very design, all it's really going to do is shuffle around ones and zeros in what is, at most, a tier-two fashion — but typically constructed so that it's actually functionally tier-one.

Here's an important clarification: the universe itself may be tier three, but it may give rise to tier-one lookalikes. Even though fundamentally it's still tier three, from the point of view of whatever is being processed at the algorithmic level, it's still generally tier one — and you can't make that bridge without actually tapping into the physics that implements the system.

## The PageRank Monadology: local rules plus a holistic update

In a tier-three system, you potentially have both local rules that update the states of the nodes, and a kind of meta-rule. It says: when certain conditions are met — and in what I call the **PageRank Monadology**[^pagerank-monadology], the concept I worked on in the posts, the trigger is the detection of what we might call a **strongly connected component** — then you apply a holistic update. A strongly connected component is a property of directed graphs: a region of the graph such that from any point you can reach any other point in that region, but you can't escape to the broader graph. In a way, these are the regions where information — or the flow of being, perhaps more directly — gets trapped.

The meta-rule says: whenever you find one of these boundaries, you apply a holistic update. The straightforward example is PageRank. PageRank was used back in the day by Google to estimate: if you click randomly from one link to another across the entire internet, what's the probability you'll find yourself at a given website? Think about the holistic update similarly. You can absolutely compute this on a classical digital computer — but you'll need to serialize it and do local updates, or, in the case of some of these algorithms, do matrix multiplications until you reach convergence (there are a bunch of tricks to find it a little faster). Still, fundamentally, in how you implement it, you need to individually read all the values of all the nodes and how they're connected, then make local updates one at a time, with a memory cache keeping track of all these partial operations.

What I'm saying is that a tier-three system has this PageRank step as just one step. The moment you close the boundary, then — as a whole, almost instantly from the point of view of an external observer — the weights of the nodes have updated. And now, based on these updated nodes, you reconnect the graph and change its connectivity, its topology. So you generally have this two-step structure: local rules, then a holistic trigger gets activated, which triggers a holistic update, and that holistic update feeds back into what the local rules get applied to.

I hope this makes sense. This is really just a conceptual category — a type of system. I don't want you to focus too much on PageRank, or even too much on the fact that we're using a graph. It's really structural and conceptual. What I'm trying to point at is that there is some mechanism that enables a component of reality to behave as a unit — with a kind of **variable individuation**. What belongs inside the boundary versus outside depends on local conditions; but once the boundary is established, you get behavior that would be too fast from the point of view of individual updates, or for which there would be nowhere to put the computation from that point of view. And it may actually accomplish something interesting, useful, or recruitable from the point of view of patterns evolving in such a universe.

## Why the unity of experience implies a tier-three universe

I would argue that the mere fact that moments of experience contain many pieces of information at once — and I think this is a fact. You can ask how we know it for a fact, and question the validity of introspection; that's a very rich topic we could talk about for hours. For now I'll just say: this is a fact. It's an explanandum — something you have to explain, something you start with — and it's as empirical and as obvious as it gets. Even the etymology of "obvious" points this way: it traces back to being "in front of the road," in the path ahead of where you're going. The unity of experience is obvious in that sense.

It's true that you don't experience everything simultaneously, in quotes. But the information you do experience involves contrasts and reshiftings of attention — all of which, even a basic contrast, like the contrast between two colors on a wall, already contains multiple qualia simultaneously. Or you could say it contains a new quale: the gestalt quale of the contrast itself. That's not contained in just taking the individual pixels of experience and adding them up; there's generally some **co-witnessing** operation that closes over the question "what is the quale that is being co-witnessed?" So based on that alone, we can infer that the universe must be a tier-three system or greater — something at least that sophisticated — because moments of experience have this holistic property. For some reason, they're being recruited by natural selection for something useful, and they're fundamentally unitary — not unitary in the sense of physical synchrony, not unitary in the sense of even basic resonance, but unitary in a much deeper sense: being part of the same perspective, and that perspective having holistic effects.

Here's the thing, though: even here, you can infer from the facts of experience that the universe must be this type of thing, but that doesn't yet tell you exactly what the rules are, what the updates are, or what kind of holistic triggering mechanism is in place. You need to do a lot of work to get there. But I do think that if we were really, really smart, we would be able — at least in principle — to derive mathematical properties of the quantum mechanics of the universe based on phenomenology alone.

## Deriving physics from phenomenology

Maybe not from the normal phenomenology of everyday life, but we could have a research program that investigates the jhānas, DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, states of dissociation, and so on. Based on how attention and awareness combine to create all kinds of strange, symmetrical, high-valence configurations, I think we might be able to reverse-engineer some of the key constraints on the type of tier-three system this universe runs on. I do think this is very, very hard — but if we were a society of phenomenology geniuses with mathematical aesthetics, I think there's a chance we might have arrived at quantum mechanics from phenomenology first, and then eventually confirmed it in actual physics. Which would be so funny — the papers in that parallel reality where people say, "well, we've known for a long time that quantum mechanics is required to explain consciousness; but the idea that quantum mechanics is happening at the level of atoms, and that it has something to do with the interference patterns you see in an optical experiment in a laboratory — that's completely far-fetched, that's crackpot territory." We find ourselves in the mirror world of that timeline. But I think we can infer, for a fact, that we exist in at least a tier-three universe.

## From tier-three systems to observers: observer theory

Now, you still have to explain how this is related to an epistemic agent, or an observer. Here I'm building on the work of Sam Senchal, who I had a conversation with — you can see a write-up about that conversation on Substack[^observer-theory]. He's working on observer theory within the Wolfram ecosystem. In that framework, you define an observer in terms of properties such as its boundaries, its capacity for self-preservation, and its capacity for taking its external input into account and minimizing its free energy — essentially by predicting what's going to happen next in its environment, so it can use that to evaluate what it is and to create boundaries around its scope of action: what it can do, how it can interact with the world. So you can formally, from a computational perspective, define observers that way.

But here's the thing: with that conception of observers, you'll very likely be able to find observers if you apply that mathematical formalism to something like Conway's Game of Life. You might even say that, at a very basic level, something like the glider in Conway's Game of Life is itself a certain kind of observer, with a certain kind of boundary, persistence, and ability to predict its environment, at least implicitly. And when it comes to much more sophisticated machinery — if you implement a complex neural network sitting on top of a multi-layered Game of Life system — you'll very likely be able to say "this looks like an observer," at least statistically speaking. Especially if there's evolution, where having these boundaries, persistence, and the ability to predict the environment are all adaptive, you'll be able to find these epistemic observers there.

However, because there's still no holistic update rule — no mechanism making it act as a unit beyond the local rules, where it really still is nothing but the sum of its parts — it's not going to be a phenomenal observer. It's not going to be an experience. There's not going to be any co-witnessing relationship between the information content of a moment of experience in that world.

But here's the interesting twist. Let's define how to compute an **observer quotient**, and then apply it to systems evolving in a tier-one system, patterns evolving in tier two, and patterns evolving in tier three. My claim is that the observer quotient will spike unusually — at least under certain evolutionary conditions — in tier-two and especially tier-three systems. Precisely because this reality does have a holistic update rule, it's something epistemic observers would actually be incentivized to use as part of their information-processing pipeline, as part of the replication pipeline. Of course, these are nested processes.

It's similar to integrated information theory. IIT, in its mathematical formulation, will tell you that some Conway's-Game-of-Life-like systems have sparks of integrated information here and there. But if you apply the same math to something that has much more potential for fundamental, radical integration and holistic behavior, IIT will essentially tell you: yes, the glider has a certain integrated-information quotient, but — oh my gosh — the observers that emerge in tier-three systems have huge amounts of integrated information, huge spikes of it. So with these metrics — an observer metric or an integrated-information metric — I don't necessarily expect them to return zero in Conway's Game of Life, but I do expect them to return very large values suddenly, once you start using phenomenal binding for information processing.

I hope that makes sense. It's a tricky conceptual move — kind of putting it on its head. I'm not saying integrated information *is* what consciousness is; it's more like two sides of the same coin. Really, I'm saying integrated information is a mathematical tool that relates to coarse-graining systems, but which you'd still expect to show a spike when you have genuine ontological, monadological integration.

## Four kinds of observer system

That's the first part. The second part is: what could observers look like? Even if you exist in a tier-three universe, you might still have different instantiations of those worlds. So let me walk you through four options.

### Option one: a digital simulation

Option one is really just Conway's Game of Life again, in a sense. Say you create a simulation on a digital computer. By its very construction, you won't have the holistic behavior that matters for the computation. Maybe there's some notion of a monad in the computer — different circuits create bounded topologies here and there — but that boundedness doesn't feed back into the operations of the algorithm. The algorithm is really implemented at the level of interactions *between* monads, as opposed to anything happening *within* a monad being computationally significant. In fact, the way you build these systems is by trying to narrow down and reduce, as much as possible, the influence of the processing inside monads, restricting everything to lawful between-monad interactions.

### Option two: a grid of quantum-coherent buckets

There's another possibility. Imagine a world where you set up a gigantic grid, and within each cell you have, say, a hundred atoms or molecules that can interact and enter into a quantum-coherent state and do all kinds of holistic behavior — but the way this holistic behavior scales is limited by the fact that each bucket can only interact lawfully with its adjacent buckets. So maybe you remove the boundaries between adjacent buckets in a periodic fashion: periodically, a bucket can double in size, but then it gets halved again. Here, even though you have this monadological integration of information, you still wouldn't expect a phenomenal observer, because the observers that exist computationally would arise at the level of the interactions between these monads. At no point would you have a monad that spans many of these buckets simultaneously and has a top-down causal effect.

### Option three: the monad-observer system (the brain)

Now, you could transform that system into something else. If how many buckets you allow to be part of the same integrated, unified monad depends on the individual monad configurations, then, in a lawful way, you may trigger holistic states that influence the behavior of the system as a whole. And maybe the brain is like that. Maybe the brain has that feature: each neuron is doing its own thing — even the microtubule structure of each neuron is itself a very complex neural network of a certain sort — but then there's a mechanism such that, when a given neuron is really good at predicting, minimizing energy, or doing something metabolically efficient or useful, it briefly gets allowed to be part of the broader monad. And the broader monad incorporates the contents of many neurons simultaneously.

Conceptually, that already puts us in completely different territory — what I'd call the **monad-observer system**. That would be the brain. At the very center, arguably spanning the thalamus in its segmentation, you have an optical image that is topologically closed. And then you have a lot of these images trying to merge with the central one. There's a crazy long tail of increasingly smaller micro-experiences that contain just the solutions to various local constraint-satisfaction problems, all trying to fit together into this unified coalition of perspectives that forms the central monad.[^process-topological-monadology] And constantly, the central monad is maybe flickering on and off — a coherence moment that maybe happens hundreds of times per second. All of a sudden, we're talking about a very different type of system than either a digital computer or the grid of fixed buckets, where the maximal monad is just two adjacent squares interacting.

### Option four (ruled out): the mega-monad

Finally, the other option — which I think we can empirically rule out — is the **mega-monad**: the idea that the entire universe is just one gigantic experience. I could see arguing for that if we redefine our terms or radically shift our ontology. But if you give credence to the idea that phenomenal boundaries are doing some non-trivial information processing — that they accelerate something, or function as a kind of causal multiplier, or even as a timeline-selection mechanism that boosts probabilities for some outcomes — then without that, you're not going to have experiences as we understand them. So I don't think it makes much sense to say all of existence is one gigantic experience. Rather, you could say it's a network of experiences of variable size that has an internal and collective topological structure. Maybe you could say the universe is a gigantic mind — but it's not a gigantic experience. Although maybe it is a gigantic experience every now and then: maybe, between one second and the next, the entire universe becomes unified and then fragments again. That's another possibility. But you still have to explain the fragmentation that we do know exists and is real — this moment, this moment, this moment.

## Monads that know their own nature: self-awareness and self-knowledge

Based on all of this reasoning, you can arrive at a picture of what we are. Self-awareness is always a little bit illusory, because you never actually become aware of yourself *as* a monad. What you can become aware of is how monads interacted to give rise to your current experience. In some sense, the output of your current experience might be this self-reflective step — so it's not that you can become aware of it directly; rather, you can become aware of the echoes of the previous self-reflective step.

So to become self-aware — to understand the structure of reality, how it connects all the monads together, and so on — is something that can only happen in very specific circumstances: where you're in a tier-three universe that has gone through a very big, amazing, terrifying, incredible process of evolution, where the sizes of these monads have learned, so to speak — evolved — such that there's a central one that functions as a kind of mini world-simulation of the environment. But eventually, in order to predict itself and its own mechanics, that central monad needs to pay attention to how monads interact with each other. That itself becomes part of the error signal it's trying to minimize. So a very rarefied, advanced, enlightened state of consciousness may involve a learning process where you're not only learning to minimize prediction errors from your environment, but also learning to minimize your own prediction errors — the prediction errors that arise from the interaction between the monads. When you turn attention back onto itself, metaphorically speaking, you're training a monadological system that can predict its own dynamics.

Now, there are probably a lot of near enemies here, and false starts. There are many metaphysical views that maybe accomplish this quite well, but not entirely well. And I'm not aware of any theory of consciousness that can accurately anticipate and predict what happens on DMT — that seems to me outside of current empirical science, just in terms of the mathematical structures that emerge and the complexity and holism involved in such experiences. It's kind of like running a quantum-coherence experiment: in principle you can solve Schrödinger's equation, but it might take longer than the age of the universe for a very sophisticated system. Well, here is a very sophisticated system learning to model itself — learning to model its own boundaries.

The last insight I'll leave you with is that there are tiers of knowledge as well — tiers of self-knowledge. Because what presents itself in experience is really whatever has been optimized for the replication of the genetic vehicle that gave rise to it. By default, we come into this world as the central monad of our experience, without that much self-understanding. We don't even know, for the most part, where our boundaries end and begin — whether we're a whole body, whether you end at the point where the hairs start to grow, or whether you also include the hairs. There are a lot of models of what you are and what your boundaries are that are decent enough at predicting future experiences that, if you just run with them, you're actually fine for the most part. You only really encounter the ways in which your self-concept is totally insane when you look into the regions of the state-space of consciousness that drastically contradict your predictions. High-end meditation, DMT, and 5-MeO-DMT-type stuff really challenge all of these models — as does logical reasoning that is very grounded in first principles.

So what I'll argue is that self-awareness, in this very deep sense of a monad becoming aware of its own properties, is generally reality-transforming — because you're creating a new structure. You're creating how a quantum-coherent system reacts to itself for the first time, in a certain configuration — something the universe may not actually have encountered before, or have the means to predict or anticipate. It needs to run the experiment. But once you identify one of these beautiful attractors that really minimizes a lot of the prediction errors internally, it might be an attractor that's really solid, self-replicating, and anti-fragile. It might, however, require a very thorough search process, with a lot of near enemies.
