Hello everybody, welcome back. Today we're going to talk about a classic problem in philosophy—some people say it's an unsolved problem. It's called the problem of other minds:[^other-minds] how do you know that others are conscious, that they experience a rich inner life just as you do?

The solution I will provide is original. It is new. Well, it was posted on Qualia Computing six years ago or so,[^workable-solution] and it's been extensively discussed within the Qualia Research Institute. But it's not something you will find in academic papers, in the literature. Even if you are a PhD student in philosophy of mind, I do not expect you to have encountered this particular solution. Or if you're somebody very interested in mind uploading, AI, and consciousness, and you've read all of LessWrong and the AI papers and so on, I also don't expect you to have encountered it. And yes, I do have the confidence to say that I think this is advancing the field—actually providing a way forward.

## The qualia of the day: wire puzzles

Before I go into it, let's do the qualia of the day, which is wire puzzles. These are really cool; I really enjoy them. *(As an aside: if you want to send me a gift for whatever reason—niche perfumes, fun gadgets, fun experiences, and wire puzzles are always appreciated, especially new ones. I'm a big collector of little things like that. I have entire boxes of wire puzzles, and obviously a lot of perfumes and little experiences—fun gadgets that stimulate the mind in novel ways.)*

I think there's a very deep, rich set of metaphors and ideas that can come out of these puzzles. For one, they really teach you to distinguish between the trivial and the significant. In the first puzzle I'm holding, for example, the idea is that you have to take a piece off a wiggly wire shape. If you're familiar with wire puzzles, you will probably very quickly realize that the wiggly component is trivial—it's not really part of the puzzle; it's more of a decoration. But sometimes it isn't. Sometimes parts of a puzzle that look funny—if they're shaped like a dog or something—actually matter: the exact shape and angle of the dog's ears may matter for the solution. So a lot of things that look trivial may actually not be.

This is probably one of the most basic wire puzzles, but I really like it because it illustrates a lot of the metaphors. Very importantly, to arrive at a solution you have to realize something—and this is a very fun figure–ground inversion—which is that if you look at the pieces topologically, as opposed to as a rigid structure, the piece is already out. If there are operations that get it out without any topological changes—no breaking, no sticking, no making holes, just moving it and wiggling it around—then topologically speaking it was already out. And I find that very beautiful.

One way of thinking about it is that once you have really examined the puzzle, you can close your eyes and imagine that the solution is already there, because the piece is already out. You just have to figure out in what way it is already outside. It was free all along.

And of course there are a lot of metaphors there. There are a lot of crazy situations in life where you may feel claustrophobic because there are too many constraints: you're surrounded by impossible expectations, or very difficult people, and so on. You may feel trapped; you may think there's absolutely no way out. But you can take the attitude that you're always free—you have always been free; you've just convinced yourself, maybe using data from the outside environment, that you weren't.

Then the question is: assuming that you are free, in what way are you free? How is it the case that you're already out? In this case—well, I'll show you the solution. It's not too complicated: you have to realize that the little shape was outside all along. And there you go.

The other very beautiful thing is that solving these puzzles never requires force. If you do require force, it's a poorly crafted wire puzzle. I would say there are three things, also very beautiful, that allow you to solve these puzzles: cleverness, perseverance, and freedom of mind—not becoming claustrophobic about it, realizing you're already free.

This second puzzle I like a lot. It's a little more complicated, though still in the relatively easy category, and for illustration purposes it's quite wonderful. I'll solve it for you. There are a couple of steps, and that's usually what's involved: realizing that there are a few key operations you have to do to take the puzzle from one state to another, often through several stages. Here, for example, I've just done one operation; now I've got to move the piece all the way over and move the circle. The spoiler for this one is that the tricky part is solving two constraints at once: how to get the piece out of one circle, and how to get it out of the other. And the answer is that you do it simultaneously—you make it go through both circles at the same time. And there you go: you're outside. *(I'll just put it back in, because of OCD, I don't know. Clean up after yourself: redo the puzzle, put it back in.)*

So that's wire puzzles. And there's a beautiful metaphor here for the problem of other minds, because I would claim that the solution is, in some sense, staring us in the face. It was there from the beginning: there was no problem of other minds, if you had been able to conceptualize it properly from the very beginning. There might be a couple of somewhat non-trivial moves in the solution that I will offer, but in retrospect, hopefully it will be clear that we weren't permanently enclosed in our egocentric world simulations, unable to understand the rest of the universe. No—you're actually free. We just need the proper techniques and understanding to realize the fundamental freedom that we all have.

## The problem of other minds and solipsistic crises

So let's talk about the problem of other minds. A lot of people legitimately have solipsistic crises at some point in their life, often in their teen years—around the time when you're old enough to start having interesting ideas, but not old enough for people to have broken you into not caring about ideas very deeply. Teenagers, on average, are actually some of the people who take ideas most seriously, despite whatever "oppositional defiant disorder" behavior they may have. To some extent, that behavior is them trying to get out of the casing of mind constructs made of other people's expectations. Teenagers are trying to solve a wire puzzle, you could say—sometimes not very successfully or wisely. But there's this "think for yourself" impulse, and that's very beautiful. I think it should absolutely be cultivated in tandem with a commitment to help all sentient beings, to counterbalance it. But don't let the group mind be an oppressive force. Remember, you're always fundamentally free; it's just a matter of how you solve the puzzle to realize that freedom.

The desire to understand the experience of others is, I think, a very profound thing. If you're in a solipsistic crisis and you're skeptical of the consciousness of others, it can be extremely claustrophobic. Likewise, there's a qualia you could associate with the feeling that you will always be stuck in your particular experience and never able to experience what others experience, however much they describe it. For some people—not everybody—there's fundamentally a feeling that you're all alone in your own universe. Again, it's a particular qualia; I don't think it's an insight into the nature of reality, although it can present itself that way. It's actually a weird alloy of feelings, thought forms, and philosophical perspectives that congeals into a claustrophobic feeling. And if you have a claustrophobic feeling like that, it's a wire puzzle: you're actually fundamentally free, and you just need to figure out the proper operations to realize that freedom.

### Wanting to share experiences

It would be amazing to be able to show one's experiences to others—speaking as somebody who cares so deeply about unique, beautiful, let's say high-entropy alloys of consciousness. There are so many hyper-valuable experiences full of insight and understanding that it would be amazing if we could record them and share them with others. For example, the beautiful experience you might have had when you solved a particular math proof, if that floats your boat. Or your first kiss—what was that like? Or a meditation insight. Of course, a lot of people meditate for the purpose of liberating themselves from suffering and so on. But to some extent, I think a lot of the motivation of people who encourage others to meditate is somewhat based on wanting to share those insights and understandings with somebody else. Finding reliable ways of doing that would be awesome.

I'll read you a quote from Nick Bostrom's little essay, or story, "Letter from Utopia."[^letter-from-utopia] It's very beautiful; I highly recommend listening to it or reading it. There's a part that really struck a chord, which exemplifies for me this urge to share the most beautiful and profound experiences you have with others. It's written from the point of view of a hypothetical entity in the future that exists in utopia, and it says to you:

> You could say I am happy, that I feel good. You could say that I feel surpassing bliss. But these are words invented to describe human experience. What I feel is as far beyond human feeling as my thoughts are beyond human thought. I wish I could show you what I have in mind. If only I could share one second of my conscious life with you!

If you're on MDMA, that's a very valid and beautiful feeling to have: I wish I could show you one second of that beautiful, pure state of love, that sense of understanding and incredible self-honesty. That would be very beautiful.

### To understand all is to forgive all

Importantly, there's also a French saying: *"Tout comprendre, c'est tout pardonner"*—in English, "to understand all is to forgive all." I think its premise is a deeper understanding that there are no good or bad people, or good or bad non-human animals. We're all trapped in our individual world simulations, and the parameters of our world simulations make what we do—how we act—feel like it makes total sense, even though it may actually be batshit insane from a more objective, sober, maybe grounded point of view, if such a thing exists. (I'll talk about that in a minute.) Even in the face of atrocities and the horrible things that happen in life, if we could understand what it feels like to be in the world simulation of the people who committed those atrocities or behaved in that way, it would make sense. It would make sense that from where they stood, it felt like the appropriate, if not reasonable, course of action.

That's something we will all have to wrestle with as we understand consciousness better: that all of this energy we have put into constructing a big evil, a big demon, a big bad thing out there that we should fight—when the target isn't, for example, actual raw suffering, but an agent—is a very crazy, delusional mismatch of reference. We simply don't know what we are talking about. Of course, we need to be very open to information in order to understand other people's perspectives and where they're coming from. But even with that, it's so difficult to overcome our own feelings, the parameters of our own world simulation: the things we think we have to protect or else everything will be destroyed, and so on. All of those things are very idiosyncratic and individual. The feeling of trying to avoid a horrible situation is probably very much present even in people who are doing the worst things you could imagine to others. They're being driven by feelings very similar to the ones that drive you and me. We'll just have to come to terms with that.

That said, one interesting point: I think this perspective—that everybody is, in some fundamental sense, trying their best—becomes quite accessible on 5-MeO-DMT. Again, it's such a powerful psychedelic that I wouldn't recommend it willy-nilly. In general, actually, I would not recommend it. I would recommend it only to people who really require a therapeutic benefit, or to hardcore qualiacore psychonauts who are very rational and actually know what they're doing: starting with small doses, being very rational about it, having a sitter, not doing it while drunk, et cetera. But it does seem that for a lot of people, the very powerful, very deep non-dual qualia that emerges on 5-MeO-DMT very much gives you this perspective of "to understand all is to forgive all": even the worst cult leaders, the worst army generals, the most cruel Genghis Khans, and so on—they're all part of the universal wave function, and that's us, ultimately. The wave function is shooting itself in the foot, you could say, when it's hurting itself and falling into crazy evolutionary dynamics and the game theory that comes with them. It's a horrifying situation in a lot of cases, but it makes sense from the point of view of every point in space-time. Every point in space-time is trying to avoid its own hell—which, of course, because of egocentric bias, is perceived as avoiding hell itself—and pursuing its own heaven as well. And if they don't have an adequate, high-fidelity representation of the minds of others, then goodness knows what you're capable of doing in order to avoid your own personal hell or pursue your own personal heaven. Anyway, that is something 5-MeO-DMT is very powerful at: giving you a visceral understanding that everybody's trying their best. It's really crazy.

### The romanticism of merging minds

I would say, though, that there is quite a bit of romanticism when it comes to sharing our experiences with others. The truth is that we are encased in self-replicating machinery that has a lot of adversarial strategies, and ultimately a lot of what we do and feel actually furthers the welfare of our genes rather than that of conscious beings. Again, I would refer you to my discussion of consciousness versus replicators[^replicators] for an extensive treatment of how to get out of this mess we're in, and basically enact the preferences and the telos of consciousness itself as opposed to those of our selfish genes. For that reason, if you were actually going to connect and phenomenally bind your consciousness with those of the people and non-human animals around you, it would unfortunately be pretty horrifying in a lot of situations. People lie all the time; they hide their true feelings. Worse, something like 10% of people have high-impact chronic pain; something like 2% of people have schizophrenia, some of them extreme cases of it; there's depression, extreme anxiety, paranoia, you name it—and it's everywhere around us.

And of course there's the hippie desire: let's all take high doses of LSD, dance around a campfire, become one, and everything will be better. That actually feels amazing when it happens, because of annealing dynamics that give rise to high-harmony, high-energy, high-symmetry, high-valence states of consciousness, which imprint you with a feeling that we are all one. It's a beautiful thought, and there is something very beautiful about it. I think it can actually solve a lot of game-theoretical problems. But we should be pretty down to earth about the actual lived experiences of the organisms around us, and in a lot of circumstances they're not great.

Now, if you could do so selectively—group mind-melding on MDMA? Yes, sign me up. That would be amazing: joining a group mind where everyone is on MDMA, or 2C-B, I don't know—one of those mind spaces. Fantastic. But with typical Darwinian states of consciousness, I suspect we would be pretty horrified. Apologies; I know this is not the standard view. It clashes with the romanticism in this space.

## Epistemological optimism

Anyway, getting into the actual weeds of the problem: I, and people at QRI in general, are epistemological optimists. That means that for a lot of classic problems that many people have given up on—classic problems where people have written books and made entire careers out of trying to convince others that the problem is unsolvable—we think you just need to be more clever and patient, and persevere. In some sense, start out with the assumption that the problem is solvable and that there is actually gold in here—beautiful platinum, the gems of the state space of consciousness. They're waiting for you. You just have to put in the work, be motivated, and pursue them, and I promise there are riches upon riches to be found. I'm very serious. Don't let the PTSD of grad school or postdoc positions close your mind and make you completely cynical about our ability to understand. No, no, no, no. I think it's better to take exotic states of consciousness seriously and really study what they are capable of, and you will realize that there are many kinds of understanding that aren't part of our standard medium of thought but are part of the state space of consciousness. It's beautiful: there are gems of understanding out there to be discovered and cultivated.

Some examples: I'm extremely optimistic about the prospect of mapping the state space of consciousness in a rigorous, helpful, insightful way. Also, something that Mike Johnson and David Pearce have championed for a while is the idea that **valence**—hedonic tone, the pleasure–pain axis—is as objective a feature of an experience as, for example, the rest mass of an electron: an objective property of the universe. How good I am feeling right now, how good you're feeling right now—the spectrum of consonance and dissonance, what's actually bugging you, the imperfections, the tensions, and so on—all aggregate into a holistic sense of how good you feel, and that is as objectively so as the rest mass of the electron, which is something you can measure in a lab; two labs will agree if they have the proper instruments. In the future, I very much expect that with a new science of consciousness we will be able to point a theory at an organism and say: "Yep, this lizard is in a lot of pain; this rabbit is in ecstatic bliss. I know that with quite high confidence, because everything lines up."

Perhaps I might lose some credibility with some people, but I'm such an epistemological optimist that I think it is possible to understand why there is something rather than nothing—so much so that I have an hour-long video on that on this channel, if you're curious.[^zero-ontology] I know you will probably start out very skeptical: who is this guy who thinks he can answer the question of why there is something rather than nothing? But I think that in a lot of these cases, people just lack encouragement, lack faith that there's something to be found, and, for sure, just don't try hard enough. That's my perspective here.

## Classic solutions and why they fall short

Okay, so let's get on to the actual solution. Well, first: what are the classic solutions? How do people tend to approach this problem? One very classic "solution" is: we're all made of the same atoms. If we know that atoms can give rise to conscious experience, and you're made of atoms as well, then chances are you're also conscious. That's a kind of indirect inference. I'm not going to say there's zero value here, because there clearly is some, but it's not particularly persuasive. If you're solipsistic, you can still be a dualist, for example, or you could imagine that all of this is a simulation, so the story that everything is made of atoms may not even be something you take very seriously. This particular argument may not really do it for you, and I don't think it does it for a lot of people.

Another tentative line of argument appeals to similar evolutionary timelines, or to our very similar genetic makeup. I think I really believed this when I was 16—that was my solution: our evolutionary history is such that we're all so similar genetically that chances are our brains give rise to the same kind of phenomenon. I think this is a good probabilistic argument. But again, if you're stuck in a solipsistic hellscape, a solipsistic-asylum type of state of mind, this will not persuade you at all. You might think: no, you guys are figments of my imagination, I am God, and you don't actually exist. So let's find something better.

Other people argue from preference architecture: we all share a very similar preference architecture, and maybe that has something to do with consciousness. That's one way of going about it. Some people—Wittgenstein and others—even argue from something that I personally find completely unpersuasive, but that a lot of people for some reason find worthwhile: the notion that there's no private language,[^private-language] that language only exists in order to sync up experiences across different minds. I don't think Wittgenstein was looking particularly at qualia and valence and things like that; he was thinking more of agents, minds, people's ability to use language, and so on. Anyway, the reasoning goes that language cannot arise with only one person, only one mind, because it's about communicating internal states. Therefore, because language exists, there is a community of users of that language, all sharing the same substrate, or one similar enough for the language to evolve. Again, this is a probabilistic argument, and I personally don't buy it, because you can evolve little critters in a cellular automaton that you can show are not conscious—because they don't solve the binding problem—and they can still evolve a language, and it's not going to be a private language. Not very persuasive, I'm sorry.

Another line of argument is based on algorithms. Suppose we map out the algorithmic correlates of consciousness, as opposed to the neural correlates—which a lot of functionalists don't care very much about, because of substrate independence (again, there are videos about this I've already put out[^digital-sentience]). A functionalist might say: if it turns out that a specific type of reinforcement-learning algorithm is necessary, or necessary and sufficient, for consciousness, and you can show that another brain is implementing the same algorithm, then you can know with confidence that it is also conscious—something of that sort. This presupposes a particular place for consciousness within what's called Marr's levels of analysis:[^marr] in particular, it assumes that consciousness emerges at the algorithmic level, which at QRI we don't think is true. So those are the classic approaches. Personally, I just don't find them very persuasive. In general, behavioral or algorithmic arguments are very brittle, especially because you can definitely construct counterexamples. So I don't think they're very credible.

## Qualia formalism

Where it starts to get really interesting—similar to what I was saying about being able to tell the valence of a lizard or a bunny—is if we arrive at a very accurate formal theory of consciousness within **qualia formalism**, where for each moment of experience there is a mathematical object whose features are isomorphic to the phenomenology, and you show this for yourself. You have a measuring device that translates the state of your brain into a state of the wave function, which gets translated into a mathematical object, and it reads out: you're experiencing blue, you're experiencing a red square, you're experiencing nostalgia, ennui, postmodern angst, and so on. And it's true—you can corroborate that isomorphism for yourself, and then point the theory and the measuring device at different organisms and realize: oh my gosh, yes, it turns out my childhood friend was conscious all along. I think that's a more persuasive line of argument. But it will probably not convince a lot of people who believe in a simulationist account, or who start out solipsistic and idealistic in the sense of "everybody else is a figment of my imagination and I am God." For that type of person, a formal theory of consciousness might not be persuasive, because they might just consider it God imagining more things. It won't necessarily hit the points that really matter here.

Importantly, though, one phrase I would leave you with for this approach is the idea that *the Standard Model of physics doesn't break down in the central nervous system*. As far as we know, particle accelerators make particles collide at very, very close to the speed of light; they record what happens, and to something like 17 significant digits of precision, the same thing happens over and over again. Physics seems to be extremely well verified in a lot of circumstances, and chances are that at room temperature you don't need exotic physics: what physics is telling us is what's actually going on, and that's happening in your brain too. There isn't a fundamental discontinuity in the laws of reality just because something is happening in your brain. In that sense, a qualia formalist theory of consciousness that agrees with your own experiences in a perfect structural match is something I would personally find very persuasive. Not everybody would, though—and that's why we will go into the core solution here, the innovation.

## Mind-melding through a thalamic bridge

You actually require two elements. I used to think that only one of them was necessary, and the truth is that this one element takes you very far. It is the idea of an interthalamic bridge, following from the principle that the two hemispheres of your brain are connected through the corpus callosum and through the thalamus, and you can sort of dissociate them and make them into different subjects of experience, depending on your interpretation of the research. If you cut the corpus callosum, you get split-brain patients. So, in a sense, you can show that a mind that was unified can be segmented into two separate subjects of experience. That's very suggestive of the possibility that if we had the proper hardware—potentially even biological neurons, as a tube connecting your thalamus to my thalamus—it might just be an engineering problem to make it work: to synchronize our brain waves and solve the phenomenal binding problem so that we become one subject of experience.

This would be so strange and trippy, I'm sure. All of a sudden you would have two visual fields, and if you introspected about your past, you would probably have images coming from two incompatible pasts, and incompatible senses of self. You would also realize to what extent the sense of an avatar of who you are was just a particular flavor of qualia. As the Buddhists would say, if you identify with form, you're in for a bad time, because you're not actually any kind of form—even your innermost sense of self is really not what you are. You truly are pure consciousness embedded in a particular structure. But basically, if you do a thalamic bridge with somebody else and all of a sudden find yourself with two visual fields, two emotional fields, two body maps, and so on, I would say that's a successful mind-meld.

### The hardware-upgrade objection

I used to think that this was enough: that if you could mind-meld with others and all of a sudden had two visual fields, et cetera, that would prove beyond a shadow of a doubt that the other people were conscious to begin with. I think I used to talk about this around 2011—and even way earlier, actually—but at least in the rationalist community. And I specifically remember both Eliezer Yudkowsky and Brian Tomasik, who are, I guess, thought leaders in that space to a pretty significant extent, saying: no, that's not going to be enough.[^hardware-upgrade] Why? For a subtle reason. If you connect additional hardware to your brain and all of a sudden you have new abilities or new capacities for experience, you're not going to know for sure whether it is the interaction, the interface, or the aggregate of the new hardware and your central nervous system that allows for the new kinds of experience—while the new pieces of hardware, independently of you, might not be conscious. In other words, Brian Tomasik and Eliezer Yudkowsky were pointing out that even if you connect your brain to somebody else's via a thalamic bridge and your consciousness becomes drastically expanded, all you will have learned—rigorously speaking, at the very least—is that the pairing of your nervous system with theirs is conscious. You won't have learned that the other nervous system is conscious all on its own.

Now, there are obviously parsimony considerations here—Occam's razor considerations. If you legitimately have a much bigger experience when you connect to somebody else—literally more qualia: two visual fields, and when you count the number of specks of experience, it comes out double—there is an argument you can make: the hardware of my nervous system would cap out at a certain amount of qualia it could generate, and anything above that means I must be incorporating new hardware that, in a sense, has the capacity for consciousness on its own.

That line of reasoning also fails, as it turns out. The reason is that we already know of interventions that increase the qualia capacity of your nervous system by a very big margin. Smoking DMT, for example, or taking ayahuasca: at the peak of an intense experience, you may legitimately be experiencing several times as many qualia. If you aggregate all the specks of your experience in normal everyday life and get something like 100 micro-phenomenological specks per second, maybe on DMT you get to something like 400 or 800. And that's just with your own nervous system. Of course, some people will use this as evidence that it's not just your brain—that you're actually interacting with other entities in hyperspace, or some other mind-independent region of the wave function, or whatever it may be. And sure, that's an interesting perspective. But on parsimony grounds, I would just suggest that the brain is, as Anders and Maggie would describe it, a **qualia warp engine**: it has a tremendous capacity for creating world simulations of unfathomable depth, brilliance, and intensity. It's just that it usually runs at a very low capacity, because more isn't necessary for evolutionary reasons. So the qualia-amount line of argument may just not pan out in this case.

## Phenomenal puzzles

So what can we do? Again, other people have thought of merging with others and using that as proof that others are conscious. That is not the innovation. The innovation comes from the second ingredient, which is **phenomenal puzzles**:[^phenomenal-puzzles] puzzles that you can only solve by doing comparisons between actual raw sensate qualia varieties—they could also involve thought forms and so on, but basically the raw qualia values of experience. Comparing them is the signature that you're actually interfacing with a conscious entity. This is actually kind of tricky, but I'll give you one example of a phenomenal puzzle.

### The geometry of color

The puzzle might be: arrange all colors—all qualia values of phenomenal color—into a map, with each color assigned a point, such that the distance between any two points is proportional to the number of just-noticeable differences between them. For some qualia varieties, such as phenomenal color, there actually is a real solution to this, which is called the CIELAB color space.[^cielab] The QRI logo is pretty close to a perfect rendition of it. I think there's a slight imperfection in how it's aligned—I'm sure we will correct that in the future—but it's pretty close. Essentially, what you will see is that two axes emerge. One is the yellow–blue axis—although in the logo, the segment opposite yellow comes out purplish. That's the slight misalignment, though it's pretty subtle: the true opposite of yellow would be the bluer segment just below it, as opposed to the more purple one. Then you have the red–green axis. And then you have the black–white axis, a third dimension: how bright a color is.

It turns out that this is a Euclidean space. And this is not my opinion; this is from psychophysics. You get people to tell you when two colors are just perceptibly different, and that becomes the unit of distance—it's called a just-noticeable difference. Empirically, if you arrange the colors in a map that way, you will see that they form this Euclidean space. And that's not even a given, because mapping things out in terms of just-noticeable differences doesn't entail that you will get something that fits a Euclidean space at all. In the generic case, so-called hyperbolic embeddings are generally more accurate. I've been predicting for years that the state space of smell is a hyperbolic space, and I just saw a paper yesterday suggesting that hyperbolic embeddings are a far better fit for the map of just-noticeable differences—the metric of scent space.[^olfactory-space] The reason I suspected it was that combinations of scents can be pretty sensitive. Well, that's a rabbit hole; I'll cover that topic another time. But with color, it turns out to be a 3D Euclidean space.

### How many primary colors are there?

Importantly, once you do that work, you will realize something—and I know it sounds silly, but it really isn't—about how many primary colors there are. A lot of people will say there are three: RGB, obviously; that's just how monitors work. Other people will say red, yellow, and blue, because those are the paints you need to create all the colors. They're both wrong, actually. It's not the case that there are three primary colors; there are four. I know this sounds subjective—who are you to say this? But if you actually do a CIELAB, basically a linearized color mapping, and introspect on the qualities that you see, you will realize that there are two kinds of colors. There are primary colors, which are yellow, blue, green, and red,[^unique-hues] and then there are four derived colors: orange, bluish green, yellowish green, and purple. Seriously—isn't it the case that orange contains both red and yellow qualia? Orange is kind of a chemical compound of the qualia of red and yellow, present simultaneously. It's a composite color. And I mean this phenomenologically: I'm not talking about RGB or about combining paints or anything like that. I'm talking about qualia, about the status of colors as qualia.

![[fig-unique-and-derived-hues.svg|Four primary hues at the ends of the yellow–blue and red–green axes, four derived hues between them, and lightness as a third axis]]

Yellow, by contrast, sits geometrically between yellowish green and orange. But when you introspect on yellow, look at it very carefully, and contrast it, you will see that it is not an average of the two colors neighboring it. Not at all—it's actually pretty clear. Again, you need to pay attention to the colors to truly understand this; don't take my word for it. Look at a rainbow for a long time and take the question seriously, and you will realize that yellow is a pure color, not a mixture of orange and yellowish green, whereas yellowish green is a mixture of green and yellow. That is the true map. And this idea that yellow and blue mixed together make green—as a kid, I remember, it didn't make any sense. It's crazy. Green is a beast of its own, a completely different thing from both yellow and blue. So what's going on there? With orange, it kind of makes sense that you add yellow paint and red paint and get orange—that's a middle point. But not green. Green is its own thing. Again, it sounds silly, but it really isn't. The answer is that linear combinations of paints and so on don't give rise to the exact geometric midpoint between those colors. The mapping is more complicated: it has to do with the receptor types in our eyes and the particular way in which spectra add up or cancel out, and it's a whole mess. But underneath that mess, at the level of qualia space, there is a much simpler, perfectly geometric structure. And it is something you can arrive at independently if you do a just-noticeable-difference study and take qualia seriously.

So the puzzle of what the primary colors are is, I would say, an advanced puzzle. It's not trivial. You need some equipment, but you also need to be conscious to solve it, because it requires qualia comparisons. There's obviously a caveat about whether a p-zombie could be doing some kind of structural isomorphism instead. But I think at this point, super-strong parsimony considerations suggest that the actual comparing of qualia has computational and functional implications. This gets a little into panpsychism: the behavior of the world can be explained in terms of qualia comparisons happening all around us at a microscopic level, and physics is describing the behavior of qualia comparisons and their evolution. When you introspect, you are pointing that property of the universe—the fact that qualia comparisons can be done—at mapping out the state space.

### Why a puzzle alone is not enough

So that's a particular puzzle. But having a puzzle is not enough, because the way you communicate the puzzle is not epistemically grounded. When you say, "Hey, can you map out the state space of color and tell me what the primary colors are, and what the derived colors are?", the problem is that we cannot trust that there will be an identical mapping from sensory stimuli to qualia values. To a large extent, we know that's just not the case. Obviously, there are people who are colorblind—red–green colorblind, et cetera. There are also people who, for example, have different receptor types in the tongue, such that cilantro tastes like soap to them. My prediction is that we actually live in very different mappings: the mapping from sensory input to qualia differs from person to person, and there's a significant genetic load on it, as well as a significant environmental load and a load from training or cognitive style—what you pay attention to, which attention dynamics you follow, and so on. So the mapping differs. You can't just ask a person to solve this puzzle, because even if they show you the solution, you won't be really sure that they are conscious; the mapping is uncertain. Even if they seem wrong—if they arrive at a different set of inferences—they could still be right; it's just that their mapping is different. I hope that makes sense.

## The protocol in eight steps

That is where you also need a phenomenal connection—a reversible thalamic bridge, mind-melding. It's in the combination of these two things, phenomenal puzzles and mind-melding, that you're able to arrive at a solution to the problem of other minds. I have it in a diagram titled "Prove that your friend has a mind of its own in eight easy steps with mind-melding and phenomenal puzzles."

![[fig-mindmeld-phenomenal-puzzle-protocol.svg|The protocol in eight steps (the qualia calibration, step 1.5, is not drawn)]]
<!-- pin -->

An important thing to point out is that the solution only works in one direction: you need to do the test twice for each person to know that the other was independently conscious. The other caveat is that if the other person doesn't solve the puzzle, it doesn't mean they're not conscious; they might just not have been able to solve it. So this test can have a lot of false negatives, but it pretty much doesn't have false positives—modulo how hard it is to fake a solution to the puzzle. If the puzzle is properly calibrated and very difficult, and the other person does solve it in this setup, you would know with very high confidence that they are conscious.

The steps are as follows. You start out with A and B, and let's say you want A to know that B is conscious. What do you do?

**Step 1.** A and B arrive; neither of them has heard the qualia puzzle at all.

**Step 1.5.** *(I apologize—there's actually one step in between.)* You mind-meld and do something called a **qualia calibration**. As you're connected to the other person, all of a sudden you experience two visual fields, and in some sense you and the other person become one. *(Arguably, you were all one to begin with, due to open individualism; or each time slice is its own entity, due to empty individualism. Either way, I wouldn't find it ontologically too surprising if mixing two nervous systems together and putting them in sync allows them to sustain a unified experience.)* If that happens, there will be something kind of crazy as well: even if the two visual fields are looking at the same objects, the objects may look different in each, because the mapping may be different. Your blue might be my green. We don't know how likely that is at the moment. So first and foremost, before you do the puzzle or anything like that, you need to connect and do a qualia calibration: you look at color spaces, scent spaces, and so on from both visual fields, or both sensory fields, at once, and create a table of how the qualia values map onto each other—of how the lenses of your sensory apparatus, and the way each brain processes that sensory information, correspond.

That is the qualia calibration, the first part. Then you disconnect.

**Step 2.** You put B to sleep; B has to be temporarily unconscious here. And A learns the qualia puzzle. In the diagram I'm using a very simple qualia puzzle: of three colors—a specific green, a specific orange, and black—which one is the odd one out? The solution should be pretty obvious: the odd one out is black, because of information content. The specific green and the specific orange each have a level of qualia specificity; their Kolmogorov complexity is higher than that of black, which is a much more basic, low-information qualia. I do think you can rank qualia in terms of their intrinsic, irreducible information content, and there's probably something very deep in physics about that correspondence. But okay: the question is, which one is the odd one out?

**Step 3.** A knows the puzzle but not its solution, and B hasn't heard it yet—B is not even supposed to see or hear the puzzle.

**Step 4.** Then you mind-meld. As you become one with the other person, you "tell the puzzle to yourself" again—but now in a new mental environment that contains two visual fields, maybe two inner dialogues, and so on. You take the qualia calibration into account, so that you say: okay, I've got green, orange, and black, or whatever it is, and these are the qualia values I mean—I'm not talking about wavelengths of light or anything like that. Thanks to the qualia calibration, you can faithfully pass on the information that these are the qualia we're talking about, not the frequencies of light.

**Step 5.** This one is very important: you put A to sleep, or make A completely unconscious. Ideally you would sedate A—put A out of commission with high-dose ketamine or propofol or something like that. You want to avoid any kind of subconscious processing at all, so that A is almost frozen in time: no **qualia computing** happens in A for a little while, and B is given enough time to do all the qualia comparisons necessary to arrive at the solution. The cool thing is that phenomenal puzzles could be calibrated for specific time windows. You'd say: people with this ability, this working memory, et cetera, will generally solve this qualia puzzle within ten minutes or so—99% of people solve it within ten minutes. So you give the other person ten minutes to solve it.

**Step 6.** The other person runs out of time, or says, "Hey, I found the solution"—in this case, circling the black dot, because it's the odd one out, the one that contains the least information by a very long shot.

**Step 7.** You mind-meld again. A hasn't had any chance to think about the problem or to do the qualia comparisons that you can't get away without doing in order to solve it. *(Sorry, I bit my tongue. A sip of coffee.)*

So you mind-meld again, and again you tell the answer to yourself—to the expanded, mind-melded self.

**Step 8.** Then you disconnect. If A can provide the answer, you can infer that B did qualia comparisons to solve the problem while A was unconscious. And if you can do qualia comparisons, that means you're conscious: you're operating with qualia, doing qualia computing. You're conscious. So there you go, folks: this is solving the problem of other minds with mind-melding and phenomenal puzzles.

There are some caveats. But I do think that by this point the parsimony considerations are just so brutally strong that you should probably give in and say: yes, this is proof that others are conscious, and that I'm not in a solipsistic world or something like that. Because qualia computing happened: a non-trivial problem was solved by doing qualia comparisons outside of your own mind. Information was produced. So, obviously, some kind of computational system that uses qualia was at work outside of you—others are conscious as well. I just think it's really cool that, at least in principle, it may be possible to prove that others are conscious and escape our solipsistic ideations.

## The Wada test extension

A fascinating extension of this I owe to Quintin, who works at QRI and is doing a lot of amazing technical work in neuroimaging and testing various theories of consciousness, with amazing neuroimaging datasets, EEG of meditators, and so on. He's one of the smartest people I know, and he has engaged very deeply with a lot of these ideas. He figured out a way of extending this to another domain.[^wada-puzzles]

For this I need to introduce a third crazy idea. This one already exists: it's called the Wada test.[^wada-test] It's pretty invasive, but it's one way of telling which is your verbal hemisphere, in order to ablate the other one to prevent seizures—it's a step in the treatment of treatment-resistant epilepsy. The Wada test involves a particular artery in the neck, where the blood is already split into two channels. If you inject a drug like pentobarbital into only one of these arteries, it will reach only one hemisphere at a time. So you can put your left hemisphere to sleep for, say, half an hour, and then put your right hemisphere to sleep for maybe another half an hour. I find it mind-boggling, obviously, that you could see what it's like to be only half of you, one half at a time. Experimenting with this for the sake of consciousness research would be fascinating. I would love to try it on myself, if it's not too unhealthy—there are a few micromorts—but I think I'd be fine with trying it out. I'm just very curious what it's like to have half of your brain literally asleep, in deep, deep sleep, and the other half fully conscious.

That on its own is interesting: you may have weird memory sequences where you start out as both of your hemispheres, then you're "lefty" for a little bit, then you're "righty" for a little bit, and righty has no recollection of what happened to lefty. But when you're the whole brain again, you remember both lefty and righty: you know that lefty came first, and you also know that righty had no recollection of lefty. Obviously, this is crazy from the point of view of personal identity, and a strong argument against closed individualism, or any theory of consciousness with an epiphenomenal, enduring, metaphysical ego or anything of the sort.

But again, for epistemological reasons, some people may not be convinced that one of the hemispheres is conscious. Take somebody like Eliezer Yudkowsky, who has super-strong beliefs that what brings consciousness into existence is verbal reasoning, or the capacity to self-report attentional states, or things of that sort.[^yudkowsky-debate] I think that's completely wrong-headed and misguided. I think consciousness is an evolutionarily ancient discovery by biology; I sure suspect that in the Cambrian explosion there were already a lot of tiny world simulations with very primitive qualia. The cortex is a tiny component of what actually gives rise to consciousness. But if you believe that consciousness requires verbal reasoning or self-reporting or something of the sort, then you may be skeptical that the right hemisphere is conscious on its own. Even if you do a Wada test and have these crazy, different lefty and righty memories, somebody like Eliezer Yudkowsky might say that this is just after the fact: your whole brain is reconstructing synaptic impressions laid down in the right hemisphere and imposing the interpretation that it was conscious at the time, when it wasn't. Again, I don't think that's a very plausible argument, but to the extent that people believe it, sure, we can say we wouldn't know for certain that both hemispheres can be independently conscious.

Here is where you can bring together, in a particular sequence, the three elements we've been talking about: mind-melding—which here just means having the full brain together—phenomenal puzzles, and the Wada test. Rather than A and B being two different people, which requires futuristic mind-melding technology that we don't currently have—it's still science fiction—A and B could be your two hemispheres, lefty and righty. We could do the same procedure with well-calibrated phenomenal puzzles: one of your hemispheres solves a puzzle by doing qualia comparisons, the other hemisphere gets the answer without having to do any of the work, and in that way it can prove to itself that the other hemisphere was conscious on its own, independently of you—of you as a hemisphere. Brilliant. I didn't think of that; Quintin did. So keep it up—keep up the amazing new thoughts. It's always very encouraging to hear people push the envelope, derive further interesting combinations of ideas, or come up with completely new ones.
