## Universalism in brief

**Arnold:** The *Philogelos*,[^philogelos] an ancient Roman joke book, contains a joke that teaches us about personal identity. A barber, an academic, and a bald man were traveling together. They agreed to take turns keeping watch through the night: barber first, academic second, bald man third. During his watch, the mischievous barber shaved the sleeping academic's head. When awakened, the academic touched his bald head and said, "How stupid is this barber! He has awakened the bald man instead of me."

The academic absurdly relies on an objective description to identify himself, despite being in the midst of a first-person experience of being awakened. He has overlooked what I'll argue is the sole decider of who I am: I am whatever is having the first-person experience I'm having. The objective description is an afterthought.

Please keep this joke in mind as I start to explain my view of personal identity, which I call **universalism**. According to my view, almost all of us are mistaken about the boundaries between persons. You don't discover who you are by checking objective facts about your name or history. You know far more fundamentally that you are the one whose experience is immediate, first-person — the one that seems to be at the center. That **immediacy** is what gives you your presence in the world and your self-interest; it is what makes your pains hurt. But every conscious being has experience with that same immediate, first-person character. Nothing about it is unique to you. Being yours is universal in experience.

Since all experience has the immediacy that makes your experience yours, universalism says you are equally all conscious beings. Each feels uniquely mine only because its content is cut off from the others. But the ordinary view adds something unnecessary to this explanation of feeling cut off: it adds the unwarranted assertion that you are also only one conscious being. This ought to be sliced out by Occam's razor. Why? Because it does nothing to explain the isolated feeling, which is already predicted by the lack of integration in both views. Cutting out this useless distinction leaves us with a simpler view: universalism.

### Split brains

**Arnold:** Consider split-brain cases. When the brain hemispheres are disconnected, two independent streams of experience appear, each feeling like the only one. But the original person can't be limited to one side: the immediacy that made experience mine must be present in both. Let me make this vivid. Each side of your visual field is processed in a different hemisphere. If your hemispheres were temporarily disconnected, both sides of the field of vision would continue with the same immediacy, unaware of each other. You'd be equally present in the experience of both, and after the suspension of integration ended, you'd remember both disconnected experiences as having been yours. Just so, you are equally present in all separate, unintegrated experiences throughout reality — in the mouse scurrying across the meadow and in the eagle spotting it from above.

### The probability argument

**Arnold:** There's a decisive probability argument for universalism. If a coin lands heads a thousand times consecutively, that's decisive evidence that it's double-headed or loaded rather than fair. Now consider your existence. On the ordinary view, it's fantastically improbable. In your conception, any other of 200 million sperm reaching the egg first would have prevented you from existing. Any different sperm in any ancestor's conception would have blocked you. The odds of you emerging from just three conceptions — yours, your mother's, and your father's — are one in eight septillion. Add all your ancestors, all of them, and forget it. So much for the ordinary view. But on universalism, your existence was guaranteed as long as any conscious being existed. Which sperm hit which egg was irrelevant detail, just as a double-headed coin explains a thousand consecutive heads.

Here's another analogy. Imagine a hotel with countless rooms, each with a sleeping person. In the **easy game**, everyone awakens. In the **hard game**, only one random person awakens. If you find yourself awake, the easy game is overwhelmingly more probable. For personal identity, only universalism — the easy game — makes your existence probable.

Notice how, in both the hotel case and the personal-identity case, it is important that the inference is made by an actual participant rather than just an outside observer. There would be nothing improbable about someone or other winning in either hard game. But it would be improbable for you, a random participant, to win in either hard game. In the book,[^finding-myself] I thoroughly examine this perspectival character of probability inferences, and other aspects of probability that even experts still find puzzling, including the Sleeping Beauty problem,[^sleeping-beauty] which I originated almost 50 years ago while thinking about cases like the hotel inference and personal identity.

### Fine-tuning and the multiverse

**Arnold:** Universalism also explains why your universe has life-friendly laws. If there's a multiverse and universalism is true, you'd find yourself in any consciousness-supporting universe. On the usual view, you'd be stuck in one universe, whether life-friendly or not, because even a duplicate of you elsewhere wouldn't be you. Making your observation probable requires a multiverse, with the lubricant of universalism to put you in it.

### What follows

**Arnold:** You who felt cheated and you who felt blessed should know there is very much more for you. Always escape alive from what harms or confines you. Find endless varieties of what you love. Death is not annihilation, for you are present in all conscious beings. Your self-interest reaches equally to all, because their pains and pleasures are equally yours. You are in the oppressor and the oppressed, the perpetrator and the victim. Knowledge of this truth could soften narrow selfishness and curb retribution. You would know it's all you.

## Parfit's naturalism and the ultra-insulating view

**Andrés:** I remember a chapter in *Reasons and Persons* where he says something like this: he analogizes the standard view of personal identity — the strong sense of the continuity of an individual person — to the sun. Then he says: we have proven that the sun doesn't exist, but at least there is the moon — Relation R and psychological continuity. But the response is to say: the moon is not the sun.

**Arnold:** We don't need to settle for the moon, I think.

**Andrés:** No.

**Arnold:** Having read my book, you know there's a section in it where I deal with what Parfit says in some detail. I think he's hung up between two inconsistent things. One is dropping personal identity — thinking it's just a fiction — and simply talking about natural processes of mental development, because he centers his attention particularly on the mind. He has sacrificed a lot of what we think is essential to personal identity. In fact, he leaves identity behind as being important at all, and turns it into something called survival. He just makes it into natural processes that he admits are not persons. So what have you got? He's hung up between two things, because even the persons somehow aren't these processes. I call his view **naturalism**, because he's not going to put up with any of the supernatural-looking things involved in the ordinary view of personal identity. So either there are no persons, just these processes; or — another way of looking at it, perhaps — there are, of course, momentary persons. Pains are experienced by something in the moment that's like a person in the moment; the problem, for him, is extending it beyond that. So it becomes a kind of Buddhism: you've got all these momentary subjects, but there isn't anything threading through all their experience of the sort that personal identity would require. Personal identity is not in tune with what's actually there.

**Andrés:** Yeah — what you call the **ultra-insulating view**, I think.

**Arnold:** Yes, that's right. Buddhism, in other words: ultra-insulating, each moment walled off from all other moments of experience. But that falls to the probability argument as well as the usual, ordinary view does, because it's even harder for one of these momentary experiencers to come into existence.

### An experience being yours, not a thing being you

**Arnold:** In fact, my whole emphasis is on **an experience being yours**, not **a thing being you**. The latter is the usual view — the wrong view — that you're in the world because a certain thing that is you is in the world. Then philosophers may come along and ask: what if we gradually change that thing? Where do you draw the line between its being you and someone else? I'm not at all interested in that. I'm interested in the fact that the only thing that makes a thing you is the immediacy of its experience. With that, it's you; without that, there'd be no reason for calling it you. That's the key to this thing. But there are people, again, who put forward positions that look like universalism but haven't made that shift. In the book, I talk about the dog and the dog's tail: in the usual view, the dog — the controlling thing — is there being a thing that's you, and an experience being yours is just the tail being wagged by that.

**Arnold:** So the usual view — "wrong way around", as the illustration labels it — has the body as a thing being you, and the experience being yours as just a tail wagged by that. Whereas in fact what's happening is that the tail is wagging the dog: the body of the dog, the important thing, is the experience being yours, which is entirely due to the first-person, immediate quality in it and in all other experiences. And then the thing having it being you is the little tail being wagged by the experience being yours. Everything that's important to you as the subject of experience is in the quality of the experience. That is your presence in the world: that you're here, that it's not blank for you. And that is your self-interest: the pains that have that quality are the ones that are yours, and that quality is what makes them so terrible in a self-interested way. If you care about what, on the usual view, you think are the pains of others — you have sympathy, and so on — you don't think of them as being in your face like that. But that style of being in your face is just general in all experience, and all pains are yours in being frightful like that. So there's nothing about the thing itself that contributes to its being you, only that it has experience that's yours — and all experience is yours anyway. Nothing is really contributed by the thing it's happening in.

So if there's a view that says there's just one thing that's me, I think that's a very unfortunate way of trying to say something like what I'm saying. There are lots of distinct things in the world: all these experiencing things are distinct from each other, and unintegrated with each other as well. So it's not revealed in any one of them that all the others are also me, just because the content of the experience in each case is cut off from all the others. But that's just a problem of information. It's not a metaphysical boundary, which it seems to be.

## What made it you in the first place

**Andrés:** Another argument — more general, in that you don't need the distinction between being an object and having an experience — was your argument for why the psychological-continuity view doesn't work, or is very suspicious. I totally agree with it: if your psychological makeup — your memories and so on — is what defines you, that's especially strange, because it means you weren't you before acquiring those experiences.

**Arnold:** Yeah, they don't seem to notice that. It's a very popular position, maybe the most popular: that psychological continuity is how you stay the same person through time. In fact, I describe the classic debate in philosophy as a debate between, on one side, those who think that what's crucial to its being you through time is that a certain thing remain the same. So far, the way I've been describing it sounds like that, but there's a variation on it I'll get to in a second. They think the thing might be the physical brain, or it might be a soul — an immaterial mental substance — but as long as that thing is there, it's still you going into the future. That's what they concentrate on: what continues you into the future. They don't ask what made it you in the first place.

**Andrés:** Right.

**Arnold:** Anyway, it's the same-thingness of it that puts you into the future experiences and makes the pains yours later on, so that you think of them in a self-interested way. And that means you could have amnesia, a total wipe of your memories, and it could still be you, because it's the same thing. That's their strong point: they can make the psychological continuity as loose as they like, and it's still you. And it does indeed seem that you would survive such things.

Their opponents are the ones who believe that psychological continuity spells out what you are, and there are some impressive reasons for that. They consider what are often called puzzle cases, going back to Locke: if the process of memories and intentions is somehow continued in another thing — you could do a science-fiction case of putting memories from one brain into another, or God could move the mental process into another immaterial substance, if that's what you think a mind is — then the thing would be different, but it would think it was you. When it started talking, it would swear it's got to be the person who was in the other body originally: "I'm over here now. Look, I've changed bodies." And it's very convincing that you would be there. Notice, then, that they're saying you can change the thing, and it would still be you as long as the psychological continuity was in the new thing. Put the two positions together, and it would still be you with a changed psychological process *and* a changed thing — and you've got universalism. Each side has going for it that it does look as though you could still exist without what the other side said was necessary. And of course, if you extend the generosity in both directions, what you've got is universalism.

Anyway, what you were pointing out is what I said: they do ignore the proper question, which is what made it you in the first place, before it continued on. The continuity makes it you only given that it was you to begin with. And it's especially troublesome when you point this out to the psychological-continuity people. Just think about it: it's still going to be you if all these memories and intentions move on, even into another thing, in some strange puzzle case. But there had to have been a beginning of this, when there wasn't yet any psychological continuity — and the thing was you, so that it could be continued into the future by these psychological connections. So what made it you then? Maybe your first experience was in the womb — I would think that's probably the case: some kind of experience in the womb, some sensations. If it was immediate — and how could it not be immediate, if it was an experience? — if it was immediate and first-person in character, it was you, and it didn't matter whether there was psychological continuity. The thing that made it you was not that.

And I'll say the same for the other side. They have to begin with something being you, and they haven't explained at all why one particular physical or immaterial thing was you instead of someone else. There's no way they get any handle on that, because there's nothing about these things that you're supposed to be that would make any of them you instead of someone else.

## Split brains and the irrelevance of objective simultaneity

**Andrés:** This also connects quite a bit with your discussion of — I believe it's an actual experiment — the Wada test, where you temporarily disable one hemisphere at a time. So you have a V shape of psychological continuity: for a little while you're diverged, but then, when both hemispheres are active again, you regain memories of both paths. That seems to contradict a lot of common-sense views.

**Arnold:** Shall I describe that case?

**Andrés:** Yes, please. I think it's very informative.

**Arnold:** I first introduce this — well, actually repeatedly, from somewhat different angles, all the way through the book. Discussing brain bisection begins in the very first of my two introductions. The first introduction I call "The Shorter and Easier Introduction" — that's its title — and it's sort of what I would say if I was at a party and someone asked me about this. I run through it in a certain way, and it includes talking about brain bisection. Then there's the second introduction, which I call "The Deepest Joke". It begins with the joke you've heard about the barber and so on. Then I begin emphasizing how my view is the reverse of the usual view, because it makes the experience being mine central rather than the thing being me, and I go through that for a while. Then I talk about how different from each other the things in experience that we count as ours, on the usual view, can be — how connected, how different. We still regard the experiences as mine. If you notice the sensations in your toes, and now add two and two, both experiences have the character of being mine, but they're so very different from each other. And I point out that most of the experiences of my life I can't remember now; I'm not really that connected to them. They're all quite disparate from each other. I've had wildly different periods. I can drop off — fall asleep — and have a very wild dream, and the experience is still mine. So I'm emphasizing how generous we can be about its still being my experience, with all of these.

But then I say that, nevertheless, there does seem to be one thing that anyone, with either view, would have to agree on: that I couldn't have two mutually excluding experiences at the same time. On the usual view, I could have wildly different experiences at different times, but there's this big thing about experiences at the same time: if they're different from each other, I can't have both. The next section of that second introduction is about brain bisection, where I show that this is not the case — that it's a mistake. I say at the beginning: look, there's one thing that's true. If you're thinking of two experiences that exclude each other, so that one has nothing of the other in it, I couldn't have them as one experience. You can't put them together and still have them exclude each other; it's just a contradiction. They exclude each other, they've got nothing to do with each other that way, and you just get a mess if you try to put them together. But what I then argue is that if you keep them separate, there's actually no problem with having both of those experiences at the same time. Later in the book, when I once again discuss brain bisection, I make the point that a barn can be both red and not red at the same time. If it's painted red in one part of the barn and painted some non-red color in another part, where's the problem? And of course I'm claiming that it's you in all these different nervous systems and so on. So you're much bigger than you thought you were — there's much more of you. You're like a big barn with a lot of different parts, all kinds of different colors at the same time.

Anyway, now I describe the case of brain bisection. People were operated on for epilepsy: the corpus callosum, which connects the hemispheres — and, it turns out, integrates their activities with each other — was cut in this operation, to keep the epilepsy from traveling from one hemisphere to the other. I know I read at one time or other that they thought it just kept the brain from sagging; they weren't too worried about cutting it. But then they somehow soon found out that in fact it was integrating the hemispheres together — it was almost entirely responsible for doing that. That's when they did the famous experiments in the 1960s; I started hearing about it back then. For example, let's say you've got one of these split-brain patients, as they're sometimes called, in the laboratory, with both hands under a table, where they can't see them. One object, like a brush, is put in one hand, and another object — a spoon is usually the one they talk about — is put in the other hand. I won't go into all the complications of it, but let's simply say that the hand holding the spoon would point to a spoon, or a picture of a spoon, if asked what was being held; the hand holding the brush would point to a brush; and neither hand could point to the object held by the other hand. There were so many ways they brought out that separate streams of experience were occurring at the same time in this brain, because the integration between them had been cut. Now, how do we interpret this? It is very disturbing to people, because it disturbs the basis of the usual view.

**Andrés:** Yes.

**Arnold:** The usual view has us as distinct things because I could only be having this experience; I couldn't be having another one at the same time. So who's having these two experiences? When you think a little more about it, you see that you can't possibly say that the original person is now having only one of them. If there'd been a stroke instead, and only one hemisphere was functioning, then for sure that person would continue into the single-hemisphere experience — whichever of the two hemispheres was left. That would be that person's experience. How could it cease to be that person's experience just because there's something else going on on the other side of the head? Suddenly the person blinks out?

**Andrés:** Yeah.

**Arnold:** It makes no sense.

### The anesthetized corpus callosum

**Arnold:** So those are the actual cases. But in this same section of the second introduction, I introduce a thought experiment that occurs in a somewhat similar form in Parfit.[^physics-exam] It's very nice, and it works like this — let me describe it as happening to me. Suppose I had a button connected to a device that was communicating with a further device right next to my corpus callosum. If I press that button, anesthetic will be injected into my corpus callosum, and temporarily there will be none of that integration between the hemispheres. Then the anesthetic wears off, and there'll be integration again. It's a very impressive case to consider. The story I like to tell is that there are two things I want to do tonight. Quite appropriate. And I'm going to press my button before these two things start, so that one thing won't interfere with the other. The prejudice is: they're happening at the same time. That will be true in both experiences, and you can see why it's quite natural that it would happen in both. And again I make the point that it can't possibly be just one of them where the experience is mine, just because something's going on across the way.

Instead, we're anesthetizing the corpus callosum, so that each hemisphere can go into separate experiences that aren't integrated. We know that if we positioned them in time — first you did the dreary studying, then the concert — fine, I'd have both experiences. But when they're objectively occurring at the same time, suddenly I can't have both of them? It's just a crazy prejudice.

**Andrés:** Yes.

**Arnold:** So then, after the anesthetic wears off — when the experiences were had at the same time, but without the integration through the corpus callosum — the hemispheres integrate again. I remember that I did the dreary studying, and it was mine. What am I remembering about it that makes it mine? ## The anthropic principle needs universalism

**Andrés:** Thank you for elaborating on all of that. I want to switch gears a little. There was a comment you made that I thought was very interesting, and I wonder if you could elaborate on it: that the anthropic principle's case for the multiverse is incomplete without universalism. I think I agree, and I hadn't heard that perspective before.

**Arnold:** I don't know anyone else who has ever noticed it, and it's the big stumbling block of physics. This is enormous. Once people understand this, they understand why the universe has the laws it does. Without understanding it, they don't have a hope in hell of understanding why our universe is anthropic. So let me explain it. First, let's make sure everyone knows what it means to say that the universe has an anthropic — human-centered — character. I give my favorite example of this in the book. There are four forces throughout the entire universe, and a certain number of particles, and all of this is uniform throughout the universe. They're quite peculiar — only four forces — and they have very arbitrary-seeming characters when you're not making the right connection.

Let me concentrate on just one of them: the strong nuclear force. Protons repel each other. If you're going to have atoms, you need protons to stick together in the nucleus of the atom, and then you can have electrons around them. Electrons are negative in charge and protons are positive, and a nucleus with protons in it — it also has neutrons — is what makes electrons attach themselves to the atom. That whole thing makes chemistry possible. Now, here's the problem. The protons have an electromagnetic force — positive charge — so they repel each other; they don't want to get anywhere near each other. But, very strangely, there's another of the four forces that attracts them together in the nucleus, only when they get close enough. Here's how peculiar it has to be for that to be the case. The electromagnetic force between positive and negative charges falls off as the square of the distance: at twice the distance, it's four times weaker. The strong nuclear force that holds the protons together falls off as the seventh power of the distance. And that's lucky. Because if it were strong enough to overcome the repulsion of the protons due to their electromagnetic charge, and fell off only as the square of the distance, it would just pull all these protons together, and you'd never have atoms with electrons around them. So it's very important that it be stronger than the electromagnetic force within about the diameter of the nucleus, but not stronger outside that, because it weakens much more quickly than the electromagnetic force. Only with this one of the four forces fixed in that particular way do you have anything interesting going on in the universe in the way of chemistry, and finally life. And there are all kinds of particular things in chemistry that have to be true — and, thank God, are true — like the carbon atom having to be peculiar. I can't tell you how many of the universe's basic determinations have to be fixed in a certain way for life to be possible — and, of course, for consciousness to be possible. So that's what it means when you talk about the anthropic principle: we're in a universe capable of producing us.

Now, if there's only one physical world, it's an incredibly lucky coincidence that it has an anthropic character — unless, as some people try to say, there's some God or something that designed it to be like that. Put that aside for now, because in science we don't like to appeal to that unless we're absolutely forced to. So let's try to understand it in purely physical terms. There is a way, and it's something I thought about many, many years ago, when it wasn't yet popular in physics — it's very popular now. I thought: if you had loads and loads of differing physical worlds — some impersonal thing at work making them different, generating universes with different basic laws and so on — and if there were enough of them, it would no longer be improbable that one or a few of them, some very small proportion of them, could produce life. When I first thought of this, I thought: yes, and of course we'd have to be in one that does that; it couldn't be one that couldn't. So that's why the one we see is like that. That's what I thought. At the same time — this is back in the '60s — I was already thinking of personal identity as being loosened, so that may have influenced my thinking that way. Years and years later, it became popular in physics to think that the anthropic principle can be explained by a multiverse — that's when the terminology got going — and that of course we'd have to find ourselves in a universe like that, with those peculiar laws. But there's a problem with that, which is that, according to the usual view of personal identity, you're stuck in one universe, whether it's life-producing or not.

If there were something exactly like you somewhere else, it wouldn't be you; it's a mere duplicate. So it's no good having all these differing kinds of universes so that one of them is going to be like that, because you're stuck in one, whether it's like that or not. You're no better off than if there were only one physical world; there's no way it could give you any advantage in explaining why your universe is anthropic. But with universalism, it is indeed a lubricant: it allows you to be put automatically wherever consciousness is created, because you simply are all conscious things. That's why you're there, and that's why your universe has that peculiar character. You'll never be able to explain it otherwise. Physicists wanted to explain it in some purely mathematical way — a unified field theory, that kind of thing, would finally explain why you had to have a strong nuclear force that falls off as the seventh power of the distance. You'll never do that, of course not. What you will do is explain the relationships universes have to each other and all the differences they fall into, and you'll explain that your universe is anthropic by combining the multiverse with universalism. You can't do it otherwise. It's so beautiful. Sometimes people discuss this in terms of a selection effect: they say that our requirement to be in a universe with this anthropic character selects it for our observation. But I point out in the book that you can distinguish a **negative selection effect** from a **positive selection effect**. The negative effect just tells you that you couldn't see the universe unless it was anthropic, but it could never explain to you why your universe turned out to be anthropic.

**Andrés:** Yeah.

**Arnold:** The positive selection effect only comes with universalism: you will be in a universe where the laws are like that. So there's a positive selection occurring there. The negative selection is worthless.

**Andrés:** This is fascinating. I really hope this argument gets popularized, because it's very clarifying.

**Arnold:** Well, yes — we'll never understand physics without it.

**Andrés:** Yeah.

### Universalism as a stage in science

**Arnold:** After I explain this in the book, there's a section where I show how universalism is just one stage in the development of science. The biggest developments in science came where we were freeing ourselves from illusions of perspective that we have due to exactly the same thing. I haven't gone into this with you yet in our discussion, but the immediacy of experience is not only all there is to an experience being mine; it's also all there is to an experience being now, and to an experience being here. That's what was getting established most importantly in science — certainly physical science: that being here was not being at the center. It loosened that up. We saw that the feeling that we were in a special place was the predictable feeling, and that it didn't actually single out, in some objective way, a special place as the center. Then, with relativity, it turned out that a time's being now was not an objective determination. Every time is experienced as now, and the only thing that makes it now is the immediacy of the experience. The most general thing that immediacy does is make it be *this* experience. Every experience that will ever occur is experienced as this experience — at this place, at this time, happening to this organism. And we turn those into objective determinations rather than subjective ones. So being this place becomes a big deal objectively — when that's all it means. Being this time becomes a big deal objectively — and then relativity wouldn't work. And being this organism, in this universe, blocks you from seeing why the laws are the way they are in our universe. So this is the progress of science, and universalism is one more step in the same direction.

And yet the end effect would be like having picked up the brains and exchanged their places, and then I would swear that I'd gone from the left to the right. So something is screwed up about the way I'm connecting experience to the particularity of brains. And what I realized was that I had been in both at the same time. So what I had there was unification, as a result of that thought experiment. This thing really grabbed hold of me, because I put it together with the duplicates in the universe: they're not mere duplicates. Bert and I will be there, even if we disappear here. If an experience were wiped out suddenly by some unexpected disaster in one of these places, and it continued on in the other, it would be the same experience continuing on.

### Types and tokens

**Arnold:** I describe this in the book, and then, as I said, I move into this ontological analysis of what's going on, where I distinguish between a token and a type. The example I use is a novel. The novel *Moby-Dick* is a detailed type, and each copy of *Moby-Dick* is a token of that type. And the logic of tokens and types is different. If you destroy a copy, the copy is destroyed, but the novel *Moby-Dick* is not destroyed; it's there in every copy. And there are characters in the novel — the ones who are having the adventures. There's Ishmael, and he's there in every copy. What I came to realize is that the pattern that is an experience is a type and not a token. It isn't held down by the token, any more than the particular atoms in a copy of *Moby-Dick* have some kind of purchase on the novel. Now we can talk just as we did with the red object. Imagine there's a world where they only ever have one copy of any novel. It's so easy to think that all the literary quality and things like that attach to the particular copy — it's the only one of *Moby-Dick* they've ever seen. But you can do thought experiments with them and wean them out of this, just as we did with the red object. You can have two copies. I've done this in lectures ever since the '70s: I've sliced a book down the middle, through the binding — of course, this is a very naughty thing to do, but I did it — and exchanged halves between two copies of the same novel. Has the novel changed over here? No. That's what I was doing in that thought experiment when I was 15, in 1961. So this is unification.

### From unification to universalism

**Arnold:** Of course, the way I was thinking of it then, I concentrated on precise duplication, with the same precise type occurring in each of the copies. But it's the gateway to universalism. Once the token no longer matters to whether it's you, you can begin thinking about to what extent the type might matter. And there are ways of showing it can't: probability arguments. They shift a bit in character, in what they're centered on, but they have the same kind of effect. Think how improbable it would be that I exist if the token had to be a particular one, as it does in the usual view: that token has to organize itself in the right place and at the right time and all that. It's absurdly improbable that I came into existence. But loosen up the token, and you're sort of in business.

Here's the probability consideration regarding particular types. If the type defined me — if I would only be that which existed in various tokens but was of a particular, narrow type — what would be coincidental is that I'd be a natural type. If you're thinking of all the types of experiencing beings there could be, most of them are chaotic. So most identities, if that's what identities were stuck to, would be chaotic ones. But if instead I find that my experience is of a type that comes about naturally, that requires that I be the kind of being that can take its type from nature itself, from what nature forms. So I'm not restricted in my type. That's why the type has to be open as well as the token, and the two together are universalism. I didn't fully realize this until 1983, when it suddenly hit me. I was already fooling around with the probability argument from '79 or '80, but I thought that we were stuck within a certain type. In fact it became very radical: I had a Buddhistic kind of view, where you were stuck in the moment of a career of a particular organism. I call these **moment universals**.[^moment-universals] They would occur wherever they are, but they weren't threaded together by anything. So it was easy to become Buddhistic about it.

**Andrés:** Yeah — a type of Buddhism.

**Arnold:** Yeah, yeah, that's right. And if you've loosened up the token completely, that can already be applied to the anthropic principle, because you can be in a different universe as long as the type turns up. So I was thinking about how probability applied to that. But then finally, in 1983, I was forced into full-blown universalism by this consideration of the improbability of the type's being natural, and I began to understand it better and better. One perhaps lucky thing — I don't know just how influential it was — was that I had a tutorial group that wanted to do Hegel, which I don't normally do. I'd studied Hegel a couple of times, as an undergraduate and then in graduate school, so I was familiar with the *Phenomenology of Mind*. They wanted to start in that, and we started in the first section, which is called "Sense-Certainty". In it, Hegel says that *now* and *here* and *mine* are universals, not particulars. That helped me see where I needed to go to solve this problem. There were other things that worked too, but it was kind of lucky that we read that.
