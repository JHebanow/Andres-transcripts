### 1. Hyperstereoscopy

The first is what I call **hyperstereoscopy**.

It turns out that not everybody has stereoscopic vision. There is a percentage of the population that, even though they have two functioning eyes, see the world as more or less flat, phenomenologically. It's similar to looking at the world with one eye: obviously you can still infer depth information from blurriness, and if you assume that something is a cube, the angles will change as a function of how far it is from you — that's just a feature of projective geometry — so you can infer distances, and of course occlusion. But phenomenologically speaking, the world is not *rendered* with actual depth as an additional dimension. That's what's called a 2.5-dimensional space.

With both eyes, if you have stereoscopic phenomenology, there is this depth quality. And there are some fascinating subjective reports — for example, of a lady who all her life had no stereoscopy despite having two functioning eyes, and then one day, at the opera, at random, it got turned on. And she was like: oh my God, oh my God, the world has depth. It was a profound change.[^stereopsis]

The thing is, most people have stereoscopy and are used to it, so they don't realize there is something profound about it. Literally: there is *profundity* in your experience. And depth of experience, I think, is not just a metaphor. Depth of experience also gives you emotional depth, for fascinating reasons having to do with harmony and resonance. So when stereoscopy gets turned on, it's profound.

How is this happening? How does the dimensionality of your experience acquire this additional z-axis? Here is, to a first approximation, what is going on.

You have two two-dimensional fields — the data streams from each of your eyes. If you have one dot in each of those two fields, you have four degrees of freedom: you would need a four-dimensional vector to specify the x and y coordinates of the dot in the left field and the x and y coordinates of the dot in the right field. Now, if that dot is actually coming from the same shared space — a three-dimensional space — something very interesting happens: the y-axis becomes redundant. The y-coordinate in the second field gives you no additional information beyond the y-coordinate in the first. But the fact that it *is* redundant is helpful, because it lets you identify which point in one field corresponds to which point in the other.[^correspondence] That's what allows you to align them. And wherever you focus, you can align them together — depending on how much you cross your eyes, you'll experience the aligned point as further away from you or closer to you. *(Excuse me.)*

But there's something much deeper going on here than a mathematical equivalence — it isn't merely that you're sacrificing a degree of freedom in exchange for a virtual dimension. You are actually *experiencing* that depth. That's so much more than "it's mathematically equivalent to a depth dimension."

So what is going on? To really understand this, you'll have to bring to mind either the article or the video on non-linear wave computing, and the article on digital sentience at QRI.[^nlwc] Essentially: your world-simulation is being constructed out of non-linear waves bouncing off each other.

When you only have one eye, the distances between points are computed by how long it takes a wave to travel from one point to another. When you integrate the two video streams, waves will start to propagate across the z-axis as well. The relationship governing how far a wave travels along the z-axis corresponds to the relative displacement in the x-axis between the two matched points in the two fields. So depth is a function of how far apart those two points are, in x, relative to each other.

Waves then start to propagate as spheres. You have a huge collection of points that are now related by a distance function equivalent to a generalization of the Pythagorean relationship: the distance is not only the square root of x² plus y², but the square root of x² plus y² plus f(z) — f of the displacement in the z-axis. And that function is what sets the speed at which the waves propagate.

There's an interesting additional point here, which is that your world-simulation is actually not Euclidean; it's this weird projective space that looks like a diorama. I suspect that the way depth is implemented involves changes in the index of refraction — that is, changes in the speed at which these waves propagate. Things that are further away are things where the waves propagate more slowly. If you're simulating a train moving very close to you versus one moving far away, in both cases you can phenomenologically recognize that they're moving at the same speed, even though obviously they're moving at very different speeds in terms of changes in your visual field. That's because you have an implicit understanding of the equivalence of relative wave-motion speed depending on position along the z-axis.

Now, something that can happen on, let's say, DMT: you already start with this stereoscopic depth perception of your world-simulation, and then there's a tracer effect. You get a stacking of your world-simulation on top of your world-simulation of a couple of milliseconds ago, on top of the one from a few milliseconds before that, and so on. You move your hand around and you get this stacking of previous moments of experience.

Sometimes there's a phenomenon called **point-of-view fragmentation**, where the different regions of this stack — which we call the **pseudo-time arrow**, and which is already stacked in normal everyday life, except that it becomes much longer on DMT — can defragment and dissociate, in such a way that rather than being understood as different events in *time*, they get interpreted as different events in *space*. This can manifest as feeling like you're seeing parallel dimensions simultaneously, in an overlapping way.

But there is an energy function in your brain — or rather, the world-simulation works this way — in which stitching together and making consistent the various input channels feeding into your experience *minimizes* energy. So just as we stitch together the information from both eyes, likewise in that state you can stitch together the different components of your pseudo-time arrow as if they were input streams from different universes. And in some cases they can be understood as input streams from essentially the same space, but looked at from a slightly tilted additional fourth dimension. Now you have this 2.5D **world-sheet** being seen through yet another angle, one not contained within the three-dimensional space. And when you stitch that together, you get something like 3.5D — though it's just a tiny sliver, a cross-section, of a 3.5D internal representation.

That's what I would call hyperstereoscopy: one of the ways in which you can actually experience higher-dimensional spaces, where waves really are propagating as if they were in 4D. Very, very strange and fascinating. Again, my long-term legitimacy strategy: I would claim this is a feature of the universe, in the sense that you can modulate how the brain works so as to instantiate these experiences — even though they're completely unbelievable unless you've had them.

### 2. Compactified extra dimensions

There are two other ways in which higher dimensions can manifest. Another one also happens in meditation — I experienced it a good number of times during the retreat — where your whole world-simulation gets oscillated along a new dimension.

It's not really a fully expansive additional dimension, because it's a *compactified* dimension. *(Sorry, very buzzwordy.)* Like in string theory, where you can have tiny dimensions that are rolled up. It's something like this: the whole world-simulation is oscillating at a particular frequency along an additional dimension — or something that functions as another dimension. It doesn't add that much depth, but it does contain, say, five snapshots of experience in a kind of time loop along that other dimension. And it absolutely has hedonic-tone effects, which can be really wonderful. ### 3. Composing symmetrical spaces

But really, the absolutely most insane hyperdimensional spaces come when you experience not only wallpaper symmetry groups but actual **space groups** — the crystallographic tessellations of space — of different kinds simultaneously in your world-simulation, where they get composed with each other. It's a product relationship, where each symmetry within each of these space groups gets multiplied by each symmetry in the other one.

It's very strange, because these are two- and three-dimensional structures implemented with resonant waves, and they interlock in such a way that they share some of those symmetries. But the new symmetry group that emerges out of this union contains additional dimensions.

It's extremely difficult to explain, but it's something to play with. If you're a mathematician and you can recognize "okay, this is a space group" — I would say absolutely try to compose them. If you see two space groups that are different, don't just go with one. Try to hold both of them in your attention simultaneously and merge them into some kind of hyperspace that contains both. That's where you can get something as high as a seven-dimensional space. It's not going to be very large — it'll have a lot of redundancy and repetition — but in terms of how waves travel and all the degrees of freedom within that space, yes, I think it can go as high as seven dimensions.

### Ways of seeing: attention as a wave modulator

But *how* you pay attention to it matters a lot too. There are different **ways of seeing**, as he calls them. And "ways of seeing" also sounds very fuzzy.

*(And if my voice cracks a little bit — I'm still slightly discombobulated from the retreat, so I may not be as fluid as in other videos. My emotional regulation might be a little bit off too. It doesn't mean I'm feeling bad in any way; actually, I feel great. There's just so much content to digest.)*

From a technical standpoint, I think your attention has not only a *size* — which could be thought of as a bucket size — but also a *wave modulation*. The fuzzy, humanities way of talking about it is that you have a way of seeing that depends on previous experiences, conditions, expectations, motivations, desires, degree of concentration, and so on. That's all true. But from a more technical perspective, I think that whenever you pay attention to something, you modulate the way you pay attention to it with your emotional tone.

We'll come back to this, but I think there's a loop between the frontal cortex — which modulates attention buckets with different resonances — and the limbic system, which functions as a wave modulator. So if you're in a bad mood, or you approach something with anger or aversion, the way you energize that internal representation (or that modality, or that region of the spectrum of your consciousness) depends on the mood: if it's angry, it's going to be done in a chaotic, slightly erratic, dissonant way. Whereas if you approach something with complete acceptance and love, the mode of attention itself will have a very soft attack–decay–sustain–release envelope, plus it's going to be in a regular rhythmic pattern, plus what's inside the wave packet itself will be made of harmonically resonant shapes.

So loving something is, quite literally, within your world-simulation, to direct a beam of energy at it that is modulated in such a way that you interlock with it and energize its consonant, resonant modes.

This connects to the intuitive notion of karma: the way you treat your internal representations always affects you emotionally. If you treat something with hate and dislike — even though phenomenologically you think it's outside of you, of course it's within your world-simulation — you're going to be energizing its dissonant resonant modes, the matrix of resonance configurations that generates very unpleasant shear patterns and pinch points and stress points. Whereas if you approach something with love and enjoyment, you're going to be energizing those internal representations in such a way that they start to couple with each other and melt into each other, generating a common currency of resonance across your world-simulation.

This is a very important insight — in the philosophical sense, not the Buddhist sense. We've talked a lot about neural annealing in terms of the resonances of your world-simulation starting to click with each other until the whole thing is in phase and coherent. But something that hadn't really dawned on me is that there's also this very important process where attention — yes, it *is* an emergent effect of the way awareness is being concentrated in the field of awareness — but there's also this additional mechanism, which I call **the Hydra**: approximately seven buckets of attention, each with its characteristic resonance, each modulated (probably through the limbic system), each coupling with different elements of the world-simulation. This is not obvious, because it isn't explicitly represented in the conceptual scheme. You can infer that this is what's going on, but you don't see it directly.

The way Rob Burbea was talking about ways of seeing really made me understand that yes, there is this wave modulation, and it is mood-dependent. Even stranger: I think this mood-dependence has a lot to do with your **interoceptive** information. If you're having a stomachache, in some sense that's going to affect your mood pretty directly. If you have a feeling of ill-being in your body, that's going to add dissonances to your mood — which you can ignore, or heal, or hallucinate away by superimposing a consonant resonant mode on top of it. There are all sorts of interesting tricks for bypassing unpleasant sensations in order to enter a jhāna.

The point is that we have a kind of **interoceptive resonance box** that produces the emotional, spectral component of your experience, and *that* is what modulates each of the waves coming from the resonances of the attention heads — the Hydra of your frontal lobes.

I was not thinking about it this way before the retreat. Or rather: this whole component — the wave modulation, and the attention heads — was missing from the picture.

I shifted away from the mindset of "I want to achieve the jhānas" in that competitive sense, which was not helpful. ### Competing clusters of coherence, and global coherence

The metaphor here is a set of pendulum clocks hanging on a wall. If the wall is very small and you have only ten of them, they will naturally synchronize. There are two phase transitions as you increase the number of connections between the clocks:

1. First you get what I call **competing clusters of coherence**: within a given cluster, every clock is synchronized with the other clocks of that cluster, but not all of the clocks are synchronized together. So the clusters are always competing over the membership of who gets synchronized with whom.
2. Then, if you add even more connections, you reach another phase transition where *all* of the clocks synchronize. That's what I call **global coherence**.

## Dependent arising, the binding problem, and zero ontology

Finally, finally, finally, finally: I'll talk about **dependent arising**, which Rob Burbea explained is a very, very subtle teaching, very difficult, and one that not even most advanced meditators get.

Given his description, I thought that dependent arising is actually the other side of the coin of the binding problem, or the boundary problem.[^boundary] Indeed, very few people really get how profound the binding problem is. And it's absolutely the same language as in Buddhism: nothing is a thing in and of itself; everything only is to the extent that it is related to other things.

But once you understand the binding problem, you realize this is something that happens *within* the world-simulation. It doesn't mean that pain isn't real. It just means that pain as a stimulus — as a low-level qualia value — acquires its valence in relation to the structure of the whole world-simulation. If it's very irregular, the only way it will actually feel good is if it somehow tessellates across the entire world-simulation, or if the tension and shear that the pain is generating dissipates properly across the world-simulation.

So it's not that any low-level sensation can mean anything. Low-level sensations do constrain the state space — they *are* constraining. But there are often many more degrees of freedom, such that even a very intensely painful sensation, in the proper wrapping, can end up being felt as if it intrinsically had positive valence. In general, though, more symmetrical, consonant, harmonious low-level percepts will be compatible with a much broader range of possible experiences that end up net positive in valence.

I hope that makes some sense. It's like how a particular note doesn't mean anything in terms of harmony unless it occurs within a musical scale. The harmony of a note is codependent: it co-arises with its position within a scale. Without the scale, it doesn't mean anything — neither consonant nor dissonant. But there are pairs, or triads, of notes for which the scale has to be very specifically engineered in order for them to be consonant, whereas there are other pairs — one note and its octave, say — that will be consonant across a lot of scales.

So yes: codependent arising does mean that the way of seeing something — the way in which you're modulating the waveform of the resonant pattern directed by the attention head — will modify the valence. Absolutely. But it will still constrain the state space of possible positive-valence interpretations. Much more could be explained here.

But I will say that codependent arising is totally compatible with a **zero ontology**.[^zero-ontology] And I think the ninth jhāna, or the unfabricated state,[^ninth] is essentially the collapsing of information content, where you get zero information content — the superposition of all possibilities. Any non-zero configuration involves the breaking of symmetry. So anything that you experience always requires a background that keeps track of the symmetries that were broken in order for you to experience it. Meaning: you cannot experience the color red intrinsically without a wrapper — similar to how you cannot have an electron without a positron.

Even though there is more matter than antimatter in the universe — that's still a mystery — presumably (definitely in the quantum vacuum, but presumably in general) the total matter in the universe cancels out to be exactly zero. The same as energy, momentum, charge, and so on. There is as much negative charge as positive charge; they cancel out. And a photon you can split into a positron and an electron.[^pair-production]

I think in that sense, the unfabricated state would be something like just a photon — or not even that, just a plain field. And then a photon might be a certain kind of symmetry breaking, and further symmetry breaking gives you an electron and an anti-electron, and further symmetry breaking gives you more structure. And then the actual experience that you have is like the Rubik's cube of reality, but scrambled in a particular way. And *that* is codependent arising — which is, maybe, the other side of the coin of the binding problem.

Zero Ontology, Physical Information, and Pure Awareness"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KdDNfTREQJU), and Arthur Witherall's essay on David Pearce's zero ontology, ["The Zero Ontology"](https://www.hedweb.com/witherall/zero.htm).

[^ninth]: The "unfabricated" attainment beyond the eight jhānas — *nirodha samāpatti*, the cessation of perception and feeling — is sometimes numbered as a ninth attainment.


[^pair-production]: A single photon cannot decay into an electron–positron pair in free space, since energy and momentum cannot both be conserved. Pair production requires a nearby nucleus (or a second photon) to absorb the recoil momentum, and a photon energy of at least 1.022 MeV.
