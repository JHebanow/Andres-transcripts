## Is consciousness primary?

**Joscha:** Yes, so let's maybe start out with a very simple question. Is consciousness primary, or is it something that supervenes on physics? And if it's the latter, does it exist within the communication between neurons, or is it some quantum phenomenon? What do you think?

**Andrés:** My working assumption is that the universe is a gigantic field of consciousness, and that it behaves according to the laws of physics. In other words, physics describes the behavior of consciousness. And this field evolves in such a way that it forms **topological pockets**: distinct regions of the field that have a clear inside and an outside, because, for example, the field lines may loop around and create an objective boundary.[^topological-pockets] I think that moments of experience are these looping structures within the field. But in that sense, consciousness would be primary. It is the ground of being, as it were.

## Which universe are we pointing at?

**Joscha:** Okay, so we probably need to clarify our terms a little bit. What exactly do you mean by consciousness in this context, and what do you mean by universe? People use these words, but physicists, for instance, often refer to the substrate we are in as a cosmos. Whereas when we talk about our experiential universe — the thing we can experience as being real — that seems to be a mental content. So we have to be clear about what it is that we are pointing at. Are we pointing at something we can experience, the universe as being the whole of experience, in a way? Or do you mean by universe the thing that physicists are studying? Or do you think it's the same thing?

**Andrés:** They're very, very related. For context: when I talk about consciousness, I'm really referring to qualia and qualia aggregates. In your visual field you have a lot of visual sensations, and they seem to be put together into a field, as if there were a bunch of pixels, all of them surrounded by a boundary. Usually this has representational content — almost all instances of consciousness generated by a human mind are about something.

For a lot of people it's very difficult to hone in on the referent of qualia, because when I talk about the way experience feels, they start from the assumption that what they're experiencing is the world outside. But in the QRI world, as it were, your consciousness is these qualia bundles: a raw set of sensations arranged in such a way that they represent an external environment. That external environment is what we call the physical universe. I essentially think that what we perceive of the universe is a tiny slice of what is actually happening around us. As Don Hoffman would say, we evolved to represent content that is necessary for our survival, rather than to represent the physical states around us as comprehensively or as unbiasedly as possible.[^interface-theory]

## Dreaming, and the constraints on the dream

**Joscha:** When you dream at night, do you also represent a slice of the world around you, in a sense?

**Andrés:** Not in that sense. I think you're instantiating a world simulation that draws from previous experiences and has representational content. In the dream you may very much believe that you're representing a state outside of you, but on my working assumption it is a sophisticated hallucination.

**Joscha:** My working assumption for everyday experience is that it's exactly the same thing, only it's tuned to predict your sensory data — which means you get constraints that determine what you can dream. And the dream is the simple structure that your mind has discovered to model the structure behind the sensory patterns.

**Andrés:** I agree completely. In some sense we're all sleepwalking. Sleepwalking, but constrained by sensory input.

**Joscha:** But that would mean we don't represent a slice of the actual physical universe; we represent a model over our sensory data. And the sensory data, if we take the results of neuroscience seriously, are constrained to what our neurons can detect and hand over to each other. So they're limited by the firing frequency of neurons, by the sensitivity of our sensory nerves, by the ability of the brain to integrate information, and so on.

**Andrés:** I agree with all of that.

**Joscha:** So how does this relate to consciousness being fundamental? Because we are now talking about something that is actually message passing between neurons.

**Andrés:** Algorithmically, to an extent — and this is where looking very closely at the implementation-level detail matters a lot. One way David Pearce would describe exactly the topic of this conversation is that, in his view, consciousness discloses the intrinsic nature of the physical.[^intrinsic-nature] Yes, the content of your experience represents information about a hypothetical external world, and we're trying to predict sensory data. However, an actual moment of experience, in this view, is an actual physical configuration that has a self-reflective quality: within the topological pocket being generated in the brain there are, in some sense, reflectors that allow it to look at itself. It has a certain self-reflectivity.

And in that sense, if you awaken to the fact that this is a dream, you can study the properties of the dream and the physics of the dream, dissociating it from the external world. I would say that this actually is studying the properties of the universe — the intrinsic properties of the universe — which is to say: how is it actually configured inside your brain?

**Joscha:** I don't really know how that would work. Can you envision this in your mind's eye?

**Andrés:** Yeah.

**Joscha:** And what is the lowest level of causal structure that makes that happen? What is a photon made of? What do you think a photon is, such that it could have qualia and self-reflexivity? Does a photon have a self-model?

**Andrés:** I don't know, and I don't think a photon has a self-model.

**Joscha:** Does a photon have enough structure to form this strange representation, or loop, that allows it to experience something?

## Is color a relational property?

**Andrés:** Well, there's a lot to unpack there, of course, because a blue object, as it were—

**Joscha:** —is not a wavelength, right? You can see this when you build robots that are meant to detect stuff. For instance, we were building software at some point to run on AIBO robots that were meant to play soccer. Our first idea was that we could make sense of the playing field by saying: this range of pixel values is green, this range is orange. And then you notice — oh, the ball is deflecting light onto the ground, so some of the green under the ball is actually tinged orange, and vice versa, the ball is reflecting the green back. So you have this weird thing that the colors, when you actually look at the pixel values, are inverted at the bottom of the ball: there the field is more orange than the ball, and the ball is now green.

And yet your perception automatically corrects for this, because your perception knows this is what orange looks like if it's next to a green surface, and vice versa. We have this high-level processing that makes sense of there being an invariance of this ball and of this field. This invariance changes when the lighting changes, but there's an underlying property that your mind is somehow extracting from it.

And if you try to generalize this over all objects that are associated with green and orange and so on, you get a cluster of features that are one step removed from the percept. So when you see red, you might associate love and blood and pain and the color of the working class and whatnot. This is all one step removed, and adds to the content. There's also the association that blue objects tend to be colder, so red appears as a warmer color than green and blue. All these things go into the perception of a color for us.

That's why I also don't think that you can have the inverted spectrum, because you would have to have one where one person always gets hot blue objects and cold red objects in their experience — and then, when they talk, they have different associations. But I don't think that the color is more than this relational property between sensory features. Do you think it's more than this?

**Andrés:** I think it is more. I think it is, in a sense, a fundamental value of reality that is being recruited to then paint over and represent this internal content. I do have several arguments for that, but the main one I would put forth is that you can study the structure of qualia space irrespective of the sensory input that gives rise to it.

The classic example is people with synesthesia. Of course, in principle you could have broken eyes, as it were, such that you don't actually get color data from your eyes — maybe just brightness data — but nonetheless have sound-to-color synesthesia, such that via specific frequencies of sound you experience specific phenomenal colors.

**Joscha:** Yes. Why do you think that is?

**Andrés:** I think those brains are wired differently, in such a way that, for example, neural connections that typically would only feed into the auditory cortex maybe cross-feed into the visual cortex. And there are specific neurons in the brain that have the right kind of internal proteins and intracellular signaling to be responsible for tuning into and expressing the qualia of color. In that way, you're using visual neurons to represent auditory input, which I think is perfectly valid. There are a lot of computations you can do that way that you can't do if you're not doing it — synesthesia can be computationally quite advantageous in some contexts.

But I do think that you can study the geometry of color space irrespective of the input with which you instantiate it. The classic experiments in psychophysics for studying color qualia space use the concept of **just noticeable differences**: they show you two colors and ask you whether they are the same.

**Joscha:** Two color chips — and then you can get people to arrange all the color chips by similarity, and make a map of their color space.

**Andrés:** Yeah.

**Joscha:** Of course, limited by the dimensions that you're putting your chips on.

**Andrés:** One overall result of the psychophysics of color is that the state space of color is three-dimensional and Euclidean.[^color-geometry] Essentially, there is a black-and-white axis, there is a red-and-green axis, and there is a yellow-and-blue axis.

## Tetrachromats: three dimensions or four?

**Joscha:** So what happens to tetrachromats?

**Andrés:** I don't know, actually. There are two options, and they're quite different. In one option, what they really have is a higher-resolution mapping, where they can tell more spectral power distributions of light apart, but they're still using the same qualia space to represent it. That is actually what I suspect is happening: that tetrachromats don't actually have additional color qualia, they just have a higher-resolution mapping between sensory input and the qualia space.

Likewise, if you have synesthesia and you go from sound to color qualia, you can still do psychophysics experiments where you assay a just noticeable difference in color qualia using different frequencies of sound. My strong suspicion is that if you do that experiment, you will reconstruct the same qualia space: black and white on one axis, red and green on another, yellow and blue on another, and it's going to be a 3D Euclidean space. Meaning that I think the state space of qualia and **qualia varieties** is something you can study universally — something an alien could be able to study, as long as they have a wide enough range of inputs that can trigger every point in that space.

**Joscha:** My hypothesis would be that tetrachromats actually do have an additional color dimension. But if you do a multidimensional scaling analysis of the just noticeable differences, and of the space in which those differences happen, I predict that it will probably still actually come out as a three-dimensional Euclidean space.

**Joscha:** Yes. So what you're basically talking about is a form of **Hebbian learning**, which means you construct your embedding space by looking at similarities. If things are similar to each other, you basically wire them together — you decrease the distance between them the more similar things are. When you take all the objects in a certain domain that you want to relate to each other, you measure their similarity by some kind of measure, and the more similar they are, the closer you put them together. ## Qualia varieties and the boundaries between modalities

**Andrés:** Okay, so digging a little bit deeper. In my conception of the state space of consciousness and so on, there are what we call **qualia varieties**, where color is a certain qualia variety and sound is a different qualia variety. And these are not arbitrary distinctions. I think they do reflect some deep structure about reality and what is possible.

One way in which you can show this is that if you take two values from a given qualia variety, there is a shortest path between them: there's one way of transforming one into the other in a smooth way, without jumps, or putting them into a space and then making associations, or anything like that. And my claim is that from any color to any color you can do this kind of smooth transformation, but you cannot do that from color to sound, because they are fundamentally different types of building block.

In that sense, you can study what these qualia spaces are in a rigorous way, with psychophysics, and map out these different spaces with different dimensionalities. And they will form very natural clusters: there's going to be color space, audio space, the space of scents, and so on. How do you make sense of these partitions, these different qualia varieties?

**Joscha:** There is an intrinsic difference between them, which means that each of these data formats has a different statistical structure. This is how we recognize them. So I don't think that the brain initially cares very much about which wire enters where; it is sorting the data that comes through the wires and looking at the statistical structure of these data.

And the next thing is that when you are integrating over all these data, you find out that colors and sound might correspond to a shared region in the three-dimensional space that surrounds you. And so you're going to associate them with each other in a particular way. This association is specific, and it doesn't allow you to merge them, because they're basically different types. And the type means that you can apply different functions to them when you make transformations.

## Which qualia varieties are possible?

**Andrés:** To follow up, because I'm very curious about your perspective: do you have any thoughts on what the range of qualia varieties is, if they correspond to different kinds of statistical structure? **Andrés:** The sort of ontology that comes to mind here is the state space of possible oscillations in a system of coupled oscillators. If you have a hierarchical neural network and you look at what the stable attractors are, there are:

## Divisive normalization and psychedelics

**Andrés:** One very novel, fascinating perspective in neuroscience that tries to explain psychedelic effects, and drug effects in general, is the paradigm that is called **divisive normalization** — I don't know if you have encountered it.[^divisive-normalization] They're talking about it as a principle for canonical computation, something that they think is happening essentially everywhere in the brain and the nervous system, to different extents, and that a lot of the computation that happens comes from stacking together a bunch of these canonical computations. The idea is very, very simple, actually, which is that for a population of neurons, the probability that it will fire, or have activity, is a function both of the inputs into it and of the surrounding context within the layer where it inhabits.

**Joscha:** How is that not input?

**Andrés:** It is a different kind of input, but you could imagine it as input from memory, or from the context, as opposed to input from new sensory signals.

**Joscha:** Is this distinguishable from the perspective of the individual neuron, or is there some kind of emergent distinction?

**Andrés:** I'm not sure. I don't think it's distinguishable from a given neuron — although maybe it is, from where it's getting the signal.

**Joscha:** So you would expect that the cortical layers, for instance, respond differently to stuff that is happening in the same cortical column versus far apart.

**Andrés:** And effectively, what some of the neuroscientists like Marco Aqil and people in that group are finding is that one of the things psilocybin does, for example, is that it changes the balance of how much the surrounding neurons in the same layer inhibit a particular population of neurons. Usually there's a lot of lateral inhibition. And based on neuroimaging studies on people on psilocybin, doing scans of edges throughout their visual field, they're claiming that essentially, on psilocybin, you have less of this inhibitory control from the surrounding neurons.[^psilocybin-normalization] In that sense, you get a more faithful response coming from the input, in some sense giving a little bit of validity to this idea of reducing the priors. And if you stack a lot of these layers together, and each of those layers now is taking the context into account a little bit less, the possible attractors that arise are very different. How does this sound?

## Hypothesis-based perception

**Joscha:** Plausible. We seem to have some bottom-up, top-down mechanism when we have perception. In our architecture, MicroPsi, we modeled this with a process that Dietrich Dörner called hypothesis-based perception, or **HyPercept**.[^hypercept] The idea is that your sensory input is cueing you to come up with a bunch of hypotheses of what you're looking at — this is the bottom-up mechanism — and it's a space that is simultaneously activated, so you basically have a superposition of possibilities of what you're looking at. And then you have a top-down process by which you have mutual inhibition between conflicting hypotheses. You also actively start looking for features that reinforce or contradict one of those hypotheses. So you actively start to have perception that checks whether something can pass for a particular kind of feature.

This active perception allows you to interpret features as reinforcing your object that normally would not even be salient, because you filter your perception to see if something is compatible with you looking at this feature, rather than this being a perfect match. So that's why it's also possible for you to work with occluded objects, and to recognize things in fog, and when they are not sharply rendered, and so on — because you just fill in these gaps based on these hypotheses. And this process is not one layer of hypotheses and one level of perception; it's a multi-stage thing, where you have a hierarchy of more and more concrete features towards the perception and more and more abstract features towards the classification. And by propagating activation back and forth, this thing eventually settles into a state where you are able to track your sensory data. That's a rough intuition.

And this is very compatible with what you just described. So you have a bottom-up mechanism, like in a vision model, that is looking for more and more complex features, and eventually these features correspond to objects. And you have a downward propagation where you are basically organizing the features into the shapes of the objects, and the objects are claiming the features that belong to them. And what you describe is that when you remove inhibition, then there will be a greater chance for a feature to fit into a larger variety of objects, and conflicting features may no longer inhibit each other. So the superpositions remain present and get integrated into the next-level object.

And to some degree, that's what we might have seen in the Inceptionism work that Blaise Agüera y Arcas's team did at Google at some point, where they were taking neural networks that were implementing vision models, were trained on varieties of image databases, and then formed hierarchical models of those features.[^inceptionism] And then they basically reduced the inhibition and allowed the top-down thing to activate more interpretations. And by feeding the spec to the equivalent of the sensory neurons, figuring out which of these neurons get activated and so on, they got massively overfitting things that look very much like psychedelic imagery.

**Andrés:** That makes sense. If you have the activation propagate across the hierarchy, and on each step you have less inhibition, then more features are tagging along.

## Reconstructing specific artifacts

**Joscha:** So this is not exactly proof that this is how it works, but it's a strong hint, and it gives us evidence that we are on track with this neural network to discover important mathematical features of the organization of perception in the brain. What would of course be more interesting is if we can reconstruct particular artifacts. So famously, for instance, ayahuasca creates these Shipibo patterns in many subjects — the patterns that the Shipibo are painting on their vases and weaving into their carpets, because they are familiar with this in their ayahuasca experiences.[^shipibo] And this is a very specific artifact that happens in visual perception when you add this particular kind of chemical, which means there should probably also be a way to induce the same features in a computer vision model, if the computer vision model is a good model of human perception.

**Andrés:** Yes. That's essentially one of the things we're trying to do with what we're calling the replication tool, which is a system of coupled oscillators that generates these very psychedelic-looking patterns. What we're trying to find is in what way the coupling dynamics — what we call the **coupling kernel**, which is how different oscillators want to be the same as or different from other oscillators at different distances — how the particular kernel generates visual effects that people then rank as being more LSD-like, or psilocybin-like, or 5-MeO-DMT-like, and so on.[^coupling-kernels] Would you say that if an approach like this, if a particular kernel, is reliably classified as, let's say, *oh yes, these are ayahuasca-like visions* — do you think that that would be useful or valid empirical evidence?

**Joscha:** I think it would definitely be interesting evidence that you are on the right track in modeling this type of artifact. But to go back to our initial question: is that not all evidence that your brain is some kind of computer made from neurons, and that consciousness is a pattern that is woven by these neurons, rather than the other way around?

**Andrés:** I would say it's compatible with both views. Where I would go next, essentially, is: how, concretely, do the low-level features and the high-level features actually get put together into a unified experience, or a unified representation? I gather that you think of it in terms of resonance, perhaps, across the layers.

## Soup, circuits, and waves

**Joscha:** Yes, but ultimately resonance is one perspective on what neurons are doing. You could very, very informally say that there are three paradigms in neuroscience about how brains represent.

- **Soup theory** basically says it's all chemicals: if something is missing, you need to change the configuration of the chemicals, and then you're no longer depressed and you can see better. Of course, that's very cartoonish — and that's also the point, to make this as strongly as possible — but I think that's mostly something that you find in pop science, where people say *oh, love is just chemical*.
- **Circuit theory** basically sees neurons as electrical circuits, similar to the transistors and gates that they're forming. Maybe there is some crosstalk between the wires, but ultimately it's very similar to something that happens in electrical circuits or computer chips.
- **Wave theory** basically says that all these neurons are vibing with each other, forming some kind of an ether in which they pass activation waves to each other. Each neuron is taking these activation waves, modulates them a little bit, and hands them on to the next one. And so the right paradigm is really resonance and oscillation.

And I think that, of course, all these three paradigms are part of the story. Each of them alone is insufficient to explain what's going on, and each of them is true to some degree. But the question is: how do you put them together in the right way to understand what's going on?

## Resonance and the transitivity of binding

**Andrés:** I think resonance must be part of the story, but I don't think it's enough to account for binding and the unity of experience, because it's not instantaneous and it doesn't have the crisp transitivity property that we have in our model. If you have two sensations that are part of your visual field and a third sensation that is part of the visual field: if A and B are part of the same visual field, and B and C are part of the same visual field, then A and C must also be part of the same visual field. And I'm not sure how you get that transitivity by default just with resonance, because resonance can happen across many different axes. So A and B being in resonance, and B and C being in resonance, doesn't imply that A and C are in resonance as well.

**Joscha:** So I wonder if you basically had some kind of carrier frequency, where the individual neurons decide which part of the wave they want to be part of. They just need to fire at the moment when the wave is passing through them, and it would depend on what they tune themselves to.

It's somewhat similar to the binding problem between two people who use radio to talk to each other over long distances. There is this big radio amateur community in the world, and if they want to talk to each other, they first of all need to have reception, which means there needs to be enough substrate connection between them to get a signal across. And then they need to tune into the same frequency, and also make sure that nobody else who has a stronger signal and conflicting messages is sending something else that is incompatible with what they are sending. So basically you need to agree on some kind of protocol.

In the case of radio, that's very simple and straightforward, because it's very close to the physics of the sound that you are recording, modulated onto the carrier that you use for electromagnetic signal transmission. But in other cases — say, where there's frequency coding, as in mobile phone connections — it's much more complicated, and you have an extremely complicated protocol that allows you to put much more information into the available bandwidth and share this channel. And so if you think about how neurons are doing it, we may be thinking sometimes: do they use amplitude modulation, or frequency modulation, when they talk to each other?

**Andrés:** I think that that is very promising. Something like a carrier frequency that is providing the underlying glue, and then the neurons are painting the values.

## Why is the binding problem hard?

**Joscha:** Yes, so that is one thing that I don't really understand in your thinking yet. You seem to believe that the binding problem is extremely difficult. It seems to be an extremely important problem.

**Andrés:** Yes.

**Joscha:** And I somehow miss that. I don't get it, because to me, there is a binding problem — for instance, when you are talking to a bunch of web servers to fetch a website over many, many different servers. Some of the stuff comes from Google, some of it comes from a database of fonts on the other end of the planet. It's not a problem. You basically have this binding that happens in your browser at any given time, and at some point you basically release it to connect to a different website and to a different set of servers. And the way in which this works is that you have a protocol that allows you to perform some kind of handshake. Once this handshake is established, you assume that the signals that you're getting are still yours; and when the signals are not coming with the same protocol for a while, you assume that the connection has been lost.

The simplest way would be: imagine you are sitting in a submarine, and the submarine can only receive knocking noises. You are very deep in the Mariana Trench, everything is pitch dark, and the only thing that you hear is knocking noises. And there are a bunch of other submarines around you, and you try to listen to them, and you get these knocks. And you want to know: is he talking to me or not? And so what you could be doing is that you, in regular intervals, do some knocking, and you vary it a little bit and see if the other side is following your variations, and whether you always get an answer. And when this is happening bidirectionally, you know that you are actually in some kind of communication. And so you could evolve a very simple protocol that allows you to perform these handshakes.

And it would, at scale, look like a group of cells tuning into the same frequency. And then you get a brain wave, which means such a large number of neurons is sending signals at the same time that you can catch it with an instrument as crude as an EEG sensor through your skull.

**Andrés:** I would say I am quite insistent on thinking that the binding problem is really complex. And the reason is that when I talk about the binding problem, I'm referring to the **phenomenal binding** problem, as opposed to a **functional binding** question.[^boundary-problem] Functional binding, I completely understand, is a solved problem — and in a classical neural network, I can totally agree. Yes, okay: from a functional perspective, the features are in some sense being correlated, being put together, and the answer that you get is this integration of a lot of pieces of information at once.

However, that is different from putting information together to generate a unified experience, because the unity of experience has an objective quality to it, where there's a precise amount of information that is contained within the moment of experience. And if you were to establish a communication protocol between two experiences, as it were, I think that even if you sync them up in a way that they can predict each other, that doesn't mean that they will generate a unified experience — where, let's say, you have two visual fields at once and two tactile fields at once. Somebody might say: well, that's just a matter of what the bandwidth of communication is. But I think there's something more difficult going on, which is that whatever the channel of communication that you're using, in some sense it is only sending packets, which are a tiny subset of the actual richness of your experience.

## Unity as the absence of contradiction

**Joscha:** Yes. But for instance, we have two retinas, and these two retinas are being pieced together in the visual cortex after going through the LGN in the middle. And they're pieced together based on neighborhood. So basically, you reconstruct the shape of your retina in V1. And that's because the neighborhood of the neurons is established via the statistical likelihood that they're firing at the same time. And so this is sufficient to figure out the topology of your retina.

But the topology of your retina is not the same thing as the topology of the space that you're looking at, because it is two-dimensional and the space that you're looking at is three-dimensional. And that's because the simplest mathematical model that you get for the correlations between the two retinas is that there are two surfaces that are receiving images projected from the same origin in a three-dimensional space. And this is a mathematical regularity that our brain is figuring out by doing enough statistics. And there are not many ways in which this puzzle can be solved. It's a puzzle that your brain needs to solve by putting the two retinas together in the right way, so that you recognize that you are looking at three-dimensional objects. And there are some circumstances where it doesn't work super well — for instance, when you put on glasses with different colors, it might be that you don't recognize the same objects, when the world is tricky. But by and large, there is only one solution for this puzzle, and that's why you converge.

And it seems to me that consciousness is related to establishing this coherence. And the result of making things compatible when you integrate everything is a single mathematical structure. It's one where there are no constraint violations anymore, where everything is a single object. And so the unity of experience is the result of you piecing everything together in a scene in the same game engine. And everything that doesn't fit into the scene — that you don't know how to fit in, that is not compatible, that produces contradictions — is not integrated. This is just noise and cruft that is outside of what you can make sense of.

And so my hypothesis would be that consciousness is precisely, subjectively, this bubble of now-ness where you can make everything compatible, where you can make everything unified. And unified not in the sense that it's one point, but in the sense that it's one cohesive structure that has no contradictions within itself. And you build the largest structure that you can make that has no such contradiction at any moment, and that fits into your working memory.

**Andrés:** Yeah, yeah.

**Andrés:** No worries. This is fantastic, thank you very much.

**Joscha:** I enjoyed this very much. I wish we had more time, but I think we can continue soon.
